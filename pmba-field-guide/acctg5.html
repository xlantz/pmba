Revenue Recognition & Receivables

When exactly does a company get to call money "revenue"? This unit answers that for five different situations — long-term contracts, retail and digital sales, sales tax, returns, and gift cards — using real companies (Costco, Target, Adobe) as the running examples. It then covers the other side of the coin: what happens when a customer who already generated revenue never actually pays, and how to measure whether a company is collecting fast enough.

Unit 5, topic by topic

The one rule everything else follows from

Revenue is recognized when it is earned — when a company has satisfied its obligation to the customer (delivered the goods, transferred control, or performed the service) — not simply when cash changes hands. Cash can arrive before revenue is earned (a customer pays in advance → unearned/deferred revenue, a liability), at the same time revenue is earned (a typical cash sale), or after revenue is earned (a credit sale → accounts receivable, an asset).

Every topic in this unit is really the same question asked in a different setting: at this exact moment, has the company actually earned this money yet?

Exam angleWhen a question describes cash arriving at an unusual time (in advance, all at once for a multi-year contract, split into installments), don't default to "recognize revenue when cash is received." Ask what was actually earned as of the date in question.

Why long-term contracts need their own method

A construction project spanning several years can't wait until completion to recognize any revenue — that would make a company's income statement look like nothing happened for years, then a huge spike at the end. The cost-to-cost method fixes this by recognizing revenue in proportion to the costs incurred toward the project so far, using the logic that costs incurred are a reasonable stand-in for how much progress has actually been made.

The formula

Revenue recognized this period = Total contract price × (Costs incurred to date ÷ Total estimated contract costs)

Income recognized is then simply that period's revenue minus that period's costs incurred.

Worked example

A $220 million contract has total estimated costs of $180 million. In Year 1, $36 million of costs are incurred. Percent complete = 36/180 = 20%. Revenue recognized = $220M × 20% = $44 million. Income recognized = $44M − $36M = $8 million.

Exam angleAlways compute percent-complete from costs (incurred ÷ total estimated), not from time elapsed or amounts billed/collected — those are distractor answers built into these questions specifically to catch that mistake.

When a retail or digital sale is actually earned

For an in-store retail sale, revenue is recognized when the sale occurs and control of the merchandise transfers to the customer — essentially at checkout. For a digital sale where the customer pays online in advance, revenue is not recognized when the payment is received or the order is submitted — it's recognized when the merchandise is actually delivered to (or picked up by) the customer. Until then, the cash collected sits as a liability (unearned revenue).

Sales tax: never revenue

When a retailer collects sales tax from a customer, that tax was never the retailer's money to begin with — the retailer is just a pass-through conduit collecting it on behalf of the taxing authority. It's recorded as a liability (sales tax payable), not as revenue, regardless of when it's remitted to the government.

Sales returns: recognize net, not gross

When a retailer expects some portion of current-period sales to be returned, revenue should be recognized net of estimated returns right away — not at the full gross amount with returns handled later as they occur. If $1,800 of sales are expected to see a 10% return rate, revenue should be reduced by $180 immediately, based on the estimate.

Exam angleThree traps live in this one topic: recognizing digital-sale revenue too early (at payment, not delivery), including sales tax in revenue, and reporting sales at the gross amount instead of net of estimated returns.

Gift cards: a liability at the point of sale

When a retailer sells a gift card for cash, nothing has been earned yet — the company has simply collected cash in exchange for a promise to provide merchandise later. The cash received is recorded as a liability, not revenue. Revenue is recognized only when the card is actually redeemed.

Gift card breakage

Breakage is the estimated portion of gift cards that will never be redeemed at all (lost cards, forgotten balances). Companies estimate this portion and can recognize it as revenue over time, rather than carrying it as a liability forever.

Deferred revenue as a leading indicator

A deferred revenue (unearned revenue) balance represents cash already collected that hasn't been recognized as revenue yet — meaning it's future revenue "in the pipeline." A reduction in that balance can be a warning sign: it may mean less previously collected consideration remains to be recognized in future periods, i.e., less committed future revenue.

Costco's membership model — a running example

Costco collects an annual membership fee up front but recognizes that revenue ratably over the one-year membership period (not all at once when collected) — a direct application of "deferred revenue, earned over time." Costco also carries an accrued member rewards liability: an estimate of rewards its Executive members have already earned (typically 2% back on qualified purchases) that Costco expects to pay out later. Since this is an estimated future obligation tied to sales already made, it's recorded as a reduction in current sales together with a liability, not as a future expense recognized only when paid.

Exam angleMembership-fee revenue (ratable, tied to time) and member-rewards liability (tied to qualifying sales) are two different mechanisms inside the same company's model — expect a question that requires you to identify which one a given number is describing.

Why an allowance, not a wait-and-see approach

A company that sells on credit already knows, from experience, that some percentage of its customers won't pay. Under the allowance method, a company estimates this uncollectible amount up front and records it as bad debt expense, building up a contra-asset account called the allowance for uncollectible accounts (or allowance for doubtful accounts). This reduces accounts receivable to its net realizable value — the amount the company actually expects to collect — without having to wait to find out exactly which customers default.

The mechanics

Recording bad debt expense increases the allowance (a contra-asset, so it has a credit balance) and, since it's a contra-asset, reduces net accounts receivable — without touching gross accounts receivable directly. Net accounts receivable reported on the balance sheet = Gross accounts receivable − the allowance balance.

Two ways to estimate it: income statement approach vs. balance sheet approach

There are two standard ways to estimate bad debt expense each period, and they start from opposite ends of the problem.

Income statement approach — pros and cons. Because it ties bad debt expense directly to the current period's sales, it does the best job of matching that expense to the revenue that generated it, in the very same period (a clean application of the matching principle from the income statement's side). The drawback: since it ignores whatever balance is already sitting in the allowance account, small estimation errors don't get corrected — they just accumulate period after period, so the allowance balance (and therefore net accounts receivable) can drift further and further from an accurate figure over time.

Balance sheet approach — pros and cons. Because it always solves for an accurate target ending balance (often using an aging schedule that assigns higher uncollectible percentages to older, more overdue receivables), it's self-correcting: any estimation error from a prior period gets automatically absorbed into the next period's adjustment. This makes accounts receivable's net realizable value on the balance sheet more reliably accurate. The tradeoff: because the target is based on the balance of receivables outstanding — which includes sales from more than just the current period — the resulting bad debt expense doesn't match as cleanly to this period's own sales specifically.

Which one is preferred? The balance sheet (aging) approach is generally considered the more accurate and more widely preferred method, precisely because it's self-correcting and keeps the balance sheet's net accounts receivable close to its true collectible value every single period — rather than letting estimation error compound the way it can under the income statement approach. This is also the method used throughout this unit's aging-analysis problems (Mohan Company, Collins Company): you're given a target uncollectible amount and asked to compute the adjustment needed to reach it, which is the balance sheet approach in action.

Bad debt expense = Desired ending allowance balance − Existing (pre-adjustment) allowance balance

Measuring collection speed: AR turnover and DSO

Accounts receivable turnover measures how many times, on average, a company collects its full receivables balance in a year:

AR Turnover = Net Sales ÷ Average Accounts Receivable

Days sales outstanding (DSO) converts that into an average number of days it takes to collect:

DSO = 365 ÷ AR Turnover

A higher AR turnover and a correspondingly lower DSO both indicate a company is collecting its receivables more quickly — the two measures always move in opposite directions from each other since one is derived from the other.

Exam angleThree common traps: (1) confusing the two estimation approaches — if a question gives you net credit sales and a rate, that's the income statement approach; if it gives you an aging analysis or a target ending allowance, that's the balance sheet approach; (2) forgetting that under the balance sheet approach, bad debt expense equals the change needed to reach the target allowance, not the target itself, whenever a balance already exists; (3) mixing up which direction "faster collection" points for turnover (up) vs. DSO (down).

Before the exam

Likely exam angles

  • Given a long-term contract's total price, total estimated costs, and costs incurred to date, compute both revenue and income recognized for the period using the cost-to-cost method.
  • Given a scenario where cash arrives at an unusual time relative to when goods/services are delivered, identify exactly when revenue should be recognized (retail sale, digital sale, gift card, or membership fee).
  • Given a sales amount and a sales tax rate, or an expected return rate, compute the correct liability or net revenue figure.
  • Given a company's beginning/ending deferred revenue and cash collected (or revenue recognized), solve for the missing piece of that three-way relationship.
  • Given an aging analysis and an existing allowance balance, compute bad debt expense as the adjustment needed to reach the target — not the target balance itself.
  • Given net sales and average (or beginning/ending) accounts receivable, compute AR turnover and DSO, and correctly state which direction of each indicates faster collection.
