Revenue Recognition & Receivables

When exactly does a company get to call money "revenue"? This unit builds the formal 5-step recognition model, then applies it across a dozen real situations — long-term contracts, retail and digital sales, sales tax and returns, gift cards, licenses, franchises, and foreign currency effects — using real companies (Costco, Target, Adobe) as running examples. It then covers the other side of the coin: what happens when a customer who already generated revenue never actually pays, how to write off a bad debt, and how to read a company's operating expenses and non-GAAP disclosures.

Unit 5, topic by topic

The one rule everything else follows from

Revenue is recognized when it is earned — when a company has satisfied its obligation to the customer (delivered the goods, transferred control, or performed the service) — not simply when cash changes hands. Cash can arrive before revenue is earned (a customer pays in advance → unearned/deferred revenue, a liability), at the same time revenue is earned (a typical cash sale), or after revenue is earned (a credit sale → accounts receivable, an asset).

Every topic in this unit is really the same question asked in a different setting: at this exact moment, has the company actually earned this money yet?

The formal 5-step model

Every revenue situation in this unit — no matter how complicated it looks — is really an application of the same five-step process:

  1. Identify the contract(s) with the customer. The parties involved must be identifiable, and the terms of the sale must be specified.
  2. Identify the performance obligations in the contract. A performance obligation is a contractual promise to transfer a good or service to the customer. If a contract bundles more than one good or service, the company must identify each one as a separate performance obligation.
  3. Determine the transaction price. If the price is variable, the company estimates revenue using the expected selling price.
  4. Allocate the transaction price to each performance obligation. When a contract has more than one performance obligation, the total price is allocated across them based on each one's standalone selling price (fair value). If a standalone price isn't available, the company uses a reasonable estimate.
  5. Recognize revenue as (or when) each performance obligation is satisfied — which happens when the customer obtains control of the good or service.

Steps 2 and 4 are exactly why "multiple element" bundles (below) have to be split apart and priced individually before any revenue can be recognized on either piece.

Exam angleWhen a question describes cash arriving at an unusual time (in advance, all at once for a multi-year contract, split into installments), don't default to "recognize revenue when cash is received." Run it through the 5 steps — specifically, ask what performance obligation was actually satisfied as of the date in question.

A handful of situations don't fit neatly into "sale happens, revenue recognized" and need their own rule — each one is still just an application of the 5-step model above, applied to a trickier contract.

Nonrefundable up-front fees

A fee paid up front that the customer can't get back (e.g., a one-time setup or activation fee) is not revenue the moment it's collected. It's recognized as the future goods or services it relates to are actually provided — the fee is really prepayment for something not yet delivered.

Bill-and-hold arrangements

A customer buys goods but asks the seller to hold onto them (for storage, logistics, etc.) rather than ship immediately. Revenue is still recognized when control of the goods transfers to the customer — not when the goods are physically shipped out the door.

Consignment sales

When a company sells goods on consignment (through an agent who doesn't take ownership of the inventory), and that seller is acting as an agent rather than the actual owner of the goods, the seller recognizes only its commission as revenue — not the full sale price of the goods.

Software licenses

Revenue recognition for a software license depends on what kind of license it is:

  • Right to use — recognized when the customer can first use the licensed IP (a point in time).
  • Promise of access — recognized over the period access is provided (e.g., a SaaS subscription), not all at once.

Franchises

A franchise agreement typically bundles two distinct performance obligations, each recognized on its own timeline: the goods component (equipment, initial inventory) is recognized when those goods transfer to the franchisee; the services component (use of the trade name/license) is recognized as those services are delivered, i.e., over the life of the franchise agreement.

Variable consideration

When the transaction price isn't fixed (rebates, discounts, performance bonuses, price concessions), the company recognizes the expected amount it will actually receive as goods or services are provided — the same "use your best estimate" logic from step 3 of the 5-step model.

Multiple element contracts

When a contract bundles multiple distinct products and services together (e.g., a phone plus a service contract), the company must separate the sale into its distinct goods and services, value each one on a standalone basis, and recognize revenue on each component separately, as that specific component's own performance obligation is satisfied.

Exam angleThese six situations are really just steps 2 and 4 of the 5-step model in disguise: identify what was actually promised (the performance obligation), and don't recognize revenue on a piece of a bundle until that specific piece has been delivered.

Why long-term contracts need their own method

A construction project spanning several years can't wait until completion to recognize any revenue — that would make a company's income statement look like nothing happened for years, then a huge spike at the end. The cost-to-cost method fixes this by recognizing revenue in proportion to the costs incurred toward the project so far, using the logic that costs incurred are a reasonable stand-in for how much progress has actually been made.

The formula

Cumulative revenue to date = Total contract price × (Cumulative costs incurred to date ÷ Total estimated contract costs)

In a contract's first year, cumulative revenue to date is the revenue recognized that year, since there's no prior period to subtract. From the second year on, you need one more step:

Revenue recognized this period = Cumulative revenue to date − Revenue already recognized in prior periods

Income recognized for the period is then simply that period's revenue minus that period's costs incurred.

Worked example (single period)

A $220 million contract has total estimated costs of $180 million. In Year 1, $36 million of costs are incurred. Percent complete = 36/180 = 20%. Revenue recognized = $220M × 20% = $44 million. Income recognized = $44M − $36M = $8 million.

Multi-year worked example (no change in estimate)

Here's the same logic carried across a full three-year contract. Total contract price is $500 million; total estimated costs stay fixed at $400 million for the whole project.

Walking through Year 2: cumulative costs are $100M + $180M = $280M, so cumulative percent complete = 280/400 = 70%, and cumulative revenue to date = $500M × 70% = $350M. Revenue recognized in Year 2 = $350M cumulative − $125M already recognized in Year 1 = $225M. Income = $225M − $180M (Year 2's own costs) = $45M. By the end of Year 3, total revenue recognized ($500M) exactly equals the contract price, and total income ($100M) exactly equals price minus total costs — the method always reconciles perfectly by completion, as long as the cost estimate never changes.

Notice that because the estimate never changes here, you'd get the identical Year 2 revenue by computing it directly from that year's own $180M of costs (180/400 × $500M = $225M) instead of the cumulative subtraction. Those two approaches only diverge once the estimate changes mid-contract — which is exactly the next example.

What happens when the cost estimate changes mid-contract

Long-term projects run into cost overruns. When a company revises its total estimated cost partway through a contract, it does not go back and restate prior years — it uses the cumulative catch-up method: recompute cumulative percent complete using the new total estimate and cumulative costs to date, then subtract whatever revenue was already recognized in prior periods. The entire effect of the revised estimate lands in the current period.

A $600 million contract originally estimated total costs at $450 million. Partway through Year 2, the company revises that estimate up to $500 million after identifying cost overruns.

YearCosts this yearCumulative costsTotal est. costs (as of this year)% completeCumulative revenueRevenue this yearIncome this year

Year 1 uses the original $450M estimate: 135/450 = 30% complete, revenue = $600M × 30% = $180M, income = $180M − $135M = $45M — nothing unusual yet.

Year 2 is where the estimate change hits. Cumulative costs are now $135M + $195M = $330M, but percent complete is now computed against the revised $500M estimate: 330/500 = 66%. Cumulative revenue to date = $600M × 66% = $396M. Revenue recognized in Year 2 = $396M − $180M already recognized = $216M. Income = $216M − $195M (Year 2's own costs) = only $21M — noticeably lower than either Year 1 or Year 3. That's the catch-up effect: the whole project just became less profitable overall (total expected profit fell from $150M under the original estimate to $100M under the revised one), and because Year 1 doesn't get restated, that entire $50M profit reduction gets absorbed into Year 2 alone.

Year 3 then proceeds normally against the (unchanged) $500M estimate, bringing cumulative costs to exactly $500M and cumulative revenue to exactly the full $600M contract price — the totals still reconcile perfectly at completion, same as the no-change example.

Exam angleAlways compute percent-complete from cumulative costs incurred to date divided by the most currently known total estimated cost — not from time elapsed or amounts billed/collected. When a question gives you a revised estimate partway through a contract, that revised number applies to cumulative percent-complete immediately; don't try to isolate "how much of the increase belongs to this year" some other way — the cumulative-minus-prior-recognized formula already does that correctly in one step.

When a retail or digital sale is actually earned

For an in-store retail sale, revenue is recognized when the sale occurs and control of the merchandise transfers to the customer — essentially at checkout. For a digital sale where the customer pays online in advance, revenue is not recognized when the payment is received or the order is submitted — it's recognized when the merchandise is actually delivered to (or picked up by) the customer. Until then, the cash collected sits as a liability (unearned revenue).

Sales tax: never revenue

When a retailer collects sales tax from a customer, that tax was never the retailer's money to begin with — the retailer is just a pass-through conduit collecting it on behalf of the taxing authority. It's recorded as a liability (sales tax payable), not as revenue, regardless of when it's remitted to the government.

Sales returns: recognize net, not gross

When a retailer expects some portion of current-period sales to be returned, revenue should be recognized net of estimated returns right away — not at the full gross amount with returns handled later as they occur. If $1,800 of sales are expected to see a 10% return rate, revenue should be reduced by $180 immediately, based on the estimate.

Sales discounts: the early-payment cousin of returns

A separate contra-revenue estimate applies when a company offers customers an incentive to pay their invoice early (a cash or early-payment discount). Just like with expected returns, the company doesn't wait to see who actually takes the discount — it estimates the expected uptake at the time of sale and reduces revenue immediately, recording the estimate in a contra-revenue account (Sales Discounts) paired with an offsetting account that will later net against the receivable when it's collected.

Worked example: a company sells 400 units on credit at $60/unit (a $24,000 credit sale); the inventory sold cost $40/unit ($16,000 in COGS). Based on history, the company expects 2% of the related receivables to be paid early enough to qualify for the discount.

When a customer actually pays early enough to earn the discount, the cash collected is reduced by the discount, Accounts Receivable is removed at its full gross amount, and the Allowance is drawn down; if the total discounts actually taken end up differing from the original 2% estimate, that difference flows through Sales Discounts (and therefore net income) in the period the difference becomes known — the same "true-up" logic used for estimated returns.

Exam angleSales returns and sales discounts are both contra-revenue estimates recognized at the time of sale, not when the return/discount actually happens — but don't merge them into one number. They're tracked in separate accounts because they represent different customer behaviors (sending the product back vs. paying the bill quickly), and a question may ask you to isolate one from the other.

Gift cards: a liability at the point of sale

When a retailer sells a gift card for cash, nothing has been earned yet — the company has simply collected cash in exchange for a promise to provide merchandise later. The cash received is recorded as a liability, not revenue. Revenue is recognized only when the card is actually redeemed.

Gift card breakage

Breakage is the estimated portion of gift cards that will never be redeemed at all (lost cards, forgotten balances). Companies estimate this portion and can recognize it as revenue over time, rather than carrying it as a liability forever.

Deferred revenue as a leading indicator

A deferred revenue (unearned revenue) balance represents cash already collected that hasn't been recognized as revenue yet — meaning it's future revenue "in the pipeline." A reduction in that balance can be a warning sign: it may mean less previously collected consideration remains to be recognized in future periods, i.e., less committed future revenue.

Costco's membership model — a running example

Costco collects an annual membership fee up front but recognizes that revenue ratably over the one-year membership period (not all at once when collected) — a direct application of "deferred revenue, earned over time." Costco also carries an accrued member rewards liability: an estimate of rewards its Executive members have already earned (typically 2% back on qualified purchases) that Costco expects to pay out later. Since this is an estimated future obligation tied to sales already made, it's recorded as a reduction in current sales together with a liability, not as a future expense recognized only when paid.

Exam angleMembership-fee revenue (ratable, tied to time) and member-rewards liability (tied to qualifying sales) are two different mechanisms inside the same company's model — expect a question that requires you to identify which one a given number is describing.

Why translation is necessary

A company that regularly does business abroad transacts and keeps its accounting records in the local (home) currency of that operation. Before those results can be combined with the U.S. parent company's financial statements, everything has to be translated into USD.

What a stronger dollar does to reported revenue

As the U.S. dollar strengthens, foreign currencies weaken relative to it — so when foreign-currency revenue gets translated into USD, it undergoes a shrinkage: the same amount of foreign sales converts into fewer dollars than it would have before. A weakening dollar has the opposite effect, inflating translated revenue without any real change in the underlying foreign business.

The net translation effect on equity is recorded in accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI), not run through net income.

Translation effects vs. transaction effects

Foreign exchange effects on the financial statements are mostly translation effects (restating foreign results into USD for reporting) rather than transaction effects (an actual gain or loss from a specific cross-currency transaction) — which is why operating cash flow generally isn't affected nearly as much by currency swings as income statement or balance sheet items are.

Isolating currency effects from real performance

Reported figures "as reported" implicitly include foreign currency translation effects mixed in with real operating performance. One common approach is to back out the foreign currency effect on the income statement, which better isolates operating profit without the distortion of exchange-rate movements. Companies often disclose this backed-out figure as a non-GAAP measure of "organic growth."

Exam angleIf a question describes a company's foreign revenue changing only because of exchange rates — not because of any real change in units sold or prices charged — that's a translation effect showing up in reported (as-reported) revenue, not an actual change in operating performance.

Why an allowance, not a wait-and-see approach

A company that sells on credit already knows, from experience, that some percentage of its customers won't pay. Under the allowance method, a company estimates this uncollectible amount up front and records it as bad debt expense, building up a contra-asset account called the allowance for uncollectible accounts (or allowance for doubtful accounts). This reduces accounts receivable to its net realizable value — the amount the company actually expects to collect — without having to wait to find out exactly which customers default.

The mechanics

Recording bad debt expense increases the allowance (a contra-asset, so it has a credit balance) and, since it's a contra-asset, reduces net accounts receivable — without touching gross accounts receivable directly. Net accounts receivable reported on the balance sheet = Gross accounts receivable − the allowance balance.

Worked example: the aging analysis

Companies typically use an aging analysis to build the balance sheet approach's estimate — grouping accounts receivable by how many days past due each balance is, and applying a higher estimated uncollectible percentage to older, more overdue balances:

Aging of accountsReceivable balanceEst. % uncollectibleEst. uncollectible accounts

The company estimates that $2,900 (2.9% of gross AR) is likely uncollectible. Net accounts receivable = $100,000 − $2,900 = $97,100 — the company's best estimate of what it actually expects to collect. That $2,900 becomes the target ending balance in the allowance for doubtful accounts, with bad debt expense recorded for whatever adjustment gets the account there (per the formula below).

Writing off an account: a different entry than the estimate

Once a company determines a specific receivable is actually uncollectible, it records a write-off — and this is a genuinely different journal entry than the original bad-debt estimate. A write-off debits the allowance and credits accounts receivable directly, for the specific amount:

Example: a customer who owes $500 files for bankruptcy. The company records: Debit Allowance for Uncollectible Accounts $500; Credit Accounts Receivable $500. This reduces both the allowance and gross accounts receivable by $500 — and notice that no expense account is touched at all. The expense was already recognized back when the original estimate was made; the write-off just confirms which specific customer's balance that estimate was for.

Reading the allowance rollforward in a 10-K

Companies typically disclose their full-year allowance activity as a rollforward, which ties the estimate and the write-off mechanics together in one table:

Beginning allowance balance + Bad debt expense (the "provision") − Write-offs, net of recoveries (the "deductions") = Ending allowance balance

Reading this table tells you two different things at once: the provision line is that period's bad debt expense (the estimate), while the deductions line is what actually got written off. A useful analytical ratio is bad debt expense ÷ write-offs — a ratio consistently above 1.0 means the company is estimating somewhat more uncollectible activity than it's actually confirming as uncollectible, which is normal, but a ratio that's unusually high or rising sharply can be a sign management is being overly conservative (or, less charitably, managing earnings by over- or under-provisioning in a given period).

Two ways to estimate it: income statement approach vs. balance sheet approach

There are two standard ways to estimate bad debt expense each period, and they start from opposite ends of the problem.

Income statement approachBalance sheet approach
Also calledPercentage-of-sales methodPercentage-of-receivables / aging method

FormulaBad debt expense = Net credit sales × estimated bad debt %Bad debt expense = Desired ending allowance − existing allowance balance

Income statement approach — pros and cons. Because it ties bad debt expense directly to the current period's sales, it does the best job of matching that expense to the revenue that generated it, in the very same period (a clean application of the matching principle from the income statement's side). The drawback: since it ignores whatever balance is already sitting in the allowance account, small estimation errors don't get corrected — they just accumulate period after period, so the allowance balance (and therefore net accounts receivable) can drift further and further from an accurate figure over time.

Balance sheet approach — pros and cons. Because it always solves for an accurate target ending balance (often using an aging schedule that assigns higher uncollectible percentages to older, more overdue receivables), it's self-correcting: any estimation error from a prior period gets automatically absorbed into the next period's adjustment. This makes accounts receivable's net realizable value on the balance sheet more reliably accurate. The tradeoff: because the target is based on the balance of receivables outstanding — which includes sales from more than just the current period — the resulting bad debt expense doesn't match as cleanly to this period's own sales specifically.

Which one is preferred? The balance sheet (aging) approach is generally considered the more accurate and more widely preferred method, precisely because it's self-correcting and keeps the balance sheet's net accounts receivable close to its true collectible value every single period — rather than letting estimation error compound the way it can under the income statement approach. This is also the method used throughout this unit's aging-analysis problems (Mohan Company, Collins Company): you're given a target uncollectible amount and asked to compute the adjustment needed to reach it, which is the balance sheet approach in action.

Bad debt expense = Desired ending allowance balance − Existing (pre-adjustment) allowance balance

The trap: what if the allowance account starts backwards?

Normally, the Allowance for Uncollectible Accounts sits with a small credit balance left over from last period's estimate, and the formula above just tops it up to the new target. But every so often, a company writes off more accounts during the year than it had reserved for — and the allowance account ends up with a debit balance instead (an unusual, "overdrawn" position). When that happens, simply subtracting isn't enough: the adjustment has to close the gap and still reach the full target, so it needs to cover both amounts added together.

Worked example: a company's aging analysis says the target ending allowance should be $22,500. But before the adjustment, the Allowance account already carries a $50,000 debit balance (it wrote off more bad debt earlier in the year than it had reserved for). Instead of $22,500 − $50,000 (which would wrongly suggest a negative or reversed entry), the true adjustment needed is:

Bad debt expense = Desired ending balance + Existing debit balance = $22,500 + $50,000 = $72,500

Think of it in plain terms: the account is $50,000 in the hole, and it still needs to end up $22,500 to the good — so the total swing required is the sum of the two, not the difference. The journal entry is a $72,500 debit to Bad Debt Expense and a $72,500 credit to the Allowance account, which moves the balance from a $50,000 debit all the way to a $22,500 credit.

Measuring collection speed: AR turnover and DSO

Accounts receivable turnover measures how many times, on average, a company collects its full receivables balance in a year:

AR Turnover = Net Sales ÷ Average Accounts Receivable

Days sales outstanding (DSO) converts that into an average number of days it takes to collect:

DSO = 365 ÷ AR Turnover

A higher AR turnover and a correspondingly lower DSO both indicate a company is collecting its receivables more quickly — the two measures always move in opposite directions from each other since one is derived from the other.

Putting DSO in context

DSO is most useful compared against something — on its own, a single DSO figure doesn't say much. Three standard comparisons:

  • Against the company's own established credit terms, to check whether customers are actually conforming to those terms.
  • Across several years for the same company, to spot a trend.
  • Against peer companies, to see whether collection speed is competitive.

When receivables grow faster than sales

If accounts receivable are growing more quickly than sales, three things tend to show up together: a lower AR turnover ratio, a higher accounts-receivable-to-sales percentage, and a lengthening DSO. This combination is generally not a favorable trend, and usually points to one of three underlying causes:

  • The company is becoming more lenient in granting credit to its customers.
  • Credit quality is deteriorating — existing customers are having more trouble paying.
  • The mix of products sold has shifted toward products or customer contracts with longer payment terms.
Exam angleFour common traps: (1) confusing the two estimation approaches — if a question gives you net credit sales and a rate, that's the income statement approach; if it gives you an aging analysis or a target ending allowance, that's the balance sheet approach; (2) forgetting that under the balance sheet approach, bad debt expense equals the change needed to reach the target allowance, not the target itself, whenever a balance already exists; (3) mixing up which direction "faster collection" points for turnover (up) vs. DSO (down); (4) treating a write-off (debit allowance, credit AR) as if it touches bad debt expense — it doesn't.

What gets deducted from revenue on the way to net income

Beyond cost of sales, a typical income statement works through several other deduction categories before arriving at net income: selling, general & administrative (SG&A) expenses; research & development (R&D) expense; acquired in-process R&D (IPR&D); amortization of intangible assets; restructuring charges; the provision for income taxes; discontinued operations; and income attributable to noncontrolling interest.

Analyzing R&D expense

Two common lenses for evaluating a company's R&D spending:

Common-size R&D expense = R&D expense ÷ Total revenue
R&D expense per patent = R&D expense ÷ Number of new patents awarded

A rising R&D dollar figure isn't automatically a concern (or a good sign) on its own — what matters is the trend relative to revenue. If common-size R&D expense is falling even while R&D dollars rise, the company is growing revenue faster than it's growing research spending, which usually isn't a red flag. R&D expense per patent is a rougher measure with a real limitation: a patent awarded in a given year may reflect research spending recognized in an earlier year, so a sudden jump in this ratio can simply reflect fewer patents being awarded in a given year rather than a genuine spending surge.

Interpreting pro forma and non-GAAP disclosures

Companies frequently report adjusted figures — most commonly Adjusted Diluted EPS — where management has made a number of deductions and additions to the official GAAP numbers because it believes doing so provides a better measure of ongoing financial performance. These adjusted income statements are referred to as pro forma or non-GAAP numbers. The SEC requires companies that disclose non-GAAP figures to reconcile them back to the official GAAP numbers, and pro forma statements should always be read within the context of the GAAP statements — never as a replacement for them.

A checklist for reading pro forma disclosures critically

  • Read the external audit report and note any deviations from standard boilerplate language.
  • Review the notes on accounting policies and compare them with industry peers.
  • Examine any changes in accounting policies from prior periods.
  • Compare key ratios over time for the same company.
  • Review competitors' ratios and consider how macroeconomic conditions may have shifted ratios across the board.
  • Identify nonrecurring items and separately assess their impact on company performance and position.
  • Recast financial statements as necessary to better reflect accounting policies consistent with peers, or to better reflect economically relevant numbers.
Exam angleNon-GAAP figures aren't inherently misleading, but they're also not audited to the same standard as GAAP figures — the SEC's reconciliation requirement exists specifically so you can trace an adjusted number back to what it excluded from (or added to) the real GAAP result.

Translation effects vs. transaction effects
Most FX effects on the statements are translation effects (restating results into USD), not transaction effects (an actual cross-currency gain/loss) — which is why operating cash flow is affected much less than the income statement or balance sheet.
Foreign Currency
Organic growth (non-GAAP)
A non-GAAP measure some companies disclose after backing out foreign currency translation effects, to better isolate real operating performance from exchange-rate movements.

Allowance method
Estimates uncollectible accounts up front and records the estimate as bad debt expense, rather than waiting to find out which specific customers default.
Receivables
Allowance for uncollectible accounts
A contra-asset account (credit balance) that reduces gross accounts receivable to its net realizable value.
Receivables
Aging analysis
Groups accounts receivable by days past due and applies a higher estimated uncollectible percentage to older, more overdue balances — the basis for the balance sheet approach's estimate.
Receivables
Write-off
Once a specific account is determined uncollectible, debit the allowance and credit accounts receivable for that amount — a different entry than the original estimate, and one that never touches bad debt expense.
Receivables
Bad debt expense (adjustment)
Equals the desired ending allowance balance minus the existing (pre-adjustment) allowance balance — the change needed, not the target itself.
Receivables
Net realizable value (of receivables)
Gross accounts receivable minus the allowance for uncollectible accounts — the amount a company actually expects to collect.
Receivables
Income statement approach (% of sales)
Estimates bad debt expense as net credit sales × an estimated rate, recording the full amount each period regardless of the existing allowance balance. Matches expense to the period's own sales well, but estimation errors accumulate over time.
Receivables
Balance sheet approach (aging method)
Estimates the desired ending Allowance balance (often via an aging schedule) and records only the adjustment needed to reach it. Self-correcting each period, so it's the generally preferred method for keeping receivables' net realizable value accurate.
Receivables
Allowance with a debit (overdrawn) balance
If the Allowance account starts with an unusual debit balance instead of the normal credit balance, the bad debt entry must ADD the debit amount to the target ending balance — not subtract — since the account has to close the gap and still reach the target.
Receivables
Accounts receivable turnover
Net Sales ÷ Average Accounts Receivable — how many times per year, on average, a company collects its full receivables balance.
Receivables
Days sales outstanding (DSO)
365 ÷ AR turnover — the average number of days it takes to collect a receivable.
Receivables
Pro forma / non-GAAP disclosure
An adjusted version of GAAP figures (most commonly Adjusted Diluted EPS) that management believes better reflects ongoing performance; the SEC requires reconciliation back to GAAP.

Deductions from income
Beyond cost of sales: SG&A, R&D, acquired IPR&D, amortization of intangibles, restructuring charges, income tax provision, discontinued operations, and income attributable to noncontrolling interest.
