Step 1 · Start here

One year, four reports: a photo and three views of what happened

A company records everything it does in one set of books, then reports it four ways. The balance sheet is a photo of what it owns and owes on one day; the income statement, equity statement and cash flow statement describe what happened between two photos. All four must agree.

The year-in-your-life analogy

Your net worth on January 1 is a photo. So is your net worth on December 31. Between them: a summary of what you earned and what you spent (the income statement), a note on how your own stake changed (the equity statement), and your bank statement showing where actual cash came from and went (the cash flow statement). They can honestly tell different stories. You may have earned $6,000 for work you finished, even though a client has not paid you yet, so your bank balance did not rise by $6,000. Both are true; they answer different questions.

Six words to know before anything else

Snapshot vs. period
The balance sheet is a snapshot (one date). The other three cover a period (a quarter, a year).
Accrual basis
Record revenue when it is earned and expenses when incurred, whether or not cash has moved.
Current vs. long-term
Will it turn into cash (or be paid) within 12 months? Current if yes, long-term if no.
Net income
Revenue minus expenses for the period. It ends up in retained earnings.
Retained earnings
All profit the company has kept, net of dividends paid.
Cash flow
Actual cash in and out, split into operating, investing and financing.

Tiny example: one month at a lawn-care company

The company starts the month with retained earnings of $20,000. It mows lawns worth $10,000, but customers have paid only $6,000 so far. It pays $4,000 of expenses in cash and a $1,000 dividend.

Income statement: 10,000 revenue - 4,000 expenses = 6,000 net income
Operating cash flow: 6,000 collected - 4,000 paid = 2,000
Retained earnings: 20,000 + 6,000 - 1,000 dividend = 25,000
Balance sheet shows 4,000 of receivables (earned, not yet collected)

Net income ($6,000) and operating cash ($2,000) differ by exactly the $4,000 customers still owe. Same company, same month: the income statement counts what was earned, the cash flow statement counts what was received. Section 01 below turns that into the cash-vs-accrual rule.

Big idea

Before anything can be built or classified, you need to know when a transaction gets recorded at all — and that's a genuine choice with three possible answers.

  • Cash basis: record revenue and expenses when cash actually changes hands.
  • Accrual basis: record revenue and expenses when they are earned or incurred, regardless of when cash moves — this is the basis required under GAAP.
  • Hybrid basis: blends cash and accrual concepts — also typical in a small-business setting.
Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders' Equity

Every transaction has to keep both sides of this equation equal — it's the identity the entire Balance Sheet is built on.

One month, both ways

In March a firm delivers $40,000 of services: $25,000 collected in cash and $15,000 billed on account. Employees earn $18,000 of wages, of which $12,000 is paid before month-end.

AccrualCash basis
Revenue$40,000$25,000
Wage expense$18,000$12,000
Net income$22,000$13,000
Balance sheet, March 31Accounts receivable $15,000 and wages payable $6,000 (accrual only)

When the customer later pays the $15,000, the firm records cash up and receivable down. No new revenue: it was recorded in March.

Exam angleGiven a transaction (a sale on credit, cash received in advance, an expense incurred but not yet paid), determine whether and when it would be recorded differently under cash vs. accrual accounting. If asked to name every recognized method, don't forget hybrid basis.

Big idea

The Balance Sheet is a snapshot at one point in time — unlike the Income Statement or Statement of Cash Flows, which cover a period. Everything on it gets classified by a simple 12-month test, and recorded at what the company actually paid, not what it's worth today.

Current vs. long-term (the 12-month test)

CurrentLong-term
AssetsCash, cash equivalents, short-term investments, A/R net, inventory, prepaid expensesPP&E, long-term investments, intangibles
LiabilitiesA/P, accrued liabilities, unearned revenue, short-term debt, current maturities of LT debtLong-term debt, other long-term liabilities
Sorting tips Inventory, receivables and prepaid expenses are current assets. PP&E and goodwill are long-term. Accounts payable and accrued salaries are current liabilities, and so is unearned revenue that will be earned within the next 12 months. Long-term notes payable are long-term liabilities.

Historical cost rule

Assets are recorded at what the company paid for them — not their current market value. A building bought for $2M twenty years ago still shows up at $2M (less depreciation), even if it's worth $5M today.

Unearned (deferred) revenue

Cash a company has received from a customer for a product or service it hasn't delivered yet. Since the company still owes something, it's recorded as a liability until earned — not revenue.

Common-sized Balance Sheets

Expresses every line item as a percentage of total assets. Useful for comparing a company against its own past years, against competitors, or against industry benchmarks, since it normalizes for company size.

Exam angleClassify a list of balance sheet items as current/long-term asset or liability — and be ready to explain the historical cost principle and why assets aren't restated to market value.

Statement of Stockholders' Equity

  • Common Stock and Additional Paid-in Capital increase by the proceeds received from stock issuances.
  • Retained Earnings increases by net income and decreases by dividends paid and by stock repurchased and retired.
  • Accumulated Other Comprehensive Income (AOCI) increases/decreases by changes in certain asset and liability fair values that are not run through the Income Statement.
Ending Retained Earnings = Beginning RE + Net Income (− Net Loss) − Dividends − Stock repurchased & retired

This roll-forward formula is tested constantly — memorize it cold, including working it backward to solve for a missing figure (e.g., net income given beginning/ending RE and dividends).

Income Statement (P&L)

Reports a company's financial performance — revenues, expenses, and net profit or loss — over a specific period of time, not a snapshot.

Statement of Cash Flows

Shows the company's ability to generate cash: sources of cash, and what it did with it. Structured around the same three activity categories from Unit 1: Operating, Investing, Financing.

The arithmetic you must be able to do

QuestionWorkAnswer
Net income: revenue $950M, COGS $560M, other operating expenses $220M, tax $38M950 − 560 − 220 − 38$132M
Ending RE: beginning $195M, NI $48M, dividends $22M195 + 48 − 22$221M
Ending RE with another $3M decrease: beginning $160M, NI $38M, dividends $15M160 + 38 − 15 − 3$180M
Back-solve NI: beginning RE $195M, ending $221M, dividends $22M221 − 195 + 22$48M
Ending cash: beginning $45M; operating +130, investing −95, financing −1545 + 130 − 95 − 15$65M

Dividends are not an expense: they reduce retained earnings but never net income.

Exam angleExpect pure arithmetic: net income from the income statement lines, a retained earnings roll-forward (forward or backward), and ending cash from the three activity totals. Show the formula first, then plug in.

Linkage mini-puzzle

Beginning retained earnings are $60 and the company paid $12 of dividends. The balance sheet shows ending retained earnings of $83. Then net income must be 83 − 60 + 12 = $35, and the income statement has to show that same $35. If the cash flow statement ends with $101 of cash, the balance sheet must show $101 of cash.

Exam angleGiven partial data across two statements, use the table (and the diagram) to find the missing connecting figure: net income links the income statement to equity, retained earnings links equity to the balance sheet, and ending cash links the cash flow statement to the balance sheet. Also be ready to apply ROA and ROE with average balances.

Big idea

An auditor is an independent professional who tests a company’s statements and then gives an opinion. The opinion is about whether the statements are presented fairly, not about whether the company is a good investment.

OpinionPlain meaning
Unqualified (“clean”)Fairly presented, in all material respects, under the applicable framework (GAAP)
QualifiedFair “except for” one specific problem
AdverseNot fairly presented
DisclaimerThe auditor could not gather enough evidence to give an opinion

What a clean opinion does not promise

  • Not that no fraud exists. Auditors give reasonable, not absolute, assurance.
  • Not that every transaction was examined. They test samples.
  • Not that the company is financially sound or will not fail.

Two separate opinions

For larger public companies the auditor gives an opinion on the financial statements and a separate one on internal control over financial reporting (the company’s system of checks). They can differ: a clean opinion on the statements can sit beside an adverse opinion on internal control (a material weakness). An adverse control opinion does not by itself mean the numbers are wrong, and it is not an accusation of fraud.

Exam anglePick the wording that matches the scope: “fairly presented, in all material respects, under the applicable framework.” Reject answers that say “no fraud,” “every transaction checked,” or “financially sound.” For the two-opinion question, the answer is that they can coexist.

Net Working Capital

Net Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities

A measure of a company's short-term liquidity — how much cushion it has to cover near-term obligations.

Cash Conversion Cycle

Cash Conversion Cycle = Avg. Days Sales Outstanding + Avg. Days Inventory Outstanding − Avg. Days Payable Outstanding

Measures how many days it takes to convert a sale into actual cash in hand — in other words, how long cash is "tied up" in the operating cycle before it's collected.

Book value vs. market value

  • Book value = Stockholders' Equity, as reported on the Balance Sheet (historical cost basis).
  • Market value (market capitalization) = shares outstanding × current stock price.

These frequently diverge — book value reflects historical cost accounting, while market value reflects the market's forward-looking expectations. Divergence isn't an error; it's expected.

Exam angleExplain why book value and market value diverge for a given company — anchor your answer in historical cost vs. forward-looking market expectations, not "one of them must be wrong."

Big idea

A cash flow statement is more than three totals. These questions turn the numbers into a story about the company’s health.

  • Is the company generating cash from operating activities?
  • Is that operating cash flow sustainable?
  • Is the company investing cash to grow infrastructure (PP&E) or enter new markets via acquisitions?
  • Is the company building liquidity with excess cash (e.g., marketable securities)?
  • Is the company paying down debt or paying dividends?
  • Is the company repurchasing stock?
Exam angleThis analytical question list is a ready-made rubric for a "read this cash flow statement and assess the company's financial health" prompt — walk through each question against the numbers given, rather than just reciting the three section totals.

Big idea

Public companies file reports with the SEC on a schedule, plus one for surprises.

A heads-up: some Unit 2 source notes list the 10-K and 10-Q the other way around (10-K as quarterly, 10-Q as annual). The table above uses the standard SEC convention (10-K = annual, 10-Q = quarterly). Double-check against your own course slides before you lock this in for an exam.
Exam angleGiven a scenario (an unplanned executive departure, a major acquisition, a data breach), identify which SEC filing the company would use to disclose it. 8-K is the one that most often trips people up, since it is the “everything else, right away” filing.

Big idea

Five institutions sit behind every financial statement you've studied so far. Know the relationships between them, not just isolated definitions — that's what actually gets tested.

InstitutionWhat it does
SECOversees financial markets; regulates public-company financial reporting
FASBNon-profit commissioned by the SEC to set accounting standards for public companies
GAAPThe rule book those standards produce — the actual accounting treatment rules
AuditorsFor-profit firms hired to test the veracity of a company's reporting and issue an opinion
PCAOBNon-profit commissioned by the SEC to oversee audit delivery and quality
SEC → FASB → GAAP (the rules)     SEC → PCAOB → Auditors (who verify compliance)

This is exactly the context behind the Amazon audit report from the HW Practice page — that report explicitly references "the standards of the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (United States) (PCAOB)," and this chain explains why: the SEC commissions the PCAOB, which oversees the auditors who verify that a company's statements comply with GAAP (the rules FASB sets under SEC authority).

Exam angleTrace the chain rather than defining terms in isolation: SEC sits at the top and commissions both FASB (rules) and the PCAOB (audit oversight) — that relationship is the actual exam-worthy insight, not the five definitions alone.

Big idea

Four historical flashpoints explain why the governance structure above exists at all — regulation is a direct response to these recurring failure patterns, not an abstract bureaucratic layer.

  1. The Great Depression — the historical origin point for federal securities regulation (the SEC itself).
  2. Enron and WorldCom (among others) — early-2000s accounting scandals that led to major reform, including the creation of the PCAOB.
  3. Auditor controversies — cases where auditors' independence or diligence was called into question.
  4. Continued fraud — an acknowledgment that financial statement fraud remains an ongoing risk despite the governance structure in place.
Exam angleIf asked "why does the governance structure (SEC/FASB/PCAOB) exist," this list is your historical justification — each institution above is traceable to one of these four failure patterns.

Anatomy of a public-company audit report

The homework hands you a real audit report and asks what it says. They follow a standard order:

  1. Title and addressee: “Report of Independent Registered Public Accounting Firm,” addressed to the shareholders and the board of directors.
  2. Opinion on the financial statements: names the statements and the dates they cover, and says they “present fairly, in all material respects” the financial position and results “in conformity with U.S. generally accepted accounting principles.” This is your clean-opinion sentence.
  3. Basis for opinion: management is responsible for the statements; the auditor is responsible for the opinion; the audit followed the standards of the PCAOB (the oversight body from the governance section); and the audit gives reasonable assurance whether misstatement is due to error or fraud.
  4. Critical audit matters (CAMs): the hardest, most judgmental areas of the audit. A CAM is a spotlight on difficulty, not a qualification.
  5. Opinion on internal control over financial reporting: often a separate section or report, with its own “effective” or “adverse” conclusion, plus a definition of internal control and its inherent limitations.
  6. Signature block: the audit firm’s name, city, the date, and the year it began serving as auditor.

Questions to ask while reading

  • Which statements, and which dates, are covered?
  • Is the opinion unqualified? Look for any “except for” or “do not present fairly.”
  • Is the internal-control opinion given separately, and is it clean?
  • Which institution’s standards are named, and why (PCAOB, set up by the SEC)?

The setup

Hartley Design is formed in January. Owners invest $100,000 for stock. During January it bills $90,000 of services ($60,000 collected, $30,000 still owed), pays $10,000 of rent, earns $30,000 of wages (pays $20,000, owes $10,000), buys $24,000 of equipment for cash, and pays a $5,000 dividend.

Step 1 — Income statement (accrual)

Revenue 90,000
Rent expense (10,000)
Wage expense (30,000)
Net income 50,000

Step 2 — Cash flow statement

Operating: 60,000 collected - 10,000 rent - 20,000 wages paid = +30,000
Investing: equipment = -24,000
Financing: 100,000 stock - 5,000 dividend = +95,000
Net change in cash (beginning cash 0) = 101,000

Notice operating cash flow ($30,000) is far below net income ($50,000): the gap is the $30,000 not yet collected less the $10,000 of wages not yet paid.

Step 3 — Balance sheet, January 31

Cash 101,000 + Receivable 30,000 + Equipment 24,000 = Assets 155,000
Wages payable 10,000 (liability)
Common stock 100,000 + Retained earnings (50,000 - 5,000 = 45,000) = Equity 145,000
Liabilities 10,000 + Equity 145,000 = 155,000 OK

Step 4 — Check the links

Cash on the balance sheet ($101,000) equals the cash flow statement’s ending cash. Retained earnings ($45,000) equals beginning $0 + net income $50,000 − dividend $5,000. If a check fails, hunt for a transaction you skipped.

A full roll-forward, column by column

A company starts the year with contributed capital (Common Stock + APIC) of $190,000 and retained earnings of $60,000. During the year it issues stock for $50,000, earns net income of $80,000, pays dividends of $25,000, and repurchases and retires $10,000 of stock.

Contributed capitalRetained earningsTotal
Beginning balance190,00060,000250,000
Stock issued+50,000+50,000
Net income+80,000+80,000
Dividends−25,000−25,000
Stock repurchased & retired−10,000−10,000
Ending balance240,000105,000345,000

This unit follows the class rule that retired repurchases reduce retained earnings, matching the roll-forward formula above. Check: total equity should also equal ending assets minus ending liabilities.

Back-solving

  • Missing repurchase: beginning RE 60 + NI 80 − dividends 25 − ending RE 105 = 10.
  • Missing equity from incomplete data: assets $600M and liabilities $380M give equity $220M. If next year no stock is issued, NI is $50M and dividends are $20M, then equity is 220 + 50 − 20 = $250M, and assets must equal liabilities + 250.
  • Every figure on a statement appears elsewhere: a missing number is almost always findable from a linkage. Ask which other statement shows it.

Every Unit 2 formula and definition on one page

Cash vs. accrual

Cash: when cash moves
Accrual: when earned / incurred
Hybrid: a blend

Credit sale: revenue + receivable now. Collection later: cash up, receivable down, no new revenue.

Accounting equation

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Equity = Assets − Liabilities

Every transaction keeps both sides equal.

Current vs. long-term

12-month test
Current: cash, A/R, inventory, prepaids, A/P, accruals, unearned rev
Long-term: PP&E, goodwill, LT debt

Balance sheet = snapshot at historical cost.

Which statement, which time frame

Balance sheet: one date
Income stmt, equity stmt, cash flows: a period

Unearned revenue is a liability until earned.

Net income & RE roll

NI = Rev − COGS − Opex − Tax
End RE = Beg RE + NI − Div − Other decreases

Back-solve: NI = End RE − Beg RE + Div. Dividends are not an expense.

Cash roll

End cash = Beg + Operating + Investing + Financing

Three sections: operating, investing, financing.

Statement links

BS ↔ IS: retained earnings
BS ↔ CFS: cash
BS ↔ Equity: stock/APIC, RE, AOCI
IS ↔ Equity: net income

Use to find a missing connecting figure.

Liquidity & value

NWC = CA − CL
CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO
Book value = equity
Market cap = shares × price

Book and market diverge by design: historical cost vs. expectations.

Audit opinions

Clean = fairly presented, in all material respects, under GAAP

Not a no-fraud guarantee. Statements and internal control get separate opinions.

SEC & governance

10-K annual · 10-Q quarterly · 8-K material event
SEC → FASB → GAAP
SEC → PCAOB → auditors

8-K: the “right away” filing.

Traps the exam sets

  • Recording revenue again when a receivable is collected. Collection moves cash and receivable only.
  • Treating a dividend as an expense. It cuts retained earnings but not net income.
  • Calling unearned revenue revenue. Cash received before delivery is a liability.
  • Forgetting a third item in the RE roll (stock repurchased/retired or another decrease).
  • Reading a clean audit opinion as “no fraud” or “financially sound.”
  • Swapping 10-K and 10-Q. 10-K is the annual report; 10-Q is quarterly.
  • Explaining a book-vs-market gap as an error instead of historical cost vs. expectations.
  • Using ending balances when ROA or ROE needs averages.
