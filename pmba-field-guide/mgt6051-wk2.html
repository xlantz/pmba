Week 2: Leadership

Leadership styles shape both performance and satisfaction: the Toy Factory showed four styles in action, Jordan, Wade & Yokoi argue for a broad “sweet range” of behaviors across seven tensions, and Kouzes & Posner describe five practices of exemplary leaders built on credibility.

How the week is organized

Class opens with your own experience of great and not-great leaders, then the Toy Factory exercise (four leadership styles), then a debrief. The readings give two lenses on the same question: Jordan et al. say leaders need a range of behaviors, and Kouzes & Posner describe five practices of leaders at their best.

The exercise in one paragraph

Teams made origami cranes in a “toy factory”: a production task that is complex but not creative. Goal: as many high-quality cranes as possible; poorly made ones were audited and not counted. You had 20 minutes to set up, produce and return, with 2 cranes deducted per minute late, and extra paper meant finding the professor (a tradeoff between paper and lost time). Each team's leader was a confederate coached privately to enact one of four styles. For every crane made, 50 cents went to children's cancer research (Huntsman Cancer Foundation). Exercise credited by the professor to Dr. Katy DeCelles.

The four styles

StyleWhat the leader doesTypical resultWhen / why
L: Laissez-faireLimited instructions and contact; often no feedback or goals.Less satisfied followers, low performance (frustrated, neither productive nor satisfied).Almost never the best fit.
D: Directive / autocraticGives directions and demands, maintains standards, sole expert, no input sought, often punishes failure; assigns tasks, stresses procedures.High performance, low satisfaction (followers stressed).Works in high-stress or urgent situations, emergencies, time pressure.
P: ParticipativeConsults the team, asks for input, treats followers as equals, encourages suggestions.Satisfied followers, medium-high performance.Raises motivation, decision quality and acceptance; but can take time and has group decision-making limits.
T: TransformationalFrames the work as meaningful; in the exercise leaders were shown how to increase task significance.Followers both more productive and more satisfied.Intrinsic motivation; meaning comes from framing, influence and motivational factors.

Why the Transformational result matters

Same task, same paper: the difference was meaning. Leaders told the team the cranes would go to children's cancer research, which raised task significance and so motivation. The crane story: Sadako Sasaki was two when the Hiroshima bomb fell (Aug 6, 1945), developed leukemia and died at 12; legend says folding 1,000 cranes grants a wish, and she wished for world peace. The crane is now a symbol of peace, health and childhood cancer support.

Example (invented)

Example: A call-center manager says only “hit your numbers” and disappears (laissez-faire). Another posts scripts, times every call and docks pay for misses (directive). A third runs a weekly huddle where agents propose fixes (participative). A fourth shows agents how their calls helped a customer keep their home (transformational: task significance).

Can leaders be trained?

The slides note this is a behavior view of leadership: if effective leadership is a set of behaviors, you can train leaders. Research began with the assumption that leaders were born, but leaders can emerge. They also ask: are all these behaviors needed, and what decides which to use?

What the quiz may probeName the style from a short scenario; match style to the typical performance/satisfaction pair; explain why transformational leaders did best (task significance, meaning); say when directive is appropriate (urgent, high-stress).

The big idea

Published in HBR (Jan 11, 2022) after surveys and interviews with over 1,000 managers worldwide. The authors argue the story “out with command-and-control, in with agile and collaborative” is too simple: during Covid-19, top-down decision-making and tactical execution proved extremely valuable. So they describe seven leadership tensions between traditional and emerging approaches. What is outdated is a fixed style that ignores context. Instead of perfecting a sweet spot, build a sweet range: the wider it is, the more versatile the leader.

The seven tensions with an example each

Tension (traditional / emerging)The two polesConcrete example (invented)
Power Holder / Power SharerHolds power to provide reassurance / steps back and lets others leadExample: In a layoff crisis the CEO decides quickly and clearly (Holder); in a product redesign she lets the team pick the direction (Sharer).
Tactician / VisionaryShort-term logical plan / long-term visionExample: A team lead who plans each week's tasks (Tactician) also paints where the department will be in three years (Visionary).
Constant / AdapterConsistent message and path / adjusts course on new informationExample: A manager repeats the same core values to the team (Constant) but changes the roadmap when a competitor launches (Adapter).
Perfectionist / AcceleratorPerfection over speed / speed over perfectionExample: Audit-ready financial statements call for the Perfectionist; shipping a beta to learn from users calls for the Accelerator.
Intuitionist / AnalystDecides with gut / decides with dataExample: A hiring manager's instinct about a candidate (Intuitionist) is checked against structured interview scores (Analyst).
Miner / ProspectorDives deep to exploit an opportunity / looks externally for opportunities and threatsExample: Optimizing the best-selling product line (Miner) versus attending industry events to find new markets (Prospector).
Teller / ListenerGives directions from expertise / listens to learn from othersExample: A senior engineer tells juniors the fix (Teller); in a post-mortem she asks open questions first (Listener).

Three stages to broaden your range

StageWhat it involvesArticle's example
Stage 1: Understand yourselfCognitive self-awareness: your strengths and weaknesses, your comfortable baseline range. Be open to formal and informal feedback; notice what you avoid or what colleagues do not hand you.A leader's colleagues kept giving him deep-dive projects (Miner) rather than exploration (Prospector); feedback confirmed it and he worked on a more curious outlook.
Stage 2: Understand your environmentSituational awareness (sense and make sense of what is present; separate signal from noise, non-judgmentally). Emotional awareness: cognitive empathy for others' emotions and intrapersonal awareness of your own; cross-check gut signals with data.A consumer-goods leader learned in her annual 360 that she spoke more than she listened (Teller to Listener). A marketing director sensed team doubt, was over-relying on gut, and gathered data (Intuitionist to Analyst).
Stage 3: Broaden your rangeIf the right behavior is outside your range, bridge it by: (a) practicing micro-behaviors; (b) seeking role models; (c) looking inside or outside your team for help when closing the gap yourself would be inefficient.(a) Perfectionist toward Accelerator by deciding by end of day. (b) Finance executive learned from visionaries (Tactician to Visionary). (c) A fintech risk manager who adapted a lot asked strong communicators to add a consistent “red thread” (Adapter plus Constant).

The article ends on a learning loop: enact a behavior, reflect on what went well and what did not, improve, and in doing so amass new behavioral experiences.

What the quiz may probeName the seven tensions and their poles; sweet spot vs sweet range; the three stages in order; classify a scenario into a tension and direction; classify a bridging action as micro-behavior, role model or team help.

The core idea

Chapter 1 introduces the framework built from decades of the authors' research: The Five Practices of Exemplary Leadership, with two commitments each (ten in total). The listed sections are: The Five Practices of Exemplary Leadership; The Five Practices Make a Difference; Leadership Is a Relationship; and Putting It All Together: Credibility Is the Foundation.

Five Practices / Ten Commitments

PracticeCommitments
1 Model the Way1 Clarify values by finding your voice and affirming shared values
2 Set the example by aligning actions with shared values
2 Inspire a Shared Vision3 Envision the future by imagining exciting and ennobling possibilities
4 Enlist others in a common vision by appealing to shared aspirations
3 Challenge the Process5 Search for opportunities by seizing the initiative and looking outward for innovative ways to improve
6 Experiment and take risks by constantly generating small wins and learning from experience
4 Enable Others to Act7 Foster collaboration by building trust and facilitating relationships
8 Strengthen others by increasing self-determination and developing competence
5 Encourage the Heart9 Recognize contributions by showing appreciation for individual excellence
10 Celebrate the values and victories by creating a spirit of community

Personal-Best Leadership Experience, explained

In 1983 the authors began asking people to describe their Personal-Best Leadership Experience: a project, program or event they saw as their own best-practice leadership. They have collected thousands through written cases and interviews, and they focus on ordinary people leading teams, departments, schools and community groups rather than headline-making executives. The message: exemplary leadership is a learnable set of behaviors available to anyone, not a trait of a few stars.

Leadership is a relationship; credibility is the foundation

General level: leadership happens between people, so it depends on trust. Credibility means people believe your words and actions match. The authors' wider research (not necessarily this edition's chapter wording) has long found people want leaders who are honest, forward-looking, inspiring and competent.

The Leadership Practices Inventory (LPI)

The LPI is the authors' assessment: 30 behavioral statements, six per practice, launched in 2002 and widely used. In this course you take it in Week 2 and send it to others (at least 4 respond) so you see how others rate your behavior. The paper due 11/19 builds on it; the rubric is on Canvas.

Example: A project manager recalls her best leadership moment, a hospital scheduling overhaul. Mapping it: she clarified what the team valued (Model the Way), pitched a better future (Inspire), tried small pilots (Challenge), shared decisions (Enable), thanked people publicly (Encourage the Heart).

What the quiz may probeList the five practices and ten commitments; match a behavior to a practice; define Personal-Best Leadership Experience; know what the LPI is (30 statements, six per practice).

Where it fits

Chapter 3 Set the Example is the second commitment under Model the Way: aligning actions with shared values. Its listed sections are Live the Shared Values and Teach Others to Model the Values. It follows Chapter 2 Clarify Values (Week 1: find your voice, affirm shared values).

The logic (general level)

IdeaPlain-language meaning
Clarify values (Ch 2)Know what you and the team stand for.
Set the example (Ch 3)Make your daily actions match those values; people watch what you do, not only what you say.
Live the shared valuesWalk the talk: use your calendar, decisions and small habits to show the values.
Teach others to model the valuesHelp others behave in line with the values, so they are not only yours; this is how values become team behavior.

Link to Week 1: Ingram & Choi's values-alignment idea says the values a company states must show up in what it does. Chapter 3 is the leader's personal version: your behavior is the evidence.

Example (invented): A manager says “we value learning from mistakes.” In a meeting a junior colleague admits an error, and the manager first thanks her and asks what the team can learn. That visible behavior is setting the example; later she asks senior staff to respond the same way, teaching others to model it.

What the quiz may probeWhich practice and commitment is Set the Example? Spot a scenario where words and actions do not match (credibility problem); explain why leaders must also teach others to model values.

Opening activity: exemplary leadership

You wrote the initials of one amazing leader you worked with directly and described them with as many adjectives as possible, then did the same for a not-great leader, and compared with a neighbor. The aim: name the behaviors that differ. These feed your Reply to Topic and your LPI reflection.

Do these behaviors matter? The Gallup data

ItemDetail
SourceGallup Survey of the American Workplace (2000-2016); the slides say 2023 results are identical.
FindingAlmost 70% of American workers are “not engaged” or “actively disengaged.” About 70 million employees are described as “roaming the halls spreading discontent.”
Candidate causesLonger hours for the same pay; widening gap between top and bottom; workers seen as replaceable. The slides say these matter but the primary cause is the boss.

Behavioral leadership and range

The slides finish with the behavior view (leaders can be trained) and a three-stage summary of Jordan et al.: Understand yourself (know strengths and weaknesses, welcome feedback); Understand your environment (listen, read emotions, check your own feelings); Broaden your range (micro steps, role models, use your team).

Example: An analyst sees that engagement scores on her team fall after each manager change. She treats the boss as a main lever and asks what the better managers did differently.

What the quiz may probeRecall the Gallup headline (almost 70% not engaged or actively disengaged; primary cause the boss); recall that behaviors imply leaders can be trained; list the three stages.

How the pieces fit

LinkHow they connect
Toy Factory + Jordan et al.Toy Factory shows that each style has a payoff and a cost (directive: output but low satisfaction; participative: satisfaction but slower). Jordan et al. explain why: no style fits every situation, so leaders need range. Directive resembles Power Holder and Teller; participative resembles Power Sharer and Listener (study-aid mapping, not stated by the authors).
Transformational + K&PBoth say meaning and shared purpose drive effort. Transformational framing in the exercise echoes Inspire a Shared Vision and Encourage the Heart (my mapping, not from the sources).
Trainable behaviorsSlides: leaders can be trained. Jordan et al.: broaden range by micro-behaviors and role models. K&P: the Five Practices are behaviors the LPI measures.
LPI as Stage 1The LPI with at least 4 raters works like the 360 feedback Jordan et al. say builds self-awareness.
Set the Example + ValuesCh 3 follows Ch 2 and Week 1's values alignment: stating values is not enough, behavior must match.

Where they differ

  • Toy Factory presents four separate styles; Jordan et al. say stop choosing one: flex along seven spectrums.
  • Gallup says the boss drives disengagement; K&P frame leadership as a relationship built on credibility.
  • Directive looks weak on satisfaction in the exercise, yet Jordan et al. report top-down decisions were valuable in Covid. Context decides.
What the quiz may probeQuestions that ask you to choose a style or behavior for a scenario and justify it using context; or to name which reading supports a claim.
