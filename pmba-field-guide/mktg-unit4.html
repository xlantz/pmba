The big idea

A customer judges an offering on three elements: product, service and brand. This chapter covers the product (service is Chapter 9, brand Chapter 10); all three must be fused into one competitively attractive offering. To compete, a product must be differentiated, meaning made visibly different from rivals. Some products allow little variation (chicken, aspirin, steel) yet still carve out identities: Perdue chickens, Bayer aspirin, India’s Tata Steel. P&G sells Tide, Cheer and Gain, each with a separate identity. At the other extreme (automobiles, commercial buildings, furniture) the seller has an abundance of ways to differ.

The chapter opens with the Ford F-150 Lightning, an electric version of Ford’s best-selling pickup (part of a $50 billion global electric-vehicle investment). It had to keep everything the gas truck did, give buyers a reason to switch, and justify a higher price. Ford made it “the ultimate portable energy source”: multiple 120-volt outlets, a model home powered for up to three days during an outage, an electric tailgate that doubles as a workbench, and a watertight front trunk (“frunk”) holding up to 400 pounds in the space the engine used to occupy. The lesson: improve on the current model without removing what made it popular.

Differentiation can be a technological leap (Intuitive Surgical’s da Vinci robotic system) or a tiny tweak (a Chiquita sticker on a banana); De Beers ties its product to special occasions and Tiffany uses packaging.

Cautionary tale: Nokia

Nokia led cell phone sales for 14 years, then was passed by Samsung, even in its home country Finland. It failed to innovate and stay relevant: it did not respond to the iPhone, which reportedly failed Nokia’s “drop test” (a phone dropped on concrete from five feet at different angles). Nokia had spent $40 billion on R&D in the prior decade and was a smartphone pioneer, but chose not to build what the iPhone became. Consumers began to associate it with an earlier era of technology — “a fatal blow” in a fast-moving, technology-intensive market. Kodak is cited as another firm hurt by failing to keep improving.

What design is

Design is the totality of features that affect how consumers perceive a product’s look, feel and function. It delivers functional and aesthetic benefits and speaks to both the rational and emotional sides of the customer. As competition intensifies, design is a potent way to differentiate and position products and services.

The power of design

  • In a visually oriented culture, form, color and graphics carry brand meaning and positioning. Virginia Postrel (The Substance of Style): in a crowded marketplace, aesthetics is often the only way to make a product stand out.
  • Especially important for long-lasting durables such as cars; Tesla built a car that is both good for the environment and aesthetically appealing.
  • Design can make brand experiences more rewarding, and is spreading into categories where it once mattered little: Herman Miller office furniture, Viking ranges, Kohler fixtures.
  • Countries with design reputations: Italy (apparel, furniture), Scandinavia (functionality, aesthetics, environmental consciousness; Finland’s Marimekko fabrics and Fiskars), and the UK, where Dyson lifted vacuums, fans and hair dryers to “a high art.”

OXO: universal design and incremental improvement

OXO’s watchwords appeal to users from grandparents to grandchildren with small, frustration-relieving fixes: a salad spinner (inspired by a child’s toy carousel) that works by pushing a button instead of pulling a string or turning a knob; storage containers that open with a top button; a peeler with thick padded handles; tongs with a non-slip handle and a lock to stay closed.

Approaches to design

Design is not just a phase in creating a product; it is a way of thinking that runs through the whole marketing program. For the company a well-designed product is easy to manufacture and distribute. For the customer it is pleasant to look at and easy to open, install, use, repair and dispose of. The designer must balance all of these goals. There is no single widely adopted method:

Portfolio vs. line

A product portfolio (the chapter also says “product mix”) is all the products a company offers, across categories and lines. A product line is a group of related products sold by the same company. Examples: the iPhone portfolio includes headphones, cables and docks, armbands, cases, power and car accessories and speakers; NEC (Japan) has communication products and computer products; Michelin has three lines (tires, maps, restaurant-rating services).

Four dimensions of a portfolio

P&G’s lines are consistent because they are consumer goods through the same channels; they are less consistent in the functions they perform for buyers.

Depth worked out: the Tide example

Analyzing a product line

  • Companies normally develop a basic platform and modules that can be added to meet different customer requirements and lower production cost (carmakers build around a platform; homebuilders show a model home to which buyers add features).
  • Managers need sales and profit by item to decide what to build, maintain, harvest or divest, plus each line’s market profile and image.
  • A line holds products for different layers of customer needs and with different margins. Supermarkets earn almost nothing on bread and milk, reasonable margins on canned and frozen foods, and better margins on flowers, ethnic food lines and freshly baked goods. Maximize profit for the whole line.
  • Brand portfolios: multibrand firms focus on core brands. Hasbro emphasizes Transformers, Dungeons & Dragons, Nerf and G.I. Joe; P&G’s “back to basics” concentrated on brands above $1 billion in revenue, such as Tide, Crest and Pampers.
  • Modernizing: overhaul piecemeal or all at once? Piecemeal lets the firm see how customers and dealers react and is easier on cash flow, but lets competitors see changes and start redesigning. In fast markets (Intel, Qualcomm, Microsoft, Oracle, SAP) modernization is continuous, encouraging customers to migrate to higher-priced items; time improvements neither too early (hurting sales of the current line) nor too late (letting rivals build reputation).

Crocs: portfolio breadth as a rescue

Crocs’ plastic clogs (Boulder, Colorado, 2002) were a hit. The 2006 IPO raised $208 million, the largest ever in U.S. footwear; sales peaked at $847 million a year later. Then the recession and consumer fatigue pushed the stock to about $1 — a “near-death experience.” By 2011 it had rebounded past $1 billion in revenue by diversifying into 300+ models (boots, loafers, sneakers) so clogs were under half of sales, using multichannel distribution (wholesale to Kohl’s and Dick’s, online, 500+ own stores), growing internationally (over half of sales), and in 2022 acquiring Hey Dude.

General illustration (not from the chapter)

A bakery sells breads (6 items), cakes (5 items) and cookies (4 items). Width = 3 lines. Length = 6 + 5 + 4 = 15 items; average line length = 15 ÷ 3 = 5. If sourdough comes in 2 sizes × 3 crusts, its depth is 6. Adding a pastry line widens the mix; adding a seventh bread lengthens it; adding a new size deepens it.

How long should a line be?

Product line length is the total number of items in the line, and company objectives drive it:

ObjectiveChapter example
Up-sellingMercedes C-Class is an entry point that catches young buyers in the hope they upgrade as they get older and more affluent.
Cross-sellingHewlett-Packard sells printers as well as computers.
Hedge against economic ups and downsElectrolux sells appliances under different brand names in discount, middle-market and premium segments.
High share and growth vs. high profitabilityShare and growth seekers carry longer lines; profit seekers carry shorter lines of carefully chosen items.

Lines tend to lengthen over time: excess manufacturing capacity pushes managers to add items, and the sales force and distributors lobby for a fuller line. But costs rise (design and engineering, inventory carrying, manufacturing changeover, order processing, transportation, new-item promotion) until management stops development for lack of funds or capacity. A cycle of growth then massive pruning can repeat; managers should use sales and cost analysis to find deadwood that depresses profit. A company lengthens a line in two ways: line stretching and line filling.

Line stretching

Every line covers part of the total possible range (Mercedes sits in the upper price range). Stretching lengthens it beyond the current range, down-market, up-market or both.

Gap’s three tiers

Gap launched in 1969 targeting Baby Boomers with a wide selection of Levi’s, then moved to its own casual, comfortable clothing for a broader demographic. It bought Banana Republic in 1983 (travel- and safari-themed, redesigned for modern urban tastes) and launched Old Navy in March 1994 (Gap-like apparel at lower prices in large warehouse-style outlets). The result was a 3-tier portfolio with minimal planned overlap: Banana Republic (fashionable high end, price premium over Gap), Gap (high-quality basics with style) and Old Navy (basics and affordable trendy items at a discount). The challenge: keep the three vibrant and distinct; research at times found them “sitting on top of each other,” and Old Navy has cannibalized Gap sales. Athleta (2008) added more coverage. With unique brand names, a firm avoids negative equity transfer from a lower brand to a higher one but gives up some positive transfer; if the parent openly owns them (as Gap does), it acts as a “shadow endorser.”

Line filling

Line filling adds items within the existing range. Motives: incremental profit, satisfying dealers who complain of missing items, consumers’ desire for variety, becoming the leading full-line company, and plugging holes to keep competitors out. It is overdone if it causes cannibalization or confusion, or addresses no segment’s needs; if items look alike customers get confused, and if prices differ they may mostly buy the cheaper one. A new item should meet a genuine market need, not just an internal one. The infamous Edsel filled Ford’s internal need for a car between the Ford and Lincoln lines but not the market’s; Ford lost $350 million in the late 1950s.

Marketing Insight: when less is more

Managers usually assume more variety means more appeal, and the chapter lists the reasons it can help: match preferences better, give consumers flexibility, and let them explore the full roster. But large assortments can also hurt:

ProblemMeaning
Information overloadMore options and attributes to process than from a small assortment.
Choice overloadMore than one satisfactory option forces trade-offs (give up one attribute to gain another).
High expectationsShoppers expect to find the “perfect” option; if it fails to appear they may walk away without buying.

Fewer options can make purchase more likely, lower the rate of return and raise satisfaction. Two factors should guide line design: consumer goals and consumer expertise. “Expert” consumers with product knowledge and clear preferences benefit from large assortments more than “novices.”

General illustration (not from the chapter)

A mid-priced running-shoe brand adds a $60 budget model under a different name (down-market stretch with a new name: premium equity protected, equity built from scratch) and a $250 carbon-plate racer (up-market stretch). Adding a seventh mid-price color of the flagship that looks like the sixth is line filling — and if buyers simply switch from one color to the other, it is cannibalization.

Packaging as a marketing tool

Packaging is all the activities of designing and producing the container for a product. It can have up to three layers, and at least one should capture attention and set the product apart. The package is the buyer’s first encounter with the product — a “five-second commercial.” Distinctive packaging (Coke bottle, Pringles canister, Red Bull can, Kiwi shoe polish, Altoids, Absolut vodka) is part of brand equity.

Labeling

A label can be a simple attached tag or an elaborate graphic that is part of the package. Four functions:

  1. Identify the product or brand (Sunkist stamped on oranges).
  2. Grade the product (canned peaches labeled A, B, C).
  3. Describe it: who made it, where and when, contents, how to use it, how to use it safely.
  4. Promote it through graphics (360-degree shrink-wrapped labels allow bright graphics and more information than glued paper labels).

Labels need freshening (Ivory soap’s label has been redesigned at least 18 times since the 1890s). Law: the 1914 Federal Trade Commission Act held false, misleading or deceptive labels or packages to be unfair competition; the 1967 Fair Packaging and Labeling Act set mandatory labeling requirements, encouraged voluntary industry packaging standards and let federal agencies set rules for specific industries; the FDA requires nutritional labeling (protein, fat, carbohydrates, calories, vitamins and minerals as a percentage of recommended daily allowance) and has acted against misleading “light,” “high-fiber,” “natural” and “low-fat.” In the European Union a low-fat claim is allowed only at no more than 3 g of fat per 100 g of solids or 1.5 g per 100 ml of liquids.

What they are

All sellers are legally responsible for meeting a buyer’s normal or reasonable expectations. Guarantees and warranties are explicit or implicit promises that the product will perform as specified or that the seller will fix it or refund the money during a specified period. Expressed or implied, they are legally enforceable. A guarantee spells out compensation if the product fails to work as promised or as customers expect. Setting out the process for failures up front adds credibility, reduces functional and monetary risk, and creates “peace-of-mind” value. For the company it intensifies focus on the customer experience, establishes accountability, speeds development of performance standards and provides guidelines for recovering from failures.

Apple: product innovation plus marketing

  • Origin: founded 1976 by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak to make computers user-friendly at home and work. Apple II (1977) brought the first color graphics. The 1984 Macintosh, one of the first mass-produced computers with a graphical user interface, launched with a Super Bowl spot built on Orwell’s 1984. Jobs was fired in 1985 by John Sculley; a focus on short-term profit over innovation eroded Apple’s position, and by 1996 its viability was questioned. Apple bought NeXT in 1997, Jobs returned (CEO officially in 2000) and launched the iMac and iBook.
  • iPod (2001): “the Walkman of the 21st century,” paired with iTunes (99 cents per track; largest U.S. music vendor by 2008, largest in the world by 2010). 300 million iPods sold in ten years. The “Silhouettes” campaign worked across cultures. Apple added mass retailers like Best Buy to its own stores, quadrupling its outlets, and a halo effect lifted Mac share.
  • iPhone (2007): set a new smartphone standard (touch screen, virtual keyboard, iOS). Despite a $499 entry price and a two-year AT&T exclusive, the one-millionth phone sold 74 days after launch. The App Store (2008) let third-party developers sell apps with Apple keeping a 30% cut, creating an “app ecosystem” that transformed gaming and social media and enabled ride-sharing; it also drew antitrust suits.
  • iPad (2010), Apple Watch (2015) (fitness and health; sluggish start, then one of the fastest-growing categories), Apple TV, HomePod (2018) and Vision Pro (2024) mixed-reality headset, whose $3,500 price raised adoption doubts.
  • Reducing cyclical demand: device demand softens late in a product’s life as buyers wait for the next model, so Apple built recurring services (AppleCare, iCloud, News+, Arcade, Apple Music 2015, Apple TV+ 2019, Apple Card, Apple Pay, advertising, App Store fees, Google default-search payments). Services revenue more than doubled from $39 billion (2018) to $85 billion (2023).
  • Dependence: in 2023 the iPhone generated $200 billion, 52% of revenue, so slower iPhone sales drag the whole company. Apple spent over $25 billion on R&D in 2022 and is consistently ranked the world’s most valuable brand.

The big idea

A sale is not the finish line; it is the start of a relationship. Without loyalty, a company must keep spending to replace customers who left for a better deal. The chapter opens with Nintendo, which reinvented itself from a hanafuda playing-card company into toys and then video games (NES, Game Boy, Wii, Switch with over 100 million consoles sold). It nurtures loyalty with a stream of product innovations, community events, tournaments, social campaigns and brand partnerships, and by taking characters into movies and theme parks. The 2023 Super Mario Bros. Movie was wildly successful and lifted sales of Mario games, which the chapter calls a positive synergistic effect (the 1993 movie flopped). Customer loyalty itself is “the result of a company’s efforts to consistently deliver a positive product, service, and brand experience.”

The customer acquisition funnel: the 5 A’s (Figure 19.1)

The funnel narrows as fewer people reach each next phase. The five relatively distinct phases are awareness, appeal, ask, act, advocate.

PhaseWhat happensChapter detail
AwarenessGateway; customer encounters the offeringVia company communication, point of sale, or advocacy by other customers and collaborators. Usually several offerings, including ones recalled from prior experience.
AppealSelective eliminationCustomers do not consider everything they know; they form a consideration set of the most appealing.
AskInformation search on the consideration setFriends and family, media, the company; online reviews, price-comparison apps, trying products in stores.
ActMove from evaluation to actionMore than purchase: consumption and the post-consumption experience.
AdvocateReaction after consumptionFrom simple repurchase up to recommending without being asked (evangelists).

The funnel is not strictly linear. People loop back, re-evaluate, and may skip phases (an impulse buy has no consideration set). Advocates may not be buyers at all: Apple products are often advocated by non-buyers.

Example: Hoka moves people through the funnel

Founded in France in 2009 (Jean-Luc Diard and Nicolas Mermoud), Hoka shoes were chunky, cushioned and often called ugly, the opposite of sleek minimalist running shoes, yet offered stability and comfort. Deckers Brands bought Hoka in 2012 when sales were about $3 million; a little over a decade later sales were closing in on $2 billion, about 40% of Deckers’ revenue. The chapter links this growth to funnel stages: comfort appealed beyond athletes (nurses, waiters, postal workers), hybrid work let people try the shoes casually, and word of mouth took over as believers told their stories (advocacy). Hoka protected a premium price (about $125 to $175), widened distribution (REI, Foot Locker, Dick’s plus its own DTC) and launched the “Fly Human Fly” campaign in 2023.

Balancing acquisition and retention

Managers estimate conversion rates (the percentage at one stage who move to the next) to spot a bottleneck. The chapter’s example: if the percentage of recent users is much lower than the percentage of triers, something may be wrong with the product or service that blocks repeat buying.

General illustration (not from the chapter)

1,000 shoppers try a new snack (triers) but only 300 are still buying in the latest period (recent users). The trier-to-recent-user conversion is 300 / 1,000 = 30%. If every other stage converts at 60% or better, this is the bottleneck, so fix repeat-purchase reasons (taste, price, availability) before buying more awareness.

What satisfaction is

The chapter defines satisfaction as a person’s feelings of pleasure or disappointment from comparing the perceived performance of a product or service with expectations. It is the key to building loyalty.

  • Asymmetric effect: the negative effect of missing expectations is disproportionately stronger than the positive effect of exceeding them. Customers also judge a favored brand more kindly.
  • Where expectations come from: past buying experience, advice of friends and associates, public information and discourse, and promises from marketers and competitors. Set them too high and buyers are disappointed; too low and you do not attract enough buyers.
  • Satisfaction is not always the ultimate goal. Raising it by cutting price or adding service can lower profit, and spending on customers can divert money from other stakeholders (employees, dealers, suppliers, stockholders). Deliver high customer satisfaction and acceptable satisfaction to stakeholders.
  • Raising expectations and delivering: Kia entered the U.S. with low-cost, high-quality cars reliable enough for 10-year warranties; Amazon’s consistent on-time fulfillment raised expectations.
  • Why it matters more now: the internet lets dissatisfied customers reach hundreds, thousands or tens of thousands of others.

Quality is described as “fitness for use,” “conformance to requirements,” and “freedom from variation,” or “the totality of features and characteristics of a product or service that bear on its ability to satisfy stated or implied needs.” The seller has delivered quality when the product meets or exceeds customer expectations. What loyalty is

Loyalty is “a deeply held commitment to rebuy or repatronize a preferred product or service in the future despite situational influences and marketing efforts having the potential to cause switching behavior.” It is a continuum of strength, from plain satisfaction to advocacy and evangelism, where customers see the offering as part of their own identity and feel responsible for its success. Wegmans is the example: “Wegmaniacs” (superfans) packed its 2011 Northborough opening with nearly 25,000 people in a town of barely 14,000; in 2015 more than 4,000 people asked for a store near them. Its stores feel like a European open-air market, with in-store chefs, events, model trains and “telepathic” service. It grew from a 1921 produce pushcart to a $12.5 billion business in 2023 with about 53,000 employees.

The chapter names three of the most effective strategies: interact closely with customers, develop loyalty programs, and build brand communities.

Strategy 1: Interact closely with customers

Connecting employees directly with the people they serve motivates staff: a brief visit from a scholarship student raised university fundraisers’ weekly productivity by 400 percent, and a patient’s photo improved radiologists’ diagnostic accuracy by 46 percent. It also keeps customers engaged: those who believe the company is listening are more likely to stay loyal than to chase a better deal.

  • Roblox (began 2006, took off in the COVID-19 pandemic; over a half-billion daily users) lets brands like Nike (Air Max made for the platform), Gucci, Burberry and Walmart meet younger Gen Z consumers, with the longer-range aim of turning them into loyal customers.
  • Listening mechanisms: Deere has over 90 percent annual retention in some product areas and uses retired employees to interview defectors and customers. Build-A-Bear’s Cub Advisory Board of 6- to 14-year-olds gives a “paws up or down” verdict, and many in-store products are customer ideas.
  • Be a customer advocate: take the customer’s side, understand their point of view, and modify the offering to create greater value.

Strategy 2: Loyalty programs

Loyalty programs are promotional incentives that reward customers who buy frequently and in large amounts and that create cross-selling opportunities. Pioneered by airlines, hotels and credit card companies, they now include supermarket and drugstore cards and apps. Key ideas:

  • First mover wins: the first firm in an industry gains the most, especially if rivals are slow to respond. Once competitors copy, programs can become a financial burden for all.
  • Lock-in: some programs generate rewards in a way that creates significant switching costs. They can also give a psychological boost of feeling special and elite.
  • Lowe’s “doubled down” with two programs for two segments: MVPs Pro Rewards (2022) for professionals, who shop much more often, with discounts, rewards and prize chances; and MyLowe’s Rewards (2024) for DIY homeowners, with a gamified three-tier bronze/silver/gold points system, more points for sharing more information, personalized offers and affinity groups (pet owners, first-time homeowners, decorators).

The big idea

Customer relationship management (CRM) is the process of carefully managing detailed information about individual customers and all customer touch points to maximize loyalty. A related idea, customer value management, is the company’s optimization of the value of its customer base; it analyzes individual data on prospects and customers to build strategies to acquire and retain customers and drive their behavior. CRM lets firms give real-time service from individual account information and customize offerings, services, programs, messages and media.

Customization and the customer database

Customization adapts the physical product and/or the service experience so it is as personally relevant as possible to as many customers as possible, a throwback to a century ago when merchants knew customers by name. Tools: call centers, online, digital and mobile tools plus AI and data analytics. But do not over-automate: many customers still prefer a live representative, and employees can build bonds by personalizing relationships.

  • BMW: 500 side-mirror, 1,300 front-bumper and 9,000 center-console combinations; buyers get a video link to watch the car “born.” Loyal customers load up on options (more profit); detailed manufacturing and procurement cuts slack and inventory costs.
  • Coca-Cola Freestyle: a touchscreen dispenser that mixes 125 sparkling and still brands.
  • Customer database: the foundation of CRM, an organized collection of comprehensive information about individual customers or prospects that is current, accessible and actionable for lead generation, lead qualification, sales or relationship maintenance.
  • Recommendation systems may contribute 10 to 30 percent of an online retailer’s sales, but can backfire when recommendations keep missing (buy a few baby gifts on Amazon and your recommendations suddenly look less personal).

Permission marketing

Permission marketing is marketing to consumers only after gaining their expressed permission. It rejects “interruption marketing” by overly aggressive campaigns. Rising pressure: the EU general data protection regulation, Apple’s limits on tracking and Google’s move to phase out third-party trackers. A common format is asking users to opt in to app tracking. Caveat: personalization assumes consumers know what they want, but preferences are often undefined or conflicting, so the chapter suggests “engagement marketing” (firm and consumer working together) may be a better name.

Dealing with complaints

Counting complaints misleads, because very few dissatisfied customers complain; most think it is not worth the effort or do not know how or to whom, and simply stop buying. Customers whose complaint is resolved, especially quickly, are far more likely to return.

  • Make complaining easy: suggestion forms, toll-free numbers, websites, apps, email. 3M says over two-thirds of its product-improvement ideas come from listening to complaints.
  • Monitor proactively: since many do not complain, watch social media. JetBlue’s team monitors its X/Twitter and Facebook; after complaints about a fee for a folded bike in a standard-size bag it dropped the fee.

Practices to recover goodwill (in the chapter’s order):

  1. Set up a 24-hour, seven-day toll-free hotline (email, chat, phone, fax).
  2. Contact the complaining customer as quickly as possible; delay breeds dissatisfaction and bad word of mouth.
  3. Identify the true source of the dissatisfaction; some customers want a sign the company cares more than compensation.
  4. Accept responsibility; do not blame the customer.
  5. Resolve swiftly and to the customer’s satisfaction, weighing the cost of the fix against the customer’s lifetime value.

Not every complaint is valid; large firms attract opportunists. Taco Bell answered a lawsuit and rumors that its mix was mostly filler with full-page ads headlined “Thank you for suing us,” saying the mixture was 88 percent beef, and bought the search terms “taco,” “bell” and “lawsuit” so its response ranked first. Zero complaints is unrealistic; the aim is to balance satisfaction with the firm’s strategic and monetary goals. Another tactic: shape your public image in quiet times and leverage the goodwill in hard times (Nike, once targeted by critics using search engine optimization, now shows its environmental and community initiatives in search results).

Not all customers are worth keeping

Every company loses money on some customers. The 80/20 rule says 80 percent or more of profits come from the top 20 percent of customers. In extreme cases the most profitable 20 percent (per capita) produce over 100 percent of profits, the middle 60 to 70 percent roughly break even, and the least profitable 10 to 20 percent reduce profits. The implication: a firm could improve profit by “firing” its worst customers. Note that the biggest customers are not always the best (they demand service and discounts); the smallest pay full price but are costly to transact with; midsize customers who get good service and pay nearly full price are often most profitable.

What CLV is

Customer lifetime value (CLV) is the monetary equivalent of the value customers will create for the company during their tenure. It can be one customer’s value or the cumulative value of all customers (“the sum of lifetime values of all customers”), and it is also called customer equity. A profitable customer is a person, household or company whose revenue stream over time exceeds, by an acceptable amount, the company’s cost stream of attracting, selling and serving them. The emphasis is on the lifetime stream, not on a single transaction. CLV can be assessed individually, by segment or by channel. Many companies measure satisfaction, but few measure individual customer profitability; banks that linked transactions found they lose money on more than 45 percent of retail customers.

Measuring CLV

CLV is typically the net present value (NPV) of the stream of future profits expected over the customer’s lifetime purchases. Subtract the expected costs of attracting, selling and servicing the account from expected revenues, then discount at an appropriate rate (the chapter says, say, 10 to 20 percent, depending on cost of capital and risk attitudes). Results can reach tens of thousands of dollars or six figures. Common model factors: revenue from the customer, cost of acquiring and servicing, probability of repeat buying, likely tenure, and the discount rate. Also count strategic value from referrals and positive word of mouth; customers who give feedback and suggestions can drive even greater loyalty.

General illustration: CLV worked through (not from the chapter)

A customer costs $60 to acquire (today) and yields $100 per year in revenue minus servicing cost for 3 years. Discount rate 10%.

PV = 100/1.10 + 100/1.10² + 100/1.10³ = 90.91 + 82.64 + 75.13 = 248.69. CLV = 248.69 - 60 = $188.69.

Higher discount rate: at 20%, PV = 83.33 + 69.44 + 57.87 = 210.65, so CLV = $150.65 (the chapter’s range is 10 to 20%; a riskier firm discounts more).

Add repeat-buying probability: if only 80% of customers stay each year, expected profits are 100, 80, 64: PV = 90.91 + 66.12 + 48.08 = 205.11, so CLV = $145.11. This is why retention drives CLV.

Customer profitability analysis and activity-based costing

Profitability by customer is usually analyzed with activity-based costing (ABC), which identifies the real cost of serving each customer by the resources consumed. Revenue from the customer minus all costs. In B2B, costs include making and distributing the product, plus phone calls, travel to visit, entertainment and gifts. ABC assigns indirect costs (clerical, office expenses, supplies) to the activities that use them, not as a proportion of direct cost; both variable and overhead costs are tagged to each customer. Mismeasuring cost means mismeasuring profit and misallocating marketing effort. One time-based solution computes the cost of one minute of overhead and charges each activity for the minutes it uses.

General illustration (not from the chapter)

A supplier sells a client $10,000 with $6,000 product and delivery cost. The client makes 40 calls of 15 minutes; overhead is $2 a minute, so calls cost 40 x 15 x $2 = $1,200. Two site visits cost $400 each = $800. Profit = 10,000 - 6,000 - 1,200 - 800 = $2,000. A client who looks like $4,000 of profit on product margin alone is really half that.

Starbucks: experience, data and a rewards engine

Starbucks opened in 1971 selling only beans. Howard Schultz (joined 1982) brought the Italian coffeehouse idea, bought the company in 1987, and envisioned an “affordable luxury” and a “third place” between work and home. Expansion was planned: all stores company-owned, and a “hub” strategy of clustering stores in a new market, which often cannibalized 30 percent of a store’s sales but was offset by marketing and distribution efficiencies. Early on it did no advertising, relying on word of mouth, PR and the logo cup. By 2002 core customers visited 18 times a month, the highest frequency of any U.S. retailer.

  • Loyalty: Rewards members earned “stars” redeemable for drinks, food and merchandise, spent more and visited more often. Partnerships with Delta SkyMiles and Marriott Bonvoy let members link accounts. By 2024, mobile order and pay was over 30% of transactions, and over 60% of morning business in the U.S. came from Rewards members.
  • Digital benefits: convenience, customization and exclusive offers for customers; for the company a direct channel, individual-level data and a path to acquire members. In mid-2024 it said it would open the app to non-members and invest $600 million over three years to digitize stores and personalize better.
  • People and purpose: broad employee benefits (including part-timers, Bean Stock), turnover about half the retail rate; ethical sourcing with Conservation International (2001); cup goal of 100% compostable, recyclable or reusable by 2030.

Accor: loyalty across many brands

Founded 1967 (Novotel, Lille airport; Paul Dubrule and Gerard Pelisson), Accor grew by owning and acquiring brands from budget to luxury, so travelers could stay within the group as budgets changed. Loyalty evolved: Compliments from Accor Hotels (2001, cross-brand) → A|Club (2008, free, budget hotels excluded) → Le Club AccorHotels (budget hotels included, book with points) → ALL: Accor Live Limitless (2019, premium tiers, points for dining, app and site covering Live, Work and Play).

  • Asset-light strategy (from 2010): shift from owning to franchising and managing hotels, spending on brands and customer experience rather than real estate; by 2020 it had sold 96% of its hotels (contentious: six board members resigned).
  • Digital response: online travel agencies and alternative hospitality services (Airbnb, VRBO) threatened profits. Accor centralized its CRM databases into one platform, “Voice of the Guests,” with a recommendation engine, one-click booking and online check-in; over 93% of those who tried the welcome service wanted to use it again. A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow, because today’s dollar can earn interest. At a 5% rate, $1 today becomes $1.05 in a year ($1 × 1.05) and $1.1025 in two ($1 × 1.05 × 1.05). Reverse that to ask what a future dollar is worth today:

Source note. The formulas on the slides came through as scrambled symbols in our copy. Each one below was rebuilt from the definitions on the same slides, derived step by step, and checked numerically: closed form against a brute-force sum of 20,000 periods.

Notation: Mn or m = margin per period; i = interest (discount) rate per period; r = retention rate (the share of customers who stay each period; churn = 1 − r); AC = acquisition cost, paid at period 0; N = known average lifetime.

1. Known average lifetime (slide: “CLTV for known average lifetime N”)

CLTV = Σn=1..N Mn / (1 + i)n − AC

An NPV where the customer is the investment: AC is the outlay, the margins are the returns.

2. Using a retention rate (slide: “CLTV using retention rate”)

CLTV = Σn=1..∞ Mn · rn / (1 + i)n − AC

Instead of a fixed end date, a fraction r of customers survives each period, so the margin expected in period n is weighted by rn.

The derivation (both use the same trick)

Let a = r/(1+i). The “most common” series is m·(a + a² + a³ + …). Call S = a + a² + a³ + …. Then S = a + a·S, so S(1 − a) = a and S = a/(1 − a). Substitute a and multiply top and bottom by (1+i): S = r/(1 + i − r). So CLTV = m·r/(1 + i − r) − AC.

The case’s series is (m/(1+i))·(1 + a + a² + …) = (m/(1+i))·1/(1 − a) = m/(1 + i − r). Ignore AC until the last step, as the slides suggest.
