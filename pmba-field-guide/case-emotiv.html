Emotiv Systems, Inc.: It’s the Thoughts That Count

A start-up with a headset that reads brain signals must choose how to launch: wait for a console maker’s endorsement or ship for the PC, and settle price, channels and promotion along the way. The case, section by section

Case brief only: the analysis and recommendation are yours (course policy: no AI for case analyses). This page restates what the case says, checks its numbers, and explains the Unit 2 frameworks. It does not answer the prep questions.

The setting in one paragraph

It is October 2007. Tan Le, president and cofounder of Emotiv Systems, is awake past 2 a.m. in San Francisco, on the phone with CEO and cofounder Nam Do in Sydney. After about five years of R&D, the company has a wireless EEG headset (the EPOC) and software that classifies brain signals into 30 “mental states.” Management wants to launch for the 2008 holiday season, aimed at consumers through video games. The open questions are tangled together: which platform(s) to launch on, how to price, where to sell, what to put in the box, and how long to keep chasing a console maker before the 2008 Game Developers Conference (GDC) forces a public announcement.

The decision facing the protagonist

Le must decide how to respond to Do's push to keep seeking a console endorsement versus her instinct to launch PC-only, and then settle the linked choices. The case frames them as: platform and timing (including whether to delay the launch and what to do with Sony Europe's expiring “EPOClite” offer), price (skimming versus penetrating, with possible rebates), retail plan, an out-of-the-box experience, and the demand forecasts that Sony and Microsoft executives keep requesting. A longer-term vision (headsets worn all the time) frames everything.

Consoles: razor-and-blades plus network effects

Since the Atari 2600 (1977), the console model has been a low-priced box plus profitable game titles. A new generation arrives every five to seven years, and one maker usually ends up with over 50% share. The “winner-take-most” pattern comes from the games side: third-party studios license the right to publish and typically pay the console maker a royalty of about 10% to 15% of a game's retail price. More consoles attract more games, and more games attract more buyers (a network-effects loop). Game development costs jumped: roughly $3 million for a PS2 title five years earlier versus up to $20 million on the PS3 (similar for the Xbox 360). Console-enabled game sales (hardware and software) were expected to top $40 billion globally in 2007.

PC gaming

Games are computationally heavy, so players often add memory, graphics and sound cards costing thousands of dollars, and many build their own machines. Most titles target Windows, so developers do not juggle separate console development kits. Publishers pay no per-unit royalty to a platform owner, and users can easily modify games. PC and console sales tend to move in opposite directions: PC gaming gained when Atari titles disappointed in the early 1980s and has declined relative to the recent console boom (an estimated 40% of US households own a console). The PC market had become dominated by hard-core players of first-person shooters and online strategy games. Insiders said revival would need casual games that run well on an average computer.

Handhelds and Guitar Hero

Handhelds: Game Boy (1989) sold over 200 million units. Nintendo's DS ($149 at launch) and Sony's PSP ($249) both arrived in late 2004; by October 2007 about 52 million DS and 27 million PSP units had sold. Guitar Hero: developed by Harmonix and published by RedOctane, it launched in November 2005 on the PlayStation 2 with a guitar-shaped controller. Guitar Hero II (2006; Xbox 360 in 2007) sold an estimated 3 million copies, and the U.S. installed base of guitar peripherals was approaching 2 million by fall 2007. Activision bought RedOctane (June 2006); MTV Networks bought Harmonix (September 2006); Guitar Hero III was heading for all three consoles and the PC; Rock Band added drums and a microphone.

Measuring the brain: why EEG

MethodWhat it measuresCase's verdict
fMRIMagnetic properties of oxygenated blood; resolution in secondsMachine about $1 million, at least $200 per hour to run, van-sized: not feasible for mass markets.
PETInjected, radioactively labeled glucoseMore portable than fMRI but expensive, and consumers would not accept radioactive injections.
EEGElectrical activity through scalp electrodes; resolution in millisecondsSignal must cross the skull and membranes, so quality suffers; still preferred for its roughly $10,000-range cost and portability.

Emotiv's R&D replaced the tangle of wires and conducting gel of standard EEG with a wireless, gel-free headset (Exhibit 3). The harder problem was software: academic EEG research averaged many noisy readings to find where processes happen in the brain, whereas Emotiv needed real-time classification of a person's state. The team used machine learning: algorithms trained on labeled examples that are then tested on new data (handwriting recognition was an early success of the approach).

The demo program shows whether all 16 sensors have contact, then lets the user train a floating 3D cube: think “pull the cube toward me” several times so the algorithm learns your pattern, then try it. After training, most users could control one cognitive state; adding more states takes much more practice because signals for similar thoughts overlap, and a person's brain activity for the same thought varies from day to day. Le worried about expectation gaps: at a 2005 game developers' conference someone asked whether the headset could move a chair across a room.

How the company got here

Le, a Vietnamese-born refugee who arrived in Australia at age four, was named 1998 Young Australian of the Year. She and Do built and licensed a text-messaging capability to a telecom company within a year, sold their company in 2003 as 26-year-old multimillionaires, and then teamed with physicist Allan Snyder and chip designer Neil Weste. They opened in Sydney in December 2003 with about $1 million of their own money, deliberately hiring mathematicians, engineers, cognitive psychologists and graduate students rather than EEG specialists. A breakthrough in late 2004 came after about 25,000 person-hours. Investors (notably Technology Venture Partners and Epicure Capital Partners) supplied $17 million.

The console-endorsement idea

Under the arrangement Emotiv hopes for, a console maker would build a driver so its machine recognizes the headset and would extend its software development kit to support brain-wave games. The maker's brand would appear on the packaging alongside Emotiv's, with some marketing support (ad mentions, shelf space). Emotiv would make and sell the headset and pay the console maker a royalty, typically 3% to 5% of the peripheral's retail price. The hope is that a big-name endorsement lifts demand, even among PC gamers, in the way Guitar Hero's console backing did.

Other launch decisions in the case

Pricing. Retailers (including Best Buy) say $399 is about the ceiling given console prices. They also say prices above $100 usually end in “99” and that something like $279 reads as close to $299, so the next sensible step below $299 is $199. No video game accessory had sold at $199; Guitar Hero's accessory sold at $99; medical EEG systems cost $10,000 or more (“high tech at a relatively low-tech price”). Do is open to dynamic pricing (one price at launch, a change in years two and three). Le is weighing a high base price with a large consumer rebate versus a lower price with no rebate; retailers say promotions should run in about 20 special weeks a year. The case also raises skimming versus penetration pricing.

Channels. Because people struggle to grasp the product until they try it, management believes it needs traditional retail with demos and physical inspection. Best Buy looks like the ideal partner; Toys R Us seems aimed at younger, more casual players than Le expects to buy EPOC (“kids may not have the money or patience”).

Out-of-the-box experience. Do is considering a bundled CD tutorial. Demiurge Studios (Cambridge, MA; worked on early Wii titles) quoted about $2.5 million for a martial-arts-master experience (lift a rock by thought; walk on water by staying calm). Le has not decided.

Unit 2 pairs this case with Chapters 6, 17 and 18. The ideas below are generic; the examples use made-up products so you can practice applying them to Emotiv yourself.

1. Segmentation, targeting and positioning (Chapter 6)

Market segmentation divides a market into groups of customers who share needs, behaviors or characteristics and who respond similarly to marketing. Common bases:

BaseExample variablesMade-up example
Demographicage, income, household size, life stageA meal-kit firm splits families with kids from singles.
Geographicregion, climate, urban/ruralA snow-tire maker sells different bundles by region.
Psychographiclifestyle, values, personality, attitudesAn outdoor brand targets “weekend adventurers” versus “gear collectors.”
Behavioralusage rate, benefits sought, loyalty, readiness to buy, occasionA software firm separates heavy daily users from occasional users.

For B2B markets, firmographic bases (industry, company size, location) and purchasing approach are used alongside these. A segment is useful only if it is measurable, substantial enough to be worth serving, accessible through channels and media, differentiable (segments respond differently), and actionable (you can build a program for it). Check any proposed segment against that list.

Targeting chooses which segments to serve. Common patterns: undifferentiated (one offer for all), differentiated (separate offers per segment), concentrated / niche (focus on one segment), and micromarketing (down to individuals). Segment attractiveness is usually judged on size, growth, profitability and competition; fit with company strengths and resources is the other half. A beachhead (a first segment chosen so the product can grow outward later) is a related idea you will hear in innovation settings.

Worked example (made up). A start-up sells a smart water bottle that tracks hydration. Possible segments: marathon runners (psychographic and behavioral; reachable via race expos and running forums; small but passionate), office workers told to drink more water (large, harder to reach), and nursing homes (organizational buyers, a different purchasing process). The founder asks of each: how large, how reachable, how differentiated in needs, how well does it fit what we can deliver? That is the structure of the reasoning, applied here to a toy example, not to Emotiv.

3. Adoption and diffusion of innovations (Chapters 6, 17, 18)

GroupShareDescription
Innovators2.5%Venturesome; enjoy novelty and technology, tolerate bugs.
Early adopters13.5%Opinion leaders and visionaries; adopt for a strategic or social edge.

What speeds adoption (Rogers' five product traits): relative advantage over what people use now, compatibility with existing habits and systems, low complexity, trialability (can people try it cheaply), and observability (can people see others using it). Moore's chasm is the gap between early adopters and the early majority: the groups want different things, so success with enthusiasts does not guarantee a mainstream market. Ask of any product: what does an early adopter get that a pragmatist will not accept, and what evidence would a pragmatist need? A related Chapter 18 idea is the Stage-Gate process, which screens ideas for desirability, feasibility and viability before heavy investment.

Worked example (made up). A foldable-keyboard gadget has high relative advantage for frequent travelers (innovators and early adopters buy it), but office workers see it as hard to try without handling it and incompatible with their desk setup, so sales stall: a chasm. The firm responds by choosing one pragmatic niche (court reporters, say), delivering a complete package for them, and using their references to cross.

4. Platform strategy: console endorsement versus being “enabled” on a PC

Platforms (consoles, operating systems, app stores) are products whose value rises with the number and quality of complementary products (games) and users: network effects. Two-sided structure: the owner serves developers on one side and consumers on the other, usually earning royalties on content, which is why console makers charge per-game fees and why PC publishers, with no platform toll, keep more of each sale.

ConceptMeaning
Chicken-and-egg problemNeither side commits first: buyers wait for complements, and developers wait for buyers. Solutions include subsidizing one side, bundling first-party content, or securing an anchor partner.
Endorsement / co-brandingA platform owner lends brand, integration and shelf space, usually for a royalty. It signals legitimacy and can lower perceived risk for buyers and developers.
“Enabled” or compatibleThe product works with a platform without the owner's blessing or support. You bear the integration cost but keep independence.
First-party vs third-partyFirst-party content is made by the platform owner; third-party content comes from outside publishers and developers.
Open vs closed ecosystemOpenness lowers barriers for developers (PC); control and curation raise quality and let owners tax content (consoles).

Worked example (made up). A start-up makes a new e-reader format. Bookstores will not stock titles without devices; buyers will not buy devices without titles. One path is to launch on the open web (no gatekeeper, slower reach), another is to win a big retailer's endorsement (reach, but dependence and a royalty). Compare the two on speed, cost, control and risk if the partner says no or stalls. The structure is the lesson: which path gets to critical mass, at what cost, in what time?

1. Forecasting by analogy (the Guitar Hero question, method only)

When a product is new and has no sales history, a forecast by analogy borrows the sales path of a comparable earlier product and scales it. It is a judgment-based method, quick and intuitive, but only as good as the comparison. The steps:

StepWhat you do
1. Pick the analog(s)Choose past products that resemble the new one on the traits that drive demand. More than one analog improves credibility.
2. Align the timelineLine up the new product's year one with the analog's year one (launch-year to launch-year, not calendar year to calendar year), and treat partial years carefully.
3. Choose a scaling factorExpress the new product's sales as a percentage of the analog's at each stage, and justify why it would be higher or lower.
4. Compute the pathNew product units in a year = analog units in the matching year × scaling percentage. Cumulate units to get an installed base.
5. Stress-testRun conservative and aggressive scenarios; compare to market size and what the market could absorb; revisit as real data arrive.

Checklist for judging the fit of an analog: same type of buyer? similar price? same platform and distribution? similar need being served? comparable awareness and promotion? similar learning effort for the user? similar competitive situation and timing? Also ask what is different (novelty of benefit, reliability, complements available), because that is where the analogy can break.

Worked example (made up). A firm plans a smart guitar tuner and chooses a popular fitness tracker as the analog because both are consumer wearables. The tracker sold 100,000, 400,000 and 700,000 units in its first three years. The firm chooses scaling factors of 30%, 30% and 30%, giving 30,000, 120,000 and 210,000 units (cumulative 360,000). Before trusting it the firm checks fit: trackers are mass-market and sold at electronics chains; guitar tuners serve a smaller group of musicians. So the firm lowers the factors, and runs a high scenario too. Notice that the percentages come from reasoning about fit, not from the arithmetic.

For the case, remember what Exhibit 11 gives you: Guitar Hero's unit sales by year and two sets of percentages Emotiv considered. Whether the Guitar Hero analogy is a good one depends on how well the traits above match the EPOC's situation. In particular, the case notes how Guitar Hero launched (as a game plus a peripheral, on consoles). The question “Is this ever going to be a mainstream product?” is separate from the forecast: link it to the adoption curve and the chasm idea above.

ChannelDirect versus indirect distribution, intermediary margins, market development funds, intensive versus selective distribution, and fit between channel and product (experience goods may need demos). Each channel changes the cost to serve, the reach, and the customer's first impression.pp. 11 to 12
PromotionTrial and demonstration, public relations and trade shows, advertising, word of mouth in communities, consumer promotions such as rebates, and the timing of campaigns around buying seasons. Pull promotion builds consumer demand; push promotion motivates the channel.pp. 7, 10 to 12, 14
Product (as offered)Core product plus the bundle: tutorial software, EmoKey, supporting games, and what the first experience feels like.pp. 10 to 12

Worked example (price-to-contribution waterfall, made up). A gadget retails for $100. The retailer keeps 30% ($30). A platform royalty of 5% of retail price is $5. If the unit costs $40 to make, what is left is $100 − $30 − $5 − $40 = $25 per unit. Dividing fixed costs of $1,000,000 by $25 gives 40,000 units to break even. Repeat with a different price or channel and watch how the break-even point moves. For the case, you will build your own waterfall using the raw inputs in the numbers block.

Skimming versus penetration, in short: skimming makes sense when early adopters value the product highly, demand is relatively price-insensitive at first, competitors are slow to arrive, and high unit costs make low prices unprofitable; penetration makes sense when scale lowers costs, the market is price-sensitive, there is a threat of fast imitation, or the goal is to build an installed base for complements. In both cases the key is how each plays out against cash, cost curve and competitors for the specific company.
