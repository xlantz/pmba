What counts as a service?

A service is an act that one entity performs for another that is essentially intangible and does not result in the ownership of anything. It may or may not be tied to a physical product. Services are everywhere: government (courts, schools, police), nonprofits (museums, charities), most of the business sector (airlines, banks, law firms), manufacturers’ “service factory” staff (accountants, computer operators), and retail workers. As physical products get harder to differentiate, firms turn to service differentiation (on-time delivery, faster responses, quicker complaint resolution).

Five categories of offering, by how much service is involved

CategoryExample and logic
Pure tangible goodSoap, toothpaste, salt — virtually no accompanying service.
Tangible good with accompanying servicesCar, computer, or phone with a warranty or service contract. The more technologically advanced the product, the more it needs strong supporting services.
Hybrid offeringRestaurant meal — equal parts goods and service (people come for the food and its preparation).
Major service with minor goods and servicesAir travel: the airplane is a capital-intensive good, but the primary item is the service; snacks and drinks are supporting goods.
Pure serviceBabysitting, psychotherapy, massage — primarily intangible.

Worked example: Domino’s

By 2009 Domino’s sales were falling. Research found its “delivery speed” reputation had stopped mattering and was even hurting taste perceptions (some said it tasted more like the box than a pizza), and its impersonal, efficient image meant customers pictured no kitchens, chefs, or ingredients. The comeback: well-liked new recipes for crust, sauce, and cheese plus a transparent “Oh Yes We Did” campaign admitting the old problems. The text reports revenue doubling and stock up 5000% over the next 8 years. Domino’s then invested in technology (Pizza Tracker, hot-spot delivery, AI, “smart tools”) and calls itself a “tech company that delivers pizzas.”

How hard is the service to evaluate?

Benefit typeDefinition
Search benefitsBuyer can evaluate before purchase.
Experience benefitsBuyer can evaluate after purchase.
Credence benefitsBuyer finds them hard to evaluate even after consumption (e.g., the outcome of treatment).

Services are generally high in experience and credence qualities, so purchase risk is higher. Four consequences: (1) buyers rely on word of mouth more than advertising; (2) they lean on price, provider, and physical cues to judge quality; (3) they are highly loyal to providers who satisfy them; (4) switching costs are high, so inertia makes it hard to lure customers away.

Source note: The chapter’s Publix revenue figure is OCR-garbled (“S48 billion”) and is not used here.

Exam angleClassify an example into one of the five offering categories; label a benefit as search, experience, or credence; list the four consequences of high purchase risk.

The big picture

Researchers distinguish services from products by four characteristics: intangibility, inseparability, variability, and perishability. Each creates a problem and calls for a response. The first three are covered here; perishability gets its own section next.

Intangibility

Services can’t be seen, tasted, felt, heard, or smelled before purchase, so buyers infer quality from the place, people, equipment, communication material, symbols, and price. Providers “tangibilize the intangible”: signage, reception area, employee apparel (Allied Van Lines drivers), branded trucks (UPS), fresh-baked cookies (Doubletree), brand elements such as United’s “friendly skies,” Allstate’s “good hands,” Merrill Lynch’s “bull,” Disney’s theme parks, and Apple’s Genius Bar. Banks historically used imposing buildings and stadium naming rights to signal stability.

Inseparability

Goods are made, stored, shipped, then consumed; services are typically produced and consumed simultaneously. A haircut can’t be stored or produced without the barber, and the client is often present, so provider–client interaction is a special feature of services marketing. If clients strongly prefer a provider, the provider can raise price to ration limited time. Ways around the limit:

StrategyExample from the text
Larger groupsPsychotherapists moving from one-on-one to small groups, even 300+ people in a hotel ballroom.
Work faster30 efficient minutes per patient instead of 50 less structured ones.
Virtual appointmentsDone from home rather than the office.
Train more providersH&R Block’s national network of trained tax consultants.

Variability

Quality depends on who provides the service and when, where, and to whom, so firms risk inconsistency. Buyers know this and ask others or go online; service guarantees can reduce perceived risk. Three quality-control steps:

StepDetail
1. Invest in hiring and trainingBetter-trained people show six traits: competence, courtesy, credibility, reliability, responsiveness, and communication skill.
2. Standardize the processUse a service blueprint to map the process, customer contact points, and evidence from the customer’s view.
3. Monitor satisfactionSuggestion and complaint systems, surveys, third-party comparison shopping; customer databases with analytics and AI for personalization.

Service blueprints

A blueprint plays the role an architect’s plan plays for a house: a detailed visual depiction, start to finish, taking the customer’s point of view. It helps develop new services, build a zero-defects culture, devise recovery strategies, and spot “pain points.” Its five components appear in the diagram below using the text’s overnight hotel-stay illustration. It must balance simplicity and complexity, and be flexible enough for customers who want to coproduce the service.

Worked example: ARAMARK Parks & Resorts used blueprints to find why repeat business was falling at Lake Powell Resorts & Marinas. The cause: customers had to expend too much effort themselves. After overhauling processes, facilities, and training to ease that burden, complaints dropped and repeat business rose.

Exam angleName the characteristic from a one-line scenario (haircut = inseparability; same hotel, different clerk = variability). Know the three variability steps and the five blueprint components, including backstage vs. support processes.

The problem

Services can’t be stored, so perishability hurts when demand fluctuates. Public transport must own enough equipment for rush hour even if it sits idle midday; a doctor charges for missed appointments because the doctor’s availability exists only at that moment. The goal of demand (yield) management is the right service, to the right customers, at the right place, time, and price.

Demand-side strategies

StrategyExample
Differential pricingShifts demand from peak to off-peak: matinee prices, weekend car-rental discounts.
Cultivate nonpeak demandMcDonald’s all-day breakfast; hotel minivacation weekends.
Complementary servicesGive waiting customers alternatives: cocktail lounge in a restaurant, ATMs in a bank.
Reservation systemsAirlines, hotels, and physicians manage demand levels.

Supply-side strategies

StrategyExample
Part-time employeesColleges add part-time teachers when enrollment rises; stores hire holiday clerks.
Peak-time efficiency routinesStaff perform only essential tasks at peak (paramedics assist physicians).
Increased consumer participationCustomers fill out their own medical records or bag groceries.
Shared servicesHospitals share medical-equipment purchases.
Facilities for future expansionAn amusement park buys surrounding land.

Yield management and dynamic pricing

Yield management is a flexible pricing strategy to raise profit from time-limited, fixed inventory based on predictable demand swings; it sets prices to optimize planned demand given available capacity (a hotel cuts prices in low season and raises them in high demand). Dynamic pricing adjusts prices in real time: Cintra’s Dallas toll roads can change prices every five minutes to keep speeds above 50 mph; sports teams, bands, and theme parks vary prices with demand.

Worked example (drive-throughs): They expand selling opportunities beyond sit-down meals, producing more than 50 percent of fast-food industry revenue; the fastest chains (KFC, Taco Bell, Hardee’s, Dunkin’) take around five minutes.

Exam angleClassify a tactic as demand-side or supply-side. Distinguish yield management (predictable fluctuations) from dynamic pricing (real-time adjustments).

Four realities

Service firms once lagged manufacturers in marketing; now some of the best marketers are service firms. Savvy services marketers recognize four new realities:

RealityWhat the text says
Increasing role of technologyBanking via apps means some customers never see a lobby; COVID-19 accelerated digital transformation. Technology boosts worker productivity, but pushing efficiency too hard can lower perceived quality (Amazon keeps customers satisfied even without talking to an employee; live chat blends tech with a human voice). Collected data raises security and privacy concerns.
Customer empowermentCustomers push for “unbundled services” and one provider for many items. Social media let them broadcast comments worldwide; 90% of angry customers told a friend, and now strangers too (Angi, Yelp, TripAdvisor, damaging videos). Firms respond fast, 24/7, and monitor blogs and social media (Delta, Southwest). Preventing dissatisfaction matters more than responding.
Customer coproductionCustomers play an active role in delivering the service. They feel more value and connection when engaged, but it can stress employees. One study estimated one-third of service problems are caused by the customer.
Satisfying employees as well as customersPositive employee attitudes strengthen loyalty; market a career, not just a job; train, support, and reward; use intranets, newsletters, daily reminders, roundtables; audit employee satisfaction regularly.

Worked examples

Twilio (cloud communications platform): over 60,000 business customers (Airbnb, Uber, eBay and others); acquired email platform SendGrid in 2019. Nubank (São Paulo, founded 2013): a digital bank born from frustration with delays and fees, now serving over 50 million customers in Brazil, Mexico, and Colombia, using customer data to personalize offerings.

Managing customer coproduction failures

Recovery is hard because of attribution: customers think the firm is at fault or responsible even when it isn’t. Firms handle their own failures better than customer failures (problems from a customer’s mistake or lack of understanding). Solutions:

SolutionExample
Redesign processes, redefine customer rolesStaples’ “Easy” program.
Add the right technologyComcast software to spot network glitches early and inform call-center staff.
Create high-performance customersClarify role, motivation, and ability (USAA reminds enlisted policyholders to suspend car insurance when stationed overseas).
Encourage customer citizenshipCustomers help one another (golfers encouraging proper play).
Exam angleMatch an example to one of the four realities; explain why customer failures are harder to manage than firm failures (attribution).

External and internal marketing

External marketing is the normal work of preparing, pricing, distributing, and promoting the service. Internal marketing is training and motivating employees to serve customers well. The marketing department’s most important contribution may be getting everyone else in the organization to practice marketing. Delegating authority to frontline staff can add flexibility and adaptability through better problem solving, cooperation, and knowledge transfer.

Four best practices of top service companies

PracticeKey points
Customer-centricity“Customer obsessed”: clear target customers and a distinctive strategy. Four Seasons staff pass four interviews and each hotel has a “guest historian”; Edward Jones (15,000+ North American locations) assigns one advisor and one administrator per office. Customer-centric firms are proactive, delivering needed solutions rather than only requested services.
Service qualityMeasure service as well as financial performance (McDonald’s QSCV: quality, service, cleanliness, value). Senior executives with titles like chief experience officer. Set high standards, audit yourself and competitors, use voice-of-the-customer tools (comparison and mystery shopping, surveys, complaint forms, service-audit teams).
Catering to high-value customersTop-tier customers get discounts and special service; low tiers get fees and stripped-down service. Casinos’ “whales” can be as much as 50 percent of revenue. Risk: lesser-treated customers bad-mouth the firm, and too many perks hurt profit.
Managing complaintsOn average 40 percent of customers with a bad experience stop doing business; those who complain first offer a “gift” if handled well. Empowered, on-the-spot remedies lead to higher revenue and profit. Customers judge outcomes, procedures, and interpersonal treatment.

AI in customer service (boxed feature)

Timeline: ELIZA chatbot (1966); IVR phone systems (1980s); web/mobile AI chatbots for 24/7 service (after 2010); GenAI conversational chatbots (from 2022). Estimates say GenAI could lift service productivity 30–50% or more; Bank of America’s Erica (launched 2018) had served over 37 million customers in over 1.5 billion interactions by 2023. Three limits: the tech can’t yet handle full complexity, the “humanity gap” (customers expect a human), and AI can’t fix a flawed customer experience.

Cases

Singapore Airlines: four months of new-recruit training (twice the industry average) and its 40-30-30 rule (40% of resources to training and motivating staff, 30% to reviewing processes, 30% to new ideas). Butterball Talk Line: began in 1981 with six volunteers answering 11,000 questions; now 50-plus experts answer over 100,000 questions each season. Carnival Triumph: an engine-room fire stranded about 3,100 passengers; despite generous compensation, bookings fell 20 percent and the line invested $600 million in its fleet, showing how fast one failure erodes image and loyalty.

Adding value when the product can’t be differentiated

When the physical product is hard to differentiate, success may hinge on valued services. Example: Rolls-Royce monitors engines in flight by live satellite feed; under TotalCare and CorporateCare, airlines pay a fee per engine flight hour and Rolls assumes the risk and cost of downtime and repairs.

DifferentiatorDescription and examples
Ease of orderingHow simple it is to place an order. Voice assistants predict preferences; Baxter gives hospitals ordering terminals; Harry’s three-step razor selection.
Speed and timing of deliverySpeed, accuracy, and care of delivery. Amazon (hours to a week), Uber Eats; Cemex promises concrete faster than pizza with GPS trucks and a 20-minute delivery window.
Installation, training, consultingInstallation makes a product operational; GE trains hospital staff on X-ray equipment; McDonald’s Hamburger University (two weeks); IBM, Oracle, SAP consulting; IKEA, Home Depot assembly for a fee.
Maintenance and repairKeep products working: Goodyear fleet tire tools, online “e-support,” self-diagnosing appliances (LG, Kenmore, Miele), Movado’s online repair steps.
ReturnsAn unavoidable nuisance, but a lenient policy can raise other purchases and referrals.

Managing returns

One estimate: over 15 percent of merchandise bought is returned, over $700 billion worth. Free shipping eases trial but raises return likelihood. Returns can bring a consumer into the store, and hurt merchants when goods are unsalable, lack proof of purchase, or go to the wrong store.

TypeMeaning
Controllable returnsCaused by errors by seller or customer; largely eliminated with better handling, storage, packaging, transportation, logistics.
Uncontrollable returnsCustomers must see, try, or experience the product; can’t be eliminated in the short run.

Strategy: eliminate root causes of controllable returns, build processes for uncontrollable ones, and put a higher share of returns back into the pipeline for resale. Road Runner Sports trains salespeople and uses 3D foot-scanning “Fit Finder,” yielding below-average shoe returns.

Innovation with services

New categories keep emerging: Drybar (“No Cuts. No Color. Just Blowouts”), Reddit, CareLinx. Kayak entered online travel later using Google’s per-click model and an AI-ranked search. Retail health clinics (often in drugstores/Target/Walmart) treat many simple cases with nurse practitioners: no appointment, seven days a week, most visits within 15 minutes, costs of $25 to $100. NetJets pioneered fractional ownership of private planes; Marquis Jets offered prepaid time; they merged in 2010. Cirque du Soleil reinvented the circus (no animals, themed shows, one new in-house production a year; Club Cirque accounts for about a quarter of ticket sales; over $700 million in annual revenue).

Exam angleList the five differentiators and match an example; contrast controllable and uncontrollable returns.

Managing customer expectations

Quality is tested at every service encounter. Customers form expectations from past experience, word of mouth, and advertising, then compare perceived with expected service. If perceived falls below expected, they are disappointed; great firms exceed expectations. American Express: reps are empowered to do whatever it takes; cardmembers who rate their experience positively raise spending 10–15 percent and are four to five times more likely to stay.

The service-quality model: five gaps

GapBetween…Hospital example
Gap 1Consumer expectation vs. management perceptionAdministrators think patients want better food; patients care more about nurse responsiveness.
Gap 2Management perception vs. quality specificationNurses told to give “fast” service without a speed in minutes.
Gap 3Specification vs. deliveryPoorly trained, unable, unwilling, or conflicting standards (listen vs. be fast).
Gap 4Delivery vs. external communicationsBrochure shows a beautiful room; patient finds it cheap and tacky.
Gap 5Perceived vs. expected servicePhysician visits often to show care; patient thinks something is wrong.

Five determinants of service quality (RATER)

In descending order of importance: reliability, responsiveness, assurance, empathy, tangibles. Each has a zone of tolerance: the range deemed satisfactory, from the minimum consumers will accept up to the level they believe can and should be delivered.

DeterminantMeaning
ReliabilityPerform the promised service dependably and accurately (right the first time, on time, error-free records).
ResponsivenessWillingness to help and give prompt service; keep customers informed.
AssuranceEmployee knowledge and courtesy; ability to convey trust and confidence.
EmpathyCaring, individualized attention; convenient hours.
TangiblesAppearance of facilities, equipment, staff, and materials.

Dynamic process model: perceptions depend on prior expectations of what will and what should happen plus the last service delivered. The two expectation types have opposite effects: raising expectations of what the firm will deliver can improve perceived quality, and lowering expectations of what it should deliver can also improve it. For continuous services (utilities, insurance, subscriptions) customers ask, “Am I using this enough, given what I pay?”; a negative answer risks termination.

Exam angleIdentify which of the five gaps a scenario illustrates (match the hospital examples); list RATER in order; define the zone of tolerance.

Managing self-service

Self-service technologies (vending machines, ATMs, gas pumps, hotel self-checkout, online ticketing) replace person-to-person service for convenience. Integrating them means reengineering the front office around what people do best and what machines do best. The hardest obstacle is often convincing customers to use it. Customers need a clear sense of their role, a clear benefit, and confidence they can use it; automated voices can provoke frustration.

OpenTable: world’s largest online reservation system; half of North American restaurants signed up; over 15 million diners seated monthly; over 40 percent of reservations via phone or tablet; uses diner data for customized recommendations.

Managing product–service bundles

Equipment makers must provide product-support services, now a competitive battleground; Caterpillar and Deere earn a significant share of profit from them. The typical evolution:

StageWhy
1. ManufacturerRuns its own parts-and-service department; stays close to equipment, can charge a premium as sole supplier (equipment priced low, parts and service high).
2. Authorized dealersCloser to customers, more locations, quicker service.
3. Independent service firmsLower price or faster service (auto garages, Midas, Jiffy Lube).

Pressures: some new-car warranties cover 100,000 miles, and never-fail equipment makes buyers less willing to pay 2 to 10 percent of purchase price yearly for service.

Spotlight: The Ritz-Carlton

Customer loyalty through exceptional service, defined by the Gold Standards (credo, motto “Ladies and gentlemen serving ladies and gentlemen,” three steps of service, 12 service values, employee promise). Training: certification in the first 21 days, 250 hours in year one, a 10-minute daily lineup. Standardized scripts (“my pleasure”) began to sound robotic, so the language shifted to authentic, unscripted conversation. Employees are empowered to spend up to $2,000 per day per guest, and the CLASS system records preferences ahead of arrival. Brand extensions: Ritz-Carlton Club, Residences, Reserve, and yachts.

Spotlight: Mayo Clinic

Core value: “The needs of the patient come first”; three shields = clinical practice, research, education. Teamwork assembles a patient’s care team on arrival; physicians are salaried (not paid per test), enabling collaboration. Innovation through prototype exam rooms (“Jack and Jill rooms”). Virtual visits rose from 4 percent to 85 percent during COVID and remained at 15 to 20 percent afterward.

Source note: The Ritz-Carlton lifetime spend and RevPAR dollar figures are OCR-garbled (“S250,000,” “S321”) and are not used.

Exam angleName the three conditions for self-service success; describe the manufacturer-to-dealer-to-independent service evolution.

What incentives do

Incentives (sales promotion tools) are mostly short-term tools to stimulate quicker or greater purchase by consumers or the trade. Digital coupons are the fastest-growing area (no printing, easy to update, higher redemption). Promotions give a short-run sales jump but little permanent gain; they trigger stockpiling (purchase acceleration), then a post-promotion dip, and constant discounting can devalue the offering. Consumer franchise-building tools carry a selling message (frequency awards, coupons advertising features, related premiums); price-off packs, unrelated premiums, contests, refunds, and trade allowances typically do not build the brand.

Net price analysis

List $3,000, average discount $300, promotional spending $450 (15% of list), co-op advertising $150: net price = $3,000 − $300 − $450 − $150 = $2,100.

Five-stage process and decisions

Establish objectives, select tools, develop the program, implement and control, evaluate results. Consumer objectives: more frequent or larger purchases, trial by nonusers, attract switchers. Retailer objectives: carry the brand, carry more, feature/display/reduce price, push the product. Program design: size, conditions for participation, duration, distribution vehicle, timing, and total budget (cost = administrative + incentive cost, times expected units; a coupon deal assumes only a fraction redeem).

StrategyIdeaFits when
PushUse sales force and trade promotion money to get intermediaries to carry, promote, and sell.Low brand loyalty, in-store brand choice, impulse items, benefits well understood.
PullUse advertising and promotion so consumers demand the product from intermediaries.High brand loyalty and involvement, perceived brand differences, brand chosen before the store.

Top firms (Apple, Coca-Cola, Nike) use both; push works better with a pull strategy behind it.

Tools

AudienceTools
ConsumerPrice reductions; coupons and promo codes; cash refunds/rebates; price packs (reduced-price pack, banded pack); premiums; frequency programs; prizes (contest, sweepstakes, game); tie-in promotions; seasonal discounts; financing.
TradeAllowances; free goods; price-off; payment discount (“2/10, net 30” means a 2% deduction if paid within 10 days, otherwise full amount due within 30; e.g., on a $1,000 invoice, pay $980 in 10 days).
Sales forceSales contests with prizes for results; trade shows (highest cost per contact of all communication options).

Trade-promotion problems: forward buying (buying more than can be sold during the deal) and diverting (buying in a deal region and shipping to non-deal regions). Manufacturers limit discounted amounts, smooth shipments, and demand proof of performance before paying allowances.

Exam angleCompute a net price from list price and deductions, distinguish push from pull, and decode “2/10, net 30.” Know why a post-promotion dip occurs and which tools are franchise-building.

What it is and why it works

Direct marketing is the use of consumer-direct channels to reach and deliver goods and services to customers without intermediaries. Advantages: often more cost-effective than traditional marketing; direct contact yields information on needs, usage, and improvement areas; and it enables superior service and a stronger brand image. Direct marketers often seek a measurable response, typically an order (direct-order marketing). Growth is partly a response to the high cost of reaching business markets through a sales force.

Channels listed: direct mail, catalog marketing, telemarketing, kiosks, websites, and mobile devices. The chapter details the four below.

Direct mail

Sending an offer, announcement, or reminder to an individual consumer ("salespeople with wings"). Strengths: target selectivity, personalization, flexibility, early testing, and response measurement. Cost per thousand is higher than mass media but prospects are better. Weakness: crowded mailboxes.

RFM scoring selects customers by Recency (time since last purchase), Frequency (number of purchases), and Monetary amount spent. Chapter example for a leather jacket offer: customers whose last purchase was 30 to 60 days ago, who buy three to six times a year, and who have spent at least $100 since becoming customers; points are assigned by RFM level.

Best lists are past customers. Bought broker lists often have duplicates, incomplete data, and obsolete addresses; buy and test a sample first. Response rates understate impact: if 2% order a Samsonite luggage mailing, many more became aware, formed intentions, or spread word of mouth, so some firms also measure awareness, intention to buy, and word of mouth. Privacy concerns are growing (FTD lets customers choose which reminder emails to receive).

Catalog marketing, telemarketing, infomercials

ChannelKey facts
Catalog marketingFull-line, specialty consumer, and business catalogs, in print and online. W.W. Grainger prints a catalog of nearly 3,000 pages and also posts a searchable online version. Success depends on managing lists (avoid duplication and bad debts), controlling inventory, low returns through quality merchandise, and a distinctive image. The internet and catalog retailing industry: about 40,000 companies and $520 billion revenue.
TelemarketingTelephone and call centers to attract prospects, sell, and serve. Inbound = receiving calls; outbound = initiating calls. Has lost much effectiveness (still heavy in politics); B2B is increasing partly via videoconferencing. Robo-calls (autodialed prerecorded messages) are legal in the US only for relevant information (appointment reminders, flight changes, fraud alerts) or election campaigns, not for selling.
Infomercials30- and 60-minute TV spots combining selling power with information and entertainment; suit complicated, technologically advanced products needing explanation. Hits: Proactiv, P90X, and the George Foreman grill (each hundreds of millions in revenue). At-home shopping channels also sell goods this way.
Exam angleRFM is a favorite: define each letter and apply it to a customer profile. Also know inbound vs. outbound telemarketing, why robo-calls to sell are illegal, and why response rate understates direct mail impact.

Consumer packaged goods (CPG) companies spend heavily on promotions (the slides show the spending split as a chart, not reproduced here), and promotions can have a large short-term impact on sales. The catch: the ideal response pattern is difficult to achieve.

What the evidence says (slides)

  • Permanent effects are much more likely in emerging markets than mature ones.
  • Promotion effects last about 2 weeks on average, vs. about 39 weeks for advertising.
  • The immediate sales increase comes from three sources: category incidence 22% (people buying the category who otherwise wouldn’t), brand choice 60% (switching from other brands), and purchase quantity 18% (stocking up).
  • Long-term effects, if any, come mainly from increased category incidence.

The trade side: forward buying and pass-through

Trade promotions are discounts to retailers, meant to be “passed through” to shoppers as lower prices. Retailers often forward buy instead: they stock up at the deal price and sell later at the regular price, so the discount becomes retailer profit and little reaches the consumer (low pass-through).

Problems with sales promotions (slides)
Limited long-term buying impact
Cheapens the brand image
Reinforces brand switching and deal-sensitivity
Often little “pass-through” of trade promotions to the consumer
Gives discounts to people who would buy anyway

What to do instead: the key to managing trade promotions is a strong brand (retailers need it, so they pass deals through), and price promotions work best to generate trial of superior products: once people try a better product, they stay.

Exam angleKnow 22/60/18 (category incidence / brand choice / quantity), 2 weeks vs. 39 weeks, that permanent effects are likelier in emerging markets, what forward buying and pass-through mean, and the list of problems.

Ask consumers for their ideal product and they will describe bundles that are not technically or economically feasible. Conjoint analysis measures (1) the value consumers place on each attribute, including price, and (2) how they trade one attribute off against another. The name comes from considering features jointly.

Why consider attributes jointly? The golf ball

LevelDriving distanceBall lifePrice
High275 yards54 holes$1.75
Middle250 yards36 holes$1.50
Low225 yards18 holes$1.25

Survey people and the “ideal” ball is 275 yards, 54 holes, $1.25: obvious and useless, because it can’t be built at that price. It does not tell you which features a ball must have and which can go. Conjoint instead shows people whole products, has them rank-order them (1 = best, 9 = worst), and statistically infers their preferences from the trade-offs. The slides’ finding for one buyer: Buyer 1 trades away ball life in favor of distance. You can only see that trade-off when the attributes are considered together.

Checked: every level appears exactly 3 times, and for every pair of attributes each of the 9 level combinations appears exactly once. That balance is what “orthogonal” means: each attribute’s effect can be estimated separately from the others.

Part-worth utilities and attribute importance

From the respondent’s scores, conjoint estimates a part-worth utility for every level. Part-worths can be summed to value any whole product, even one not shown. The slides’ chart reads: a low interest rate is very important; a low fee is important too, but less so; the credit limit doesn’t matter much.

Attribute importance is an attribute’s overall influence on choice: the range of its part-worths (best level minus worst) divided by the sum of all attributes’ ranges.

How the percentages come about (illustrative)

Suppose the part-worth ranges were interest 5.9, fee 2.4, brand 1.0, limit 0.7 (sum 10.0). Importance = range ÷ 10.0 = 59%, 24%, 10%, 7%. These ranges are chosen to reproduce the slide’s shares; the slide’s actual part-worth values are in a chart we don’t have.
