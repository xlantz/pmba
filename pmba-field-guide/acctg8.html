Step 1 · Start here

Equity is just “what's left for the owners”

Equity = Assets − Liabilities. It is the slice of the company that belongs to the owners after everyone who lent money has a claim on it.

The house analogy

You buy a house for $800,000. You put $100,000 down and the bank lends you $700,000. The house is the asset, the loan is the liability, and your slice — $100,000 — is your equity. Ten years later the house is worth $1,100,000 and you still owe $595,000. Your equity is now $505,000. You didn't write the bank another check for most of that; the house rose in value and you paid the loan down. A company works the same way, except the “down payment” comes from shareholders and the growth comes from profits kept in the business.

Five words to know before anything else

Shareholder
An owner. Owns shares of stock.
Par value
A tiny legal label printed on each share ($1, $0.01…). It is not what the share is worth.
APIC
“Additional paid-in capital.” Everything investors paid above par.
Retained earnings
All the profit the company has kept instead of paying out.
Dividend
A payout of profit to shareholders.

Tiny example: one company, one year

Start-up Brightside opens with $100,000 from its founder (shares sold) and borrows $50,000. It earns $30,000 of profit and pays out $10,000 to the founder.

Equity start = $100,000 (shares sold)
+ profit kept = $30,000
− dividend paid = $10,000
Equity end = $120,000

That's the whole unit in one box: money in from owners (stock issued), profit kept (retained earnings), money out to owners (dividends and buybacks). Every section below is one of those three things in more detail.

Companies issue preferred stock to raise equity money without handing out as many votes. The big question behind all of this is capital structure: how much of the company's financing should come from debt and how much from equity?

The four share counts

People mix these up constantly, and the exam loves it. Think of them as nested boxes.

  • Authorized — the maximum shares the company may ever issue.
  • Issued — shares that have been sold to investors at some point.
  • Treasury — issued shares the company has bought back and is holding.
  • Outstanding — issued minus treasury: what investors actually hold today. This is the number used for EPS and for dividends.
Exam angle“Shares outstanding” is issued minus treasury (never plus). Authorized-but-unissued shares are not treasury stock. Know the vote/priority differences between common, preferred and creditors.

Contributed capital: the money owners put in

When a company sells its own shares, it receives cash (or other assets). The balance sheet splits that cash into two equity accounts: Common Stock gets only the par value; Additional Paid-in Capital (APIC) gets everything above par. Selling your own stock never creates revenue or a gain — the owners are just contributing capital.

Worked example: 10,000 shares, $1 par, sold at $50

Cash received = 10,000 × $50 = $500,000
Common Stock = 10,000 × $1 = $10,000
APIC = $500,000 − $10,000 = $490,000
Journal entryDebitCredit
Cash500,000
   Common Stock ($1 par)10,000
   Additional Paid-in Capital490,000

Assets up $500,000, equity up $500,000. No revenue.

Why does par value even exist? Historically, states required it to protect creditors by forcing owners to leave a minimum amount of capital in the company. Today it is mostly a leftover legal label. That's why modern companies pick absurdly small par values ($0.01, $0.001) and why many simply combine Common Stock and APIC on the balance sheet — the par piece is too small to matter.

Preferred stock works identically with its own accounts: Preferred Stock (at par) and APIC—Preferred. Selling 18,400 preferred shares of $25 par at $31.75 credits Preferred Stock $460,000 and APIC—Preferred $124,200.

Exam angleGiven shares, par and issue price, compute the three numbers: cash (shares × price), Common Stock (shares × par), APIC (the difference). Expect “what is credited to APIC?” and “effect on assets and equity” (both up by the full cash).

Earned capital: profit that stays

Contributed capital is money owners put in. Retained earnings is money the business earned and kept. Every year, net income flows into retained earnings; dividends flow out.

Beginning RE + Net income − Dividends = Ending RE
$2,400,000 + $600,000 − $150,000 = $2,850,000

Net income raises equity even if the company never sells another share. A dividend lowers it. Stock issuances and treasury stock do not touch retained earnings.

How shareholders earn a return

Two channels. Distributions from the company (cash dividends, stock dividends) and change in the value of what they own (price appreciation, plus buybacks that shrink the share count). Total shareholder return = dividends + change in share price.

Cash dividends: three dates, two entries

A dividend is a distribution of profit, not an expense: it never appears on the income statement.

Stock dividends: more shares, same total equity

A stock dividend hands out extra shares instead of cash. Nothing leaves the company; value just moves between equity accounts. For a small stock dividend (under ~20–25%), the company records it at market value.

Example: 10% stock dividend

100,000 shares outstanding, market price $20, par $1.

New shares = 100,000 × 10% = 10,000
Value moved = 10,000 × $20 = $200,000
Common Stock = 10,000 × $1 = $10,000
APIC = $200,000 − $10,000 = $190,000
EntryDebitCredit
Retained Earnings200,000
   Common Stock10,000
   APIC190,000

Retained earnings ↓ $200,000, contributed capital ↑ $200,000, total equity unchanged, cash unchanged. Everyone owns 10% more shares and the same percentage of the company.

Stock splits: a re-slicing, no entry

A split divides the pie into more, smaller slices. A 4-for-1 split quadruples the shares, divides par and the price by 4, and changes no dollar amount in equity. It needs no journal entry at all.

Splits: shares × ratio, par ÷ ratio, price ÷ ratio. If a post-split price is given, multiply back to get the pre-split price.

Why would a company buy its own shares?

  • Return excess cash when it can't reinvest it at attractive returns — with more flexibility than a dividend you can never cut.
  • Offset dilution from shares issued to employees.
  • Change per-share numbers (fewer shares → higher EPS).
  • Signal that management thinks the stock is undervalued.
  • Keep shares on hand to reissue later for pay or other purposes.

The buyback: record it at what you paid

Treasury Stock is a contra-equity account. It carries a debit balance and shows up as a negative inside stockholders' equity. It is not an asset. We use the cost method: record the buyback at the price paid, ignoring original issue price and par.

Repurchase 10,000 shares at $30

EntryDebitCredit
Treasury Stock300,000
   Cash300,000

Assets ↓ $300,000. Total equity ↓ $300,000. Shares outstanding ↓ 10,000. No gain or loss.

Reissue: treasury comes out at cost, the rest goes to APIC

Reissue those 10,000 shares at $35

Cash in = 10,000 × $35 = $350,000
Cost basis = 10,000 × $30 = $300,000
Excess = $50,000 → APIC—Treasury Stock
EntryDebitCredit
Cash350,000
   Treasury Stock300,000
   APIC—Treasury Stock50,000

The core rule: transactions with your own owners stay in equity; they never run through net income. So the $50,000 is not a gain. Reissuing below cost does the reverse: debit APIC—Treasury Stock first, then Retained Earnings. The alternative to reissuing is retiring the shares, which removes them permanently from issued shares.

Exam angleRepurchase = Dr Treasury Stock at price paid. Reissue = cash received; Treasury Stock out at cost; the difference to APIC—TS (not a gain). Treasury purchases don't change the Common Stock balance. Shares outstanding after reissue = issued − remaining treasury.

Why pay in stock?

It creates an incentive to grow the company and lets the firm keep cash. The trade-off: less cash pay today, but possible dilution for existing shareholders.

The accounting in one idea

Stock pay is compensation expense, just paid with equity instead of cash. So each year: Dr Compensation Expense / Cr APIC—Stock Compensation. Net income and retained earnings fall by the expense, APIC rises by the same amount, so total equity doesn't change. The total expense is fixed at the grant-date fair value, even if the share price later swings.

Worked example: stock options

10,000 options granted, fair value $12 each, exercise price $20, three-year vesting.

  1. Total expense: 10,000 × $12 = $120,000. Per year: $120,000 ÷ 3 = $40,000. (No entry on the grant date itself.)
  2. Each of three years: Dr Compensation Expense 40,000 / Cr APIC—Stock Compensation 40,000.
  3. Exercise (all 10,000, stock at $35): employees pay the exercise price, not the market price — 10,000 × $20 = $200,000 cash. Credit contributed capital for cash + the $120,000 already built up = $320,000 to APIC (ignoring par). No new expense.

Immediately vested awards are expensed all at once. Award 4,000 shares at $42 and net income falls $168,000 immediately. The credit splits into Common Stock (par) and APIC. For an ESPP, only the discount is compensation: 30,000 shares at a 15% discount off $40 = 30,000 × $6 = $180,000 of expense.

Exam angleOptions: total fair value ÷ years = annual expense. Immediate award: shares × price, all now. ESPP: shares × price × discount %. Stock comp expense lowers net income, but APIC rises equally, so total equity is unchanged. Employees pay the exercise price at exercise.

Profit per share, for common holders

Basic EPS = (Net income − Preferred dividends) ÷ Weighted-average common shares outstanding

Preferred holders get paid first, so only what's left belongs to common shareholders. The denominator is a weighted average because the share count changes during the year (issuances add, buybacks subtract). Don't deduct common dividends and don't use year-end shares.

Quick one: $48,000,000 net income, $3,000,000 preferred dividends, 15,000,000 average shares → ($48M − $3M) ÷ 15M = $3.00. Preferred dividend on a stated rate: shares × par × rate (e.g., 425,000 × $50 × 6% = $1,275,000).

Weighted-average shares, step by step

Reading EPS sensibly

  • EPS standardizes profit per share and feeds valuation (P/E = price ÷ EPS).
  • It can rise just because buybacks shrank the share count, not because profit grew.
  • One-time gains or losses can move it without reflecting recurring operations.
  • $10 EPS is not “better” than $2 EPS across companies — share counts differ.
  • Past periods' EPS is restated for stock splits so years stay comparable.
Exam angleAlways subtract preferred dividends, divide by weighted-average shares, and compute the preferred dividend from shares × par × rate if it isn't given. Know that basic EPS is an earnings per share measure, not cash, and why a competitor with fewer shares and equal profit shows higher EPS.

Back-solving

The formula can be run in any direction: Income to common = EPS × shares; Preferred dividends = Net income − income to common; Shares = income to common ÷ EPS. Because published EPS is rounded to cents, back-solved figures can be off by a small amount (about 1 on the dollar figures), so accept a tolerance.

Traps the exam sets

  • Crediting the whole issue price to Common Stock instead of just par.
  • Treating a buyback or a reissue premium as a gain or loss. It never is.
  • Forgetting that outstanding = issued minus treasury.
  • Thinking a stock dividend or a split changes total equity or cash.
  • Deducting common dividends in EPS, or using year-end shares instead of the weighted average.
  • Using market price instead of the exercise price for cash paid on option exercise.
  • Expensing the whole option award in year one instead of spreading it over vesting.

Before the exam

Likely exam angles

  • Issue shares: compute cash, Common Stock (par), APIC, and the effect on assets and equity.
  • Roll retained earnings forward, or back into dividends.
  • Cash dividend: declaration vs. payment entries. Stock dividend: RE out at market value, par vs. APIC split.
  • Stock split: new share count, new par, pre- or post-split price.
  • Treasury stock: repurchase entry, reissue entry with APIC—TS, shares outstanding afterward, and why it's contra-equity with no gain or loss.
