Note on Low-Tech Marketing Math

The back-of-the-envelope toolkit for marketing decisions: unit and percent margins (always as a share of price), channel margins, break-even volume and share, and how a price change leverages total margin. The Unit 2 slides add the experience curve and marginal analysis (MR = MC).

The reading and slides, block by block

What this reading is

“Note on Low-Tech Marketing Math” (Harvard Business School 9-599-011, Prof. Robert Dolan, Dec. 1998, 9 pages) is a vocabulary-and-mechanics note: the “back of the envelope” calculations used in case analysis and real marketing decisions. It supplements the strategic analysis in the Note on Marketing Strategy; its closing message is that numbers only matter when compared with a benchmark (market size, growth, competitive activity) and used to support a marketing decision.

Prof. Carson’s Unit 2 slides repeat the margin and break-even material (with a bookstore example) and add two things the note does not contain: the experience (learning) curve and marginal analysis (MR = MC). Blocks 6 and 7 are slides-only; the rest follows the note. The same slide deck also covers positioning and perceptual maps (block 8).

Fixed vs. variable costs

Fixed costs stay at the same level regardless of how much is produced and sold (the note’s example: the fee paid for ad time or space, the same whether Budweiser or Nike sells a lot or a little). Variable costs change with volume (packaging, shipping, raw materials). Profit is revenue minus both.

Cases usually give a variable cost per unit. Examples in the note: Sealed Air’s SD-120 (unit = 1,000 sq ft) costs $36.31 = $28.38 manufacturing + $7.93 transportation (adds up); Warner-Lambert’s Niconil costs 12 Irish pounds per 14-day supply.

FigureShape of total costMeaning
1a: constant variable cost per unitStraight line above the fixed-cost levelTotal cost rises at a constant rate. The default assumption in most cases.
1b: decreasing variable cost per unitRises at a decreasing rateUnit cost falls with volume (efficiency gains, better supplier deals). This is the idea behind the experience curve in block 6.

Unit margin and percent margin

Unit margin (also “margin” or unit contribution, CM on the slides) = price per unit − variable cost per unit. Sealed Air: $65.35 − $36.31 = $29.04. Percent margin = unit margin / revenue per unit: $29.04 / $65.35 = .444, so 44.4%. The slides describe CM as each unit’s “contribution toward covering fixed costs.”

Warning from both sources: the denominator is price, never cost. Dividing by cost gives a markup, a different number.

Going from cost and % margin to price

Price = Cost / (1 − margin%/100). Derivation (Appendix 1): start with M/100 = (P − C)/P, multiply by P, collect the P terms, and divide, giving P = C / (1 − M/100).

Exam angleCompute unit margin and % margin from price and variable cost; compute price from cost and % margin; recognize the markup-on-cost trap (the slide example: $6 x 1.40 = $8.40 is wrong, $10 is right).

The “Beginning to Golf” channel

The note follows a golf instruction tape from manufacturer to wholesaler to retailer to consumer. Each member performs a function and is paid for it through its margin: selling price minus what it paid.

How to read it

The dollar margin tells you how much each organization earns per unit. The percentage margin tells you how much of its own sales dollar it keeps, which is why the manufacturer’s 46.67% looks so large next to the retailer’s 13%, even though the retailer sets the consumer price. Note that the manufacturer’s margin ($3.50) is also the figure used for break-even in block 4, because that is the margin on its sales.

Working backward is common: if a retailer keeps a stated % margin on the shelf price, the retailer’s purchase price is shelf price × (1 − margin%). Each level repeats the step. (Practice problem 2 in block 9 does this.)

Exam anglePercent margins at different channel levels use different bases. A question may give consumer price and margin percentages and ask for the manufacturer’s selling price, or ask which member earns the largest dollar margin (the manufacturer, $3.50).

Recovering fixed costs: break-even volume

BEV = Fixed costs / Dollar margin per unit. The slides call the same quantity BEP (break-even point) and describe it as the number of units that must be sold to cover fixed costs. Note example: $700,000 of tape-production costs (fixed) with a $3.50 margin gives 700,000 / 3.50 = 200,000 units.

Choosing between the fee and the royalty is a volume question: the royalty costs $1 times volume, so it is cheaper than the flat $175,000 below 175,000 tapes and more expensive above that. (This comparison is an extension, not in the note.) The note itself only asks what each option does to the break-even.

From units to market share

Is 200,000 units “big”? Convert to a share: with 5,000,000 golf-video units sold a year, break-even share = 200,000 / 5,000,000 = 4%. If only 1,000,000 of those are for beginners, the relevant share is 200,000 / 1,000,000 = 20%. Two judgment calls hide in this: how you define the market, and the time horizon (both shares assume fixed costs are recovered within one year).

Exam angleKnow that BEV only tells you the volume that covers fixed costs; whether it is attainable depends on market size, share, and the strategic factors in the Note on Marketing Strategy. Be ready to compute BEV after an added fixed cost versus after a lower unit margin, and to say which market definition makes the required share look bigger.

The drivers of profit

Figure 3 of the note is a profit tree. Profit = Sales revenue − Costs. Revenue = volume × price (left branch). Costs = variable cost (variable unit cost × volume) + fixed costs (right branch). Blocks 3–4 mostly worked the right-hand branch; this block works the price branch.

Total margin is a rectangle

Total contribution (total margin) = unit margin × sales volume, so on a graph with unit margin on the horizontal axis and volume on the vertical axis it is a rectangle whose area is the total margin. Base case: price $100, variable cost $60, volume 1 million units, unit margin $40, total margin $40 million.

The rows for −20% and +20% are the note’s own examples. A 20% price cut cuts unit margin by 50%, so volume must double to stand still. Revenue then rises to $160M while total margin stays $40M. The 20% increase lifts unit margin to $60, so volume can fall by 33.3% (666,667 units; the note prints 666,666) before total margin drops below $40M. The other rows are my extension using the same method.

Shortcut: required volume change = −ΔP / (current margin + ΔP), where ΔP is the signed dollar price change. Check: ΔP = −$20, margin $40 gives +20/20 = +100%.

Why the effect is “highly leveraged”

A small percentage price change is a much larger percentage change in unit margin, and that is what volume must make up. The effect is strongest when variable costs are high and margins thin. Contrast: with variable cost of only $20 (margin $80), the same 20% cut ($100 to $80) leaves a $60 margin, so volume need only rise 33.3%, not 100%.

Scope note: this is a necessary-volume test, not a forecast. It says nothing about how customers will actually respond, which depends on price sensitivity, competition and the rest of the marketing strategy.

Exam angleGiven price, variable cost, and volume, compute the volume required to hold total margin after a price cut or rise. Explain in words why a 20% cut requires a 100% volume increase when the margin is 40% of price. Expect the sign trap: a price rise lets volume fall by less than the percent that margin rises.

This topic is in Prof. Carson’s Unit 2 slides, not in the HBS note. The note only hints at it with Figure 1b (unit cost falling with volume).

The idea

Costs decline by a fixed percentage every time cumulative production doubles (1,000 … 2,000 … 4,000 … 8,000). For the nth unit: cn = c1 n−a, equivalently ln cn = ln c1 − a ln n. Here a is the learning rate; bigger a means faster cost decline. Unlike Figure 1a, unit variable cost is not constant.

Backing out a from two data points

Slide data: first-unit cost $110, and a unit cost of $60 at 1,000,000 cumulative units. Then ln 60 = ln 110 − a ln 1,000,000, so a = ln(110/60) / ln(1,000,000) = 0.6061 / 13.8155 = 0.04387. The slide writes a = .0438 (it truncates rather than rounds; .0439 is the rounded value). Using .0438, the curve gives $60.06 at 1,000,000 units, which is exactly what the slide table shows; using the unrounded a gives exactly $60.00.

How fast does it fall?

Each doubling multiplies cost by 2−a = 2−.0438 = 0.9700, i.e. cost falls about 3.0% per doubling (a “97% curve”). The 1st-to-2nd unit step is $110.00 to $106.71, the same 3.0%; the table below falls 110 → 60 over roughly 19.9 doublings.

Break-even using the experience curve

When unit cost keeps falling, break-even is reached when the sum of contribution margins equals fixed cost (not simply FC / a fixed CM). Slide example, a game console at $199: CMn = $121 − $26 − 110 n−.0438, where $121 is the net selling price (what the company receives after channel margins), $26 is non-direct cost that experience does not reduce, and 110 n−.0438 is the direct cost that does. The slide then shows 32.009 (million).

My check: CMn is negative for the first 28 units (direct cost above $95) and about $34.94 at the millionth unit. Summing CMn over the first 1,000,000 units gives about $32.19M (about $32.25M with the unrounded a). Cumulative CM first reaches $32.009M at about unit 994,900. So the slide’s 32.009 million is consistent with “fixed cost recovered by roughly the millionth unit,” but the slide text does not show the fixed-cost input and my sum does not reproduce 32.009 exactly (0.6% off), so treat that figure as the professor’s stated result and the interpretation as mine.

Exam angleKnow the formula and what a means; explain that costs fall by a constant percentage per doubling; and that break-even under an experience curve cumulates falling per-unit margins. Do not expect heavy arithmetic with logs, but you may be asked to compute a cost at some n given a, or recognize that non-direct costs are not on the curve.

Also from the Unit 2 slides, not the HBS note.

Sales response and diminishing returns

The slides draw an S-shaped sales response function: sales ($) against advertising (GRPs or dollars). At low spending returns increase (each extra dollar adds more sales than the last); past an inflection point there are diminishing returns (each extra dollar adds less).

The rule: add units until MR = MC

Keep adding advertising (or any marketing input) until marginal revenue equals marginal cost. The slide states it as marginal return = (MR − MC)/MC = 0: “one more dollar of advertising generates one more dollar of sales.” Beyond that point an extra dollar returns slightly less than a dollar of sales and profit declines. Graphically, the slope of the sales curve (MR) equals the slope of the cost line (MC).

Two crossings, one optimum

Because the curve is S-shaped, MR = MC occurs twice. The first crossing sits on the increasing-returns part of the curve, where adding a little more spending would do better than the margin, so it is not optimal (it is actually a local profit minimum). The second crossing, where the curve is flattening, is the optimum.

Worked example (generic, with margin included)

Suppose each $1 of sales brings 40% contribution, and sales respond as S = 200√A. Profit = .40 × 200√A − A = 80√A − A. Marginal contribution per ad dollar = 40/√A. Set 40/√A = 1: √A = 40, A = $1,600, profit = 80(40) − 1,600 = $1,600.

Caution on definitions: the slide measures MR in sales dollars per advertising dollar. Strictly, profit-maximizing marginal analysis uses marginal contribution (sales × margin), as in my example; the two agree only when margin is 100%. For the course, learn the slide wording and be able to explain the margin refinement.

Spending across several marketing-mix elements

The slides add two complications: you spend on multiple elements (advertising, sales force, …) and you usually run out of budget before reaching MR = MC in each one. So the rule becomes: put the next dollar where it earns the highest return; as that element’s return falls, switch; at the end of the budget, marginal returns are equal across elements (you are indifferent about the next dollar).

Generic numbers: contribution from advertising = 60√A, from sales force = 30√B, budget $1,000. Equal marginal returns: 30/√A = 15/√B, so A = 4B: A = $800, B = $200 (marginal return about $1.06 per $1 in both). Total contribution = 60√800 + 30√200 = $2,121, versus $2,012 for an even 500/500 split.

Exam angleState the MR = MC rule and what it means in dollars; explain why a second crossing on the increasing-returns part is not optimal; explain the multi-element version (equalize marginal returns under a budget constraint).

Using the numbers (the note’s closing)

Calculations translate facts and estimates into other facts and estimates. They matter in an argument only against a benchmark: market size, growth rate, competitive activity. A break-even of 200,000 units is neither good nor bad until you know the market. Intuition about which quantity to compute comes from practice, and the goal is always to support a marketing policy decision.

Positioning and perceptual maps (from the same slide deck)

Positioning is designing the offering and image to occupy a distinctive place relative to competitors in the minds of the target market (what the brand stands for). Slide examples: Toyota = quality, Volvo = safe. A perceptual map plots brands on the dimensions customers care about, along with segments’ ideal points.

Building the map and MARKSTRAT

Attribute-based methods: customers rate products on attributes (often semantic-differential scales such as slow 1–7 fast); factor analysis groups attributes into dimensions (e.g., “safe, won’t hurt stomach” = gentleness); brands are plotted on factor scores. Similarity-based methods: input similarity ratings; multidimensional scaling (e.g., PERMAP) produces the map; the researcher chooses the number of dimensions and interprets them; less up-front work but harder to interpret.

MARKSTRAT first-week decisions (slides): production (market forecast, purchase intentions), pricing (think long-term), advertising (budget per product and audience; look at awareness and sales by segment), sales force (budget per product and channel; look at distribution coverage), and marketing research (semantic scales give a perceptual map). The slides stress: do not use the “Nutrite” market at all.

Exam angleKnow that the margin and break-even tools above are inputs to marketing decisions, never ends in themselves. For perceptual maps, be ready to explain why a brand is well or poorly positioned (closeness to an ideal, differentiation, owning a dimension) and what “stuck in the middle” means.

Six problems with full solutions. All numbers are mine (computed in Python); none come from the note’s own examples.

Problem 1: price from cost and % margin

A retailer pays $24 per unit and wants a 35% margin. What price, and what dollar margin?

Answer: Price = 24 / (1 − .35) = 24 / .65 = $36.92; margin = $12.92, check $12.92 / $36.92 = 35%. (Wrong approach: 24 × 1.35 = $32.40, which yields only a 25.9% margin.)

Problem 2: working back through the channel

A product retails at $30 with a 40% retailer margin. The wholesaler earns a 20% margin on its own selling price. The manufacturer’s variable cost is $9. Find each member’s dollar margin and the manufacturer’s % margin.

Answer: Retailer pays 30 × .60 = $18.00, margin $12.00. Wholesaler pays 18 × .80 = $14.40, margin $3.60. Manufacturer sells at $14.40, margin 14.40 − 9 = $5.40, which is 5.40 / 14.40 = 37.5%. Check: 9 + 5.40 + 3.60 + 12.00 = $30.

Problem 3: break-even, added costs, market share

Fixed costs $1,200,000; manufacturer sells at $25; variable cost $13; total market is 2,500,000 units a year. (a) BEV and share? (b) A $200,000 ad campaign: how many extra units to pay for itself? (c) A price cut to $22: new BEV?

Answer: (a) CM = $12; BEV = 1,200,000 / 12 = 100,000 units; share = 100,000 / 2,500,000 = 4%. (b) 200,000 / 12 = 16,667 units (16,666.7). (c) CM = $9; BEV = 1,200,000 / 9 = 133,333 units, a 33.3% rise.

Problem 4: price change at constant total margin

Price $50, variable cost $30, volume 400,000 (total margin $8,000,000). What volume holds total margin at a price of $45? At $55?

Answer: At $45 the margin is $15, so volume = 8,000,000 / 15 = 533,333 (+33.3%). At $55 the margin is $25, so volume = 8,000,000 / 25 = 320,000 (−20%).

Problem 5: experience curve

(a) First-unit cost is $200 and the 10,000th unit costs $120. Find a. (b) If costs fall 10% with each doubling (a 90% curve) and c1 = $80, what does unit 8 cost?

Answer: (a) a = ln(200/120) / ln(10,000) = 0.5108 / 9.2103 = 0.0555. (b) Three doublings (1 to 2 to 4 to 8), so 80 × .93 = $58.32. (Equivalent: a = −ln(.9)/ln 2 = 0.1520; 80 × 8−0.1520 = $58.32.)

Problem 6: marginal analysis

Contribution from advertising is 50√A (dollars), advertising costs $1 per $1. Find the profit-maximizing A and the profit.

Answer: Profit = 50√A − A; marginal contribution = 25/√A. Set 25/√A = 1, so √A = 25 and A = $625; profit = 50(25) − 625 = $625.

Exam angleDo these cold, then check. The usual errors: dividing by cost instead of price, mixing up the margin base across channel levels, forgetting that a lower margin raises BEV, and using the wrong sign direction for a price rise.

Experience (learning) curve
Unit cost falls by a fixed percentage each time cumulative output doubles.
Experience Curve
Experience curve formula
c_n = c_1 n^(-a); ln c_n = ln c_1 - a ln n.
Experience Curve
Learning rate a
a = ln(c_1/c_n)/ln(n); slide: ln(110/60)/ln(1,000,000) = .0438 (.04387 unrounded).
Experience Curve
Per-doubling decline
With a = .0438 each doubling multiplies cost by 0.970 (about 3.0% lower).
Experience Curve
Console break-even (slide)
CM_n = $121 - $26 - 110 n^-.0438; break-even when summed CM covers fixed cost.
Experience Curve
Direct vs. non-direct costs
Direct costs follow the experience curve; non-direct costs ($26 in the slide) do not.

Marginal Analysis
MR = MC rule
Add spending until marginal revenue equals marginal cost; one more dollar returns one more dollar.
Marginal Analysis
Two MR = MC points
First is on the increasing-returns portion (not optimal); second, on the flattening portion, is optimal.
Marginal Analysis
Equal marginal returns across elements
Under a budget limit, allocate so the next dollar returns the same in each marketing-mix element.

Before the quiz

Likely quiz angles

  • Compute unit margin and percent margin; always divide by price, never by cost.
  • Find a price from cost and a percent margin (cost / (1 − margin)); spot the markup-on-cost trap.
  • Work out each channel member’s dollar and percent margin (own selling price as the base).
  • Compute break-even volume, break-even for added fixed cost, break-even after a lower margin, and break-even market share.
  • Find the volume that holds total margin constant after a price cut or rise, and explain the leverage.
  • State the experience-curve formula, what a means, and that costs fall a fixed percent per doubling.
  • State MR = MC, explain why the second crossing of an S-curve is optimal, and how a budget is split across elements.
  • Explain why numbers need a benchmark; read a perceptual map.
