Week 3: Hiring (plus Historical Perspective)

Interviews feel informative but predict poorly, because we judge people by traits instead of situations, start from sticky first impressions, and ask different candidates different questions. Structured interviews fix much of this, and the historical lecture shows how management thinking moved from fear-based “drive” systems through Taylor, Follett and McGregor.

The story

Susan David (HBR, June 25, 2012) opens with Jack, a senior manager you like, who cancels your project. Many people instantly conclude “now I see his true colors”: phony, risk-averse, visionless. The article asks: or is he? The answer is that we explain others by fixed traits and underweight the constraints of the situation. This is the fundamental attribution error, also called correspondence bias, first observed in an experiment by Ned Jones and Victor Harris about 45 years before the article.

The article's other illustration: basketball players made to shoot in a poorly lit gym look less talented than those observed in excellent lighting. We blame the player, not the lighting. Daniel Gilbert is quoted: people are too willing to take each other at face value and too reluctant to search for other explanations. He traces the bias to four root causes.

The four causes (David / Gilbert)

#CauseIn the Jack storyExample (hiring)
1Lack of full awareness of the situationYou don’t know the VP has been scrutinizing the project; Jack may be acting under duress, with regret.Example: you see a candidate’s 8-month job and think “flaky”; the company was shut down.
2Unrealistic expectations of the person“Sure, he’s under pressure, but I never would have caved.” We mispredict what we or the average person would do.Example: “I would never have stumbled on that question,” though most people do.
3Exaggerated assessments of behaviorYou know the facts, but your background knowledge makes you read confidence into every nuance: “he wanted this all along.”Example: a pause becomes “evasive” because you already suspect something.
4Failure to correct initial assumptionsWe make quick judgments and normally revise them, but when overloaded we don’t; Jack stays “one of those guys.”Example: after eight back-to-back interviews, the first impression is never revisited.

Slide version: (1) lack full awareness of the situation; (2) unrealistic expectations of others, compared to what you think you would do; (3) exaggerated assessments, because we are motivated to see others a certain way; (4) sticky first impressions, especially under cognitive load, which worsens #1 because we don’t search for situational constraints. The slide asks: “Is it the person or the situation?”

Where it matters, and the fixes

  • Stereotyping and prejudice: a woman with a sad expression is called “emotional”; a man with the same expression is “having a bad day.”
  • Organizations: the bias can stymie a company’s development; Gino & Pisano (HBR 2011) show it contributes to failures of individual and organizational learning.
  • Do not try to turn judgments off. That is probably impossible, and snap judgments can carry useful information. The most effective thing: consider that you really don’t know Jack, leaving room for continued investigation, discovery and an evolving understanding.
  • Similarity bias (Week 3 slide): if someone looks or sounds like me, or shares an extracurricular, I assume they are like me in other good ways.
What the quiz may probeLabel a short scenario with the correct cause (1-4) or as the fundamental attribution error; know that David says not to suspend judgment but to stay open; name Jones & Harris, Gilbert, and the basketball example.

What STAR is

STAR is a common way to organize an answer to a behavioral interview question (“Tell me about a time when...”):

LetterPartWhat it covers
SSituationThe context: where and when
TTaskWhat you were responsible for achieving
AActionWhat you specifically did
RResultThe outcome, ideally with evidence

Example (invented): “Tell me about a time you handled an unhappy customer.” S: a client's order arrived late the day before their launch. T: I had to keep the account. A: I called the same hour, rerouted a rush shipment, and told them the new timeline. R: the order arrived in time and the client renewed.

Why it links to Week 3

It matches the slides’ behavioral question type from both sides: interviewers use behavioral prompts, and structured scoring can check each answer for the same STAR parts. Note the slide’s warning that behavioral questions favor people with more experience.

What the quiz may probeRecognize STAR as a way to answer behavioral questions; the quiz is unlikely to go deeper than the four letters.

Factory development, 1880-1920

Industrialization, infrastructure changes, immigration, an expanding workforce, and different skill sets. The slide shows working-condition photos.

The “drive” system of management

  • Strong foreman and monitoring; ad hoc planning
  • Assumption of a “slothful” worker
  • Motivation through fear and coercion (“Have you fired anyone yet today?”); also physical abuse
  • Wages based on a day rate

Thinkers compared

ThinkerOn the slideGeneral background (not on slide)
Frederick Taylor (1856-1915)Leading proponent of scientific management; time studies; advocate for “fair” pay and piece-rate pay; saw workers as intellectually dim but capable of hard work. Pros: saves time and money; seen today in differential pay, training systems, motion studies, pay-for-performance goal setting. Con: mechanistic.Published The Principles of Scientific Management (1911); emphasized breaking jobs into standardized tasks.
Mary Parker Follett (1868-1933)Organizations operate as “communities”; managers and workers in harmony; conflicts resolved by integration; foundational ideas of worker empowerment.Her “integration” means finding a solution that satisfies both sides' underlying interests rather than compromise.
Douglas McGregorTheory X and Theory Y; managers' beliefs about others can affect their behavior toward others. X: pessimistic (resistant to change, lack ambition, irresponsible). Y: optimistic (capable of responsibility, self-direction, imagination).Introduced the ideas in The Human Side of Enterprise (1960).

Example (invented): a manager who believes staff will slack off (Theory X) installs monitoring and checklists; staff stop volunteering ideas, which “confirms” the original belief. A Theory Y manager gives a project owner authority and a deadline.

Slide discussion questions (no answers given): Do you need both X and Y? Can you change someone who is one way to be another? Does it depend on the type of worker?

Why hire at all? Order and control

At the most basic level we want employees to show up on time and do the assigned work; the best companies get more: high-quality work with error correction (“don’t pass errors on to others”), engagement and learning for continuous improvement, service to customers who care about the mission, and innovation that cares about the future. These make the firm more effective, profitable and competitive.

Organizations have a division of labor, even at team level. Small organizations coordinate by conversation; larger ones need sequences and deadlines (“you do X, give your output to Y”). That is order. The slide says the need for management is the need for order and control, and that is management’s value to the firm. Control can be voluntary compliance but is still control.

Three forms of controlMeaning
1. Specification of the division of labor, jobsDefining who does what
2. Employee selectionHiring is itself a control tool: choose people who will do the work
3. Employee motivationGetting chosen people to want to do it

The interview exercise

Pairs of people who didn’t know each other interviewed each other for exactly 7 minutes to predict the partner’s Midterm Quiz score. Incentives: interviewers do best when accurate; interviewees do best when the interviewer thinks they will perform well.

Quote on the slide, attributed to Wharton’s Ethan Mollick: interviews make you think you have learned something about a person even when you haven’t.

Class discussion: what went wrong?

  • Non-diagnostic questions (too broad about the person; the test differed from expectations; the person’s week changed unexpectedly).
  • Different questions to different candidates: everyone found a way to make their partner shine.
  • Interviewer bias: very optimistic predictions, and a similarity bias.

Best and worst hiring experiences, and the AI debate

Discussion prompts: whose company hires well or poorly, what is the process, and how do you know. Debate: Pro extensive use of AI and algorithms for hiring; Con against; and a “complex middle.” The slide gives no arguments, so none are credited here. General background (not on the slide): common pro arguments are speed and consistency; common con arguments are inherited bias in training data and lack of transparency.
