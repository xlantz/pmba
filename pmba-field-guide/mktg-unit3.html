The definition, in plain words

Marketing research is the function that links the consumer, customer, and public to the marketer through information. That information is used to identify and define marketing opportunities and problems; generate, refine, and evaluate marketing actions; monitor marketing performance; and improve understanding of marketing as a process. The researcher specifies the information needed, designs the collection method, manages the data collection, analyzes the results, and communicates the findings and their implications.

Data is not insight

Marketing insights are diagnostic information about how and why we see certain effects in the marketplace and what that means for marketers. Raw numbers or an expert’s opinion are not enough.

CaseWhat happenedLesson
WalmartA large study of U.S. shoppers found its edge was the functional benefit “offers low prices” plus the emotional benefit “makes me feel like a smart shopper.” It replaced “Always Low Prices. Always.” with “Save Money, Live Better.”Insight became a campaign.
TropicanaRedesigned its orange-juice package (dropped the orange-with-straw image) without adequately testing consumer reactions. Sales fell 20%; the old design returned after a few months.No insight, big loss.
Star WarsA 1970s research executive predicted the film would fail (post-Watergate realism, “war” in the title). He never studied the script. It grossed over $4.3 billion at the box office.He delivered information and opinion, not insight.

Companies also misuse research when they do not understand what it can do, give the researcher a problem definition that is not specific enough, or hold unrealistic expectations.

AI and marketing research

Three benefits: (1) operational efficiency (writing questionnaires, translating, coding open-ended answers, charts and reports, adaptive surveys, even AI moderators for online focus groups); (2) real-time insights (L’Oréal’s TrendSpotter scans millions of comments, images, and videos; social listening); (3) predictive analytics (Kellogg’s scans trending recipes; Mondelez built a model on its panel of “supertasters”). Two cautions: data quality (GenAI can “hallucinate,” and errors in the input data show up in the output) and credibility (managers may distrust algorithms they do not understand and ignore them).

Good marketing research is characterized by the scientific method, creativity, multiple research methods, accurate model building, cost-benefit analysis, healthy skepticism, and an ethical focus (chapter summary).

Good marketers follow a formal process with five steps (Figure 5.1). The chapter threads one running case through it: American Airlines deciding whether to offer ultra-high-speed Wi-Fi to first-class passengers on long-haul flights.

Step 1: Define the problem (not too broad, not too narrow)

VersionExample from the chapterProblem
Too broad“Find out everything you can about first-class air travelers’ needs.”Collects lots of unnecessary information
Too narrow“Find out whether enough passengers on a B777 between Chicago and Tokyo would pay $25 for ultra-high-speed Wi-Fi so we break even in one year.”Rules out useful questions (why $25? why break even?)
Just right“Will offering ultra-high-speed Wi-Fi create enough incremental preference and profit to justify its cost against other service enhancements American might make?”Focuses on the key issue yet is actionable

The business question then splits into four sub-questions (offer it at all? first class only or also business/economy? what price(s)? which planes and trip lengths?) and five research objectives: (1) which types of first-class passengers respond most; (2) how many would use it at different prices; (3) how many might choose American because of it; (4) how much long-term goodwill it adds to the image; (5) how important it is relative to other services such as a power plug or enhanced entertainment.

Step 2: Develop the plan, and check the cost

The plan is the most efficient way to gather the needed information and an estimate of what it will cost. You must decide on: data sources, research approaches, research instruments, sampling plan, and contact methods (the next three sections). The cost of the research has to be in line with its estimated benefits.

Chapter example: how much is research worth?

American estimated that launching Wi-Fi would yield a long-term profit of $50,000. If managers believe the research will lead to a better pricing and promotional plan and a higher long-term profit, they should be willing to spend up to $40,000 on it. If the study would cost more than $40,000, it is not worth doing.

Qualitative vs. quantitative, at a glance

Chapter facts behind the line: focus groups and open-end questions are exploratory; qualitative techniques are indirect and unstructured, with small samples and results that may not generalize (different researchers can draw different conclusions); survey and behavioral data are best at counting; experiments are for cause and effect. Marketers do not have to choose: for example, a panel member can complete an in-home product test with a video diary and an online survey.

Three main instruments collect primary data: questionnaires, qualitative measures, and technological (measurement) devices.

Questionnaires

A questionnaire is a set of questions presented to respondents. It is by far the most common primary-data instrument because it is flexible. Form, wording, and sequence all influence responses, so test and de-bug it. Closed-end questions list every possible answer (easy to interpret and tabulate). Open-end questions let people answer in their own words, which suits exploratory research (how people think, rather than how many think a certain way).

Measurement devices and neuromarketing

Skin sensors, brain-wave scanners, full-body scanners, eye-tracking, facial-recognition software (estimates age and gender), and biometric wrist sensors that read electrodermal activity (skin conductance). Neuromarketing is brain research on the effects of marketing stimuli, using EEG and fMRI. Findings: Super Bowl ads with the highest brain activity differed from those with the highest stated preference; brain waves predicted music purchases better than stated preferences. Cautions: expensive, not universally accepted, should not be the sole basis for decisions, and the devices can be obtrusive (electrode caps, artificial exposure conditions).

Exam angleName a question type from its example (Likert vs semantic differential vs intention-to-buy), pick the right qualitative technique for a described task, and arrange a laddering chain: attribute, functional benefit, emotional benefit, core value.

Data collection is generally the most expensive and error-prone phase: some respondents are away, offline, or inaccessible and must be re-contacted or replaced; others refuse or give biased or dishonest answers. A sampling and data-collection plan controls cost while keeping quality high.

The sampling plan: three decisions

After choosing the sampling unit, build a sampling frame so everyone in the target population has an equal or known chance of being sampled. Probability sampling lets researchers calculate confidence limits for sampling error and makes the sample more representative. Intercept interviews risk nonprobability (non-random) samples.

Share of search (SOS)

Using Google Trends data, a brand’s share of search is its portion of category searches compared with competitors. It is easier and cheaper to get than market-share data and is an early predictor of market share. One study found changes in share of search came before changes in market share by about 3 months (energy brands), 6 months (mobile phone handsets), and 9–12 months (autos). Rolling averages add stability. Caveat: search turns into sales only if the full marketing program works (price, availability, product match).

General illustration: reading share of search

Suppose a phone brand’s share of category searches climbs from 20% to 25% this quarter. By the chapter’s timing finding for handsets, you would watch for market share to follow in roughly six months, if price, distribution, and the product itself are all working.

Data mining

Marketing statisticians extract useful information about individuals, trends, and segments from the mass of available data using: cluster analysis (group objects so those in one cluster are more alike than those in others), predictive modeling (forecasting outcomes of uncertain events), and cognitive modeling (simulating human decision making and problem solving with a computer model).

Tabulate the data and develop summary measures: averages and measures of dispersion for the main variables, then advanced statistical techniques and decision models. Test different hypotheses and theories, and use sensitivity analysis to test the assumptions and the strength of the conclusions.

Research finds opportunities; the company must then measure and forecast the size, growth, and profit potential of each. The sales forecast is used by finance (raising cash), manufacturing (capacity and output), purchasing (supplies), and human resources (hiring). A forecast that is off the mark leaves the company with excess or inadequate inventory.

Chapter point: compare your growth with the industry’s

If your sales rise 5% a year while industry sales rise 10%, you are losing relative standing in the industry (share is shrinking). Industry totals often come from trade associations (who usually do not list individual firms) or from audit firms such as NielsenIQ. B2B marketers know less about share because distributors will not report competitors’ sales.

Next year: you sell 105, industry sells 1,100 (105 / 1,100 = 9.5% share)

Forecasting: the order of work and the three information bases

Forecasting is the art of anticipating what buyers are likely to do under a given set of conditions. Firms usually prepare a macroeconomic forecast first (inflation, unemployment, interest rates, consumer spending, business investment, government expenditures, net exports), ending in a GDP forecast; then an industry forecast; then the company sales forecast, assuming the company wins a certain market share. Surveys of buying intentions feed consumer confidence (the Conference Board) and consumer sentiment (University of Michigan Survey Research Center) measures. Firms can build forecasts or buy them (marketing research firms; long-range specialists such as IHS Global Insight, Forrester Research, Gartner Group; futurist firms such as the Institute for the Future, Hudson Institute, and the Futures Group produce speculative scenarios).

Why it is hard

Inputs (marketing expenses and investments) are easy to quantify in the short run, but outputs such as brand awareness, brand image, customer loyalty, and new-product prospects may take months or years to show up, and internal or external changes can happen at the same time and blur the effect.

Marketing metrics are the set of measures that help marketers quantify, compare, and interpret marketing performance. Choose metrics based on the issues you face. Marketing dashboards

A marketing dashboard is “a concise set of interconnected performance drivers to be viewed in common throughout the organization” and works like a car or plane instrument panel with real-time indicators. It should include two market-based scorecards: one for performance and one for early-warning signals. Dashboards are only as good as their data. Marketing controllers may review budget items and expenses, using business intelligence software.

Pick metrics on purpose: the wrong metrics can make managers overlook opportunities and threats and steer the company wrong, so the choice belongs in strategic planning, not as an afterthought based on whatever is easy to observe.

Built with academics from several disciplines around Freddy Heineken’s line “I don’t sell beer. After validation with consumers (87% said having a good time is more important than ever), the resulting 15 drivers joined the ongoing brand tracking survey.

What a brand is

A brand is "a name, term, design, symbol or any other feature that identifies one seller's goods or service as distinct from those of other sellers." Its ultimate purpose is to create, for consumers, the company and its collaborators, value beyond what the product and service aspects create. Branding is the process of endowing products and services with that power: it is all about creating differences between products, using brand names and other elements to tell consumers "who" and "what" the product is and why they should care.

Branding is old. Medieval European guilds required craftspeople to put trademarks on products to protect themselves and customers against inferior quality; in fine art, branding began with artists signing their works. Firms start brand creation through marketing, but a brand ultimately resides in the minds and hearts of consumers: a perceptual entity rooted in reality but reflecting consumers' views. Differences can come from the product itself (Gillette, Merck and 3M through innovation) or from non-product imagery (Gucci, Chanel and Louis Vuitton).

Anything where a consumer has a choice can be branded: a good (Tesla), a service (Singapore Airlines), a store (Nordstrom), a place (the city of Sydney), an organization (U2, AAA) or an idea (racial justice, free trade).

Case: New Coke (1985)

Battered by Pepsi taste-test challenges, Coca-Cola spent $4 million on research; blind tests showed drinkers preferred a sweeter formula. The launch caused a national uproar (letters, protests, lawsuit threats). Ten weeks later the old formula returned as "Classic Coke". Researchers had measured taste but not the emotional attachment. New Coke disappeared around 1992, and the old formula ended up with stronger attitudes and sales. (Revived briefly 34 years later with Netflix's Stranger Things.)

Customer-based brand equity (CBBE)

Customer-based brand equity is "the differential effect that brand knowledge has on consumer response to the marketing of that brand." If consumers react more favorably to a product and its marketing when the brand is identified than when it is not, equity is positive; if less favorably, negative. If there is no difference, the product is a commodity and competition will probably be based on price.

The differences come from brand knowledge: brand awareness plus all the thoughts, feelings, images, experiences and beliefs linked to the brand. Strong, favorable and unique associations: Toyota (reliability), Hallmark (caring), Amazon (convenience and wide selection). Awareness can also drive behavior: GEICO spends tens of millions so drivers think of it first, and a top-of-mind brand becomes the reference point or default option others are judged against.

The 11 benefits of strong equity

  1. Improved perception of product performance
  2. Greater loyalty
  3. Less vulnerability to competitive actions and marketing crises
  4. Larger margins
  5. More inelastic response to price increases, more elastic response to price decreases
  6. Greater trade cooperation and support
  7. More effective marketing communications
  8. Expanded licensing opportunities
  9. More brand extension opportunities
  10. Improved employee recruiting and retention
  11. Greater financial market returns

Equity is a bridge from past to future. Looking back, each year's marketing dollars are investments in consumer brand knowledge (quality, not quantity, matters; you can overspend if money is spent unwisely). Looking forward, consumer knowledge decides where the brand may go, and they grant or deny permission: Bengay aspirin, Cracker Jack cereal, Frito-Lay lemonade, Fruit of the Loom laundry detergent and Smucker's premium ketchup all failed as inappropriate extensions.

The brand value chain

A structured way to see how marketing activity creates brand value (Figure 10.1). Value flows left to right through four stages, and three multipliers raise or lower what passes from one stage to the next.

Stage 1: the firm invests in a marketing program (communications, trade or intermediary support, product R&D and design) aimed at actual or potential customers. Stage 2: this changes customers' mindset (thoughts and feelings linked to the brand). Stage 3: mindset affects buying behavior and response to all later marketing (price, channels, communication, product), driving market share and profitability. Stage 4: the investment community uses market performance to assess shareholder value.

Brand valuation (financial equity)

Brand valuation (financial-based brand equity) estimates the total financial value of the brand: the premium on a company's valuation due to owning the brand, or the net present value of the total financial returns the brand will generate over its lifetime. In well-known companies brand value is typically more than half of market capitalization. U.S. companies do not list brand equity on balance sheets (no agreement on estimates), but companies in the United Kingdom, Hong Kong and Australia do. Interest rose with the 1980s mergers and acquisitions wave.

Where brand knowledge comes from: three factors

Equity is built by positioning the brand and creating the right awareness and associations; success depends on all brand-related contacts, marketer-initiated or not. Three main factors:

  1. Brand elements (names, logos, symbols, etc.). Example: Microsoft named its search engine Bing as short, appealing, memorable, active, and multicultural.
  2. The product/service and all accompanying marketing activity and programs. Example: General Mills using QR codes, apps and AR.
  3. Other associations indirectly transferred by linking the brand to some other entity (person, place, thing). Example: 42BELOW vodka references a latitude through New Zealand and its 42 percent alcohol content, leveraging New Zealand's perceived purity.

Choosing brand elements

Brand elements are trademarkable features that identify and differentiate the brand: names, logos, symbols, characters, slogans, jingles, packages, signage. Most strong brands use several (Nike: the "swoosh", "Just Do It", and the name of the Greek winged goddess of victory). The test: what would consumers think or feel about the product if the element were all they knew? (Nature Valley suggests healthful food; Panasonic Toughbook suggests durable.) Legal protection: trademarks for names and elements, patents for manufacturing processes, copyrights and proprietary designs for packaging.

Watch for brands drifting generic (Kleenex, Kitty Litter, Jell-O, Scotch Tape, Xerox, Fiberglass): owners must keep trademark rights. A name with inherent meaning may make it harder to add a different meaning or reposition later. Important elements often capture intangible traits: insurers use symbols of strength (Rock of Gibraltar for Prudential, the stag for Hartford) or security (Allstate's "good hands", the Travelers umbrella, Fireman's Fund hard hat).

  • Slogans are efficient "hooks" ("Like a Good Neighbor, State Farm Is There"). Be careful replacing good ones: Citi dropped "Citi Never Sleeps" for "Let's Get It Done" and returned to the old one; Avis replaced "We Try Harder" (50 years) with the weaker "It's Your Space."
  • Characters have human traits that boost likability and make the brand interesting and fun: Keebler elves, Michelin's Bibendum (safety; credited with 80 percent awareness worldwide; a "Passport" limits its use), M&M's spokescandies, Ronald McDonald (since 1963), Mr. Clean (1957). They help break through clutter, soft-sell a benefit, and "don't demand pay raises or misbehave."
  • Packaging is a cost-effective way to build equity; called the "last five seconds of marketing", "permanent media", "the last salesman", even the "fifth P". It builds equity directly (functional or aesthetic differentiation) or indirectly (reinforcing awareness and image). Color codes matter (red: Ritz, Folgers, Colgate, Target, Coca-Cola).
  • Labels communicate written, electronic or graphical information on or attached to the product: identify, describe attributes, highlight benefits, instruct on use, storage and disposal, add aesthetic appeal, and leverage the brand.

Linking the brand to a person, place or thing with meaning in consumers' minds creates secondary sources of brand knowledge (Figure 10.3).

What brand architecture decides

Brand architecture gives guidelines about a company's branding strategy and which brand elements apply across new and existing products and services. It is a "product roadmap to the future" that clarifies where the brand can go and how. It defines brand breadth (boundaries: which products share a name) and brand depth (complexity: how many variations of the name). Related decisions: brand portfolios, cobranding and brand extensions.

Brand portfolios

A brand can only be stretched so far, and not every segment views it equally. The brand portfolio is the set of all brands and brand lines a firm offers in a category or segment. Reasons for multiple brands: serve multiple segments; increase shelf presence and retailer dependence; retain variety seekers who might switch; increase internal competition; exploit economies of scale in advertising, sales, merchandising and distribution.

  • Principle: maximize market coverage (no target customers ignored) and minimize brand overlap. Each brand must be clearly differentiated and appeal to a segment big enough to justify its costs.
  • Optimal portfolio: each brand maximizes equity in combination with the others. If profits rise by dropping brands the portfolio is too big; if they rise by adding brands it is not big enough.
  • Monitor over time and prune weak brands: poorly differentiated offerings suffer cannibalization and brand dilution.

Ingredient branding is a special case of cobranding that builds equity for materials, components or parts necessarily contained within other branded products. Definitions

A brand extension is using an established brand to introduce a new product in a different category or price tier (Honda: cars, motorcycles, snowblowers, lawn mowers, marine engines, snowmobiles, "six Hondas in a two-car garage"). The existing brand that gives birth to the extension is the parent brand; if it is already linked to multiple products it is also a master brand or family brand. When a new brand is combined with the parent (Hershey Kisses, Adobe Acrobat, Toyota Camry, Courtyard by Marriott) the extension is also called a sub-brand.

Most new products (typically 80 to 90 percent in any year) are brand extensions, and many top performers are (Dunkin' Donuts coffee, Progresso Light soups, Hormel Compleats). Still, many new products launch as new brands.

When to extend: vertical vs. horizontal

Judge each extension by how well it leverages parent equity and how well it contributes to parent equity (Crest Whitestrips used Crest's dental reputation and reinforced its dental authority; Bulgari moved into hotels, restaurants, fragrances, chocolate and skin care).

Questions to ask and research insights

Does the parent have significant equity? Is there a strong basis of fit? Will the extension have the optimal points of parity and points of difference? How can marketing programs enhance extension equity? What are the implications for parent equity and profitability?

  • Success needs favorable parent associations plus perceived fit (product-related attributes and benefits, or common usage situations or user types). Kate Spade went from handbags to fragrances, jewelry and apparel.
  • A brand seen as prototypical of its category is hard to extend outside it (Coca-Cola fresh-squeezed juices); positive associations in the original category can turn negative. Concrete attribute associations are harder to extend than abstract benefit associations.
  • Vertical extensions often need sub-branding to prevent negative associations (upscale) or dilution and cannibalization (downscale): Armani Exchange.

Case: Bic perfume (a failed extension)

Bic's "inexpensive, disposable" strategy created markets for ballpoint pens (late 1950s), lighters (early 1970s) and razors (early 1980s). It then launched $5 perfumes ("Nuit", "Jour", "Bic for Men", "Bic Sport for Men") in quarter-ounce bottles that looked like fat lighters, with a $20 million campaign ("Paris in Your Pocket"). It failed: Bic lacked cachet and had negative image associations. The mistake was judging fit on one or a few associations instead of all consumers' brand knowledge.

Brand revitalization

Successful brands grow by acquiring new customers and securing intense, active loyalty from existing ones, so most evolve. When a brand loses momentum or stops growing, it needs revitalization. Cadillac, Fiat and Volkswagen turned fortunes around; GM rescued Cadillac with an overhaul of its lineup (CT sedans; XT and Escalade SUVs; LYRIQ and CELESTIQ electric vehicles) and breakthrough marketing (Lenny Kravitz designing his own CELESTIQ).

First step: understand the original sources of brand equity. Are positive associations losing strength or uniqueness? Have negative associations become linked? Then decide whether to retain the positioning or create a new one.

  • Back to basics: when the marketing program fails to deliver on the brand promise. Harley-Davidson regained leadership by living up to expectations for product performance; Pabst returned to its roots, using iconic packaging and imagery and a perception of authenticity.
  • Reinvention: when the old positioning is no longer viable. Mountain Dew overhauled its image; Old Spice moved from the aftershave and cologne gift set boomers gave their dads to contemporary men's grooming for Millennials, using product innovation and tongue-in-cheek communications (P&G). It is often easier to revive a brand that is alive but forgotten.
  • Burberry (150-year history; trench coats, royal supplier): plaid was overused and counterfeited; outerwear only 20 percent of business. It reinforced its heritage and core luxury products, removed the plaid from 90 percent of products, created 300+ coats, opened stores in desirable locations, trained sales staff and redesigned its online presence for Millennials and Gen Z. (Burberry sits between the poles in this guide's reading; the chapter does not label it.)

Managing a brand crisis

Assume a crisis will come (Chick-fil-A, BP, Domino's, Toyota; financial firms such as Bank of America, JPMorgan and AIG). Repercussions: lost sales, reduced marketing effectiveness, more sensitivity to rivals' marketing, and less impact on competing brands; executives and even founders may need to step down. The stronger the brand and corporate image (especially credibility and trustworthiness), the more likely it weathers the storm, but preparation and a managed crisis program are also critical. Consumers must see the response as swift and sincere; the longer the delay the more negative impressions form, and some permanently switch.

What makes a brand "luxury"

Luxury brands (Prada, Gucci, Cartier, Louis Vuitton) are among the purest examples of branding: brand and image are key competitive advantages. Some believe it is now a $350 billion industry. They are priced significantly above typical items in the category. Once mostly about social status, luxury in many developed countries is now about style and substance, combining personal pleasure and self-expression. The shopper must feel they get something truly special, so the common denominators are quality and uniqueness. A winning formula for many is craftsmanship, heritage, authenticity and history, often critical to justify a high price. Hermès sells classics "not because they are in fashion, but because they never go out of fashion."

The big idea

Going global is a sequence of decisions, and the first is the simplest to state and the hardest to answer: should we go abroad at all? The chapter opens with Hyundai, once “synonymous with cheap and unreliable cars.” In 1999 its chairman chose quality over volume and market share; it benchmarked Toyota, adopted Six Sigma, and backed its cars with a groundbreaking 10-year warranty. From 32nd of 37 brands in J.D. Power’s 2001 U.S. quality study, its luxury brand Genesis reached number 1 by 2018, and Hyundai now sells in over 200 countries. The warning that follows: opportunities are significant but risks can be high, and companies in global industries have no choice but to internationalize.

Choosing which countries

Choose on the product and on geography, income, population and political climate. Competitive moves matter: it can pay to enter where rivals already are, to force them to defend share and to learn how they market there. Market growth is critical — a toehold in a fast-growing market can be attractive even if it will soon be crowded. KFC entered scores of countries as a pioneer by franchising and making its marketing culturally relevant: more than 25,000 restaurants in over 145 countries; in China, more than 10,000 locations in over 2,000 cities with healthy margins of about 20% per store (Dragon Twister wrap, mascot Chicky). A 2013 antibiotics accusation hurt China sales. In Africa, importing chickens is illegal in Nigeria and Kenya, so KFC added fish in Nigeria and localized menus (Ugali in Kenya, jollof rice in Nigeria).

Proximity shortcuts the choice. Physical proximity: neighbors are better understood and entry costs are easier to control (Canada and Mexico are the two largest U.S. export markets; Swedish firms first sold to Scandinavian neighbors). Cultural proximity: familiar language, laws and culture (U.S. firms often pick Canada, England and Australia over larger Germany and France). The risk: overlooking better markets and only superficially analyzing real differences. It often makes sense to operate in fewer countries with deeper commitment.

The entry ladder (Figure 20.2)

Once a country is chosen, there are five broad modes of entry. The chapter says each succeeding strategy brings more commitment, risk, control and profit potential.

Indirect and direct exporting

Indirect exporting means working through independent intermediaries. The chapter says “three types” but describes four: export merchants (buy the products, then sell abroad), export agents including trading companies (seek and negotiate foreign purchases for a commission), cooperative organizations (export for several producers, often of primary products such as fruits or nuts, partly under their administrative control) and export-management companies (manage export activities for a fee). Advantages: less investment (no export department, sales force or contacts) and less risk (intermediaries bring know-how).

Direct exporting means handling exports yourself: a domestic export department or division (which may become its own profit center), an overseas sales branch or subsidiary (sales, distribution, sometimes warehousing, promotion and service), or home-country-based traveling export sales representatives. Investment and risk are somewhat greater, as is the potential return. Many firms use exporting to “test the waters” before building a plant; the internet can replace trade shows if the site offers country-specific content, ideally in the local language.

Licensing and its variants

In licensing, the licensor grants a foreign licensee the right to use a manufacturing process, trademark, patent, trade secret or other valuable item for a fee or royalty. The licensor gets entry at little risk; the licensee gets production expertise and/or a well-known brand. Downsides: less control than over its own facilities, profits given up if the licensee succeeds, and a possible new competitor when the contract ends. Defenses: supply proprietary ingredients or components (Coca-Cola’s syrup) and lead in innovation so the licensee stays dependent.

Joint ventures

Foreign and local investors form a company and share ownership and control. It may be necessary or desirable for economic or political reasons: the foreign firm lacks financial, physical or managerial resources, or the government requires joint ownership as a condition of entry. Good partners also share brand values (McDonald’s hand-picks “compulsive achievers”). The chapter cites many alliances: Star Alliance (26 airlines), Vodafone and Telefónica sharing networks, BMW and Jaguar Land Rover on EV technology, Toyota and Didi, Airbus and Bombardier, and GSK/Sanofi and Pfizer/BioNTech on COVID-19 vaccines. Drawbacks: partners may disagree over investment or marketing (one wants to reinvest earnings, the other to declare dividends), and joint ownership can block worldwide manufacturing and marketing policies.

Direct investment and acquisition

Direct investment is the ultimate form of foreign involvement: buy part or full interest in a local company or build your own manufacturing or service facilities. If the market is large enough, there are five advantages:

  1. Cost economies (cheaper labor or raw materials, government incentives, freight savings).
  2. A stronger image in the host country by creating jobs.
  3. Deeper relationships with government, customers, local suppliers and distributors, so products adapt better.
  4. Full control over the investment and long-term policies.
  5. Assured market access if the host country demands domestic content.

The disadvantage: a large investment exposed to blocked or devalued currencies, worsening markets or expropriation; high severance pay can make downsizing expensive; and the offering may not fit local tastes. Starbucks in Australia (first store 2000, nearly 90 locations) lost $105 million and closed two-thirds of its stores in 2008 because drinks were pricier and a bit too sweet.

Acquisition couples a local presence with advanced technology, proprietary know-how and modern manufacturing. SABMiller built a presence in 75 countries by buying local brewers and calling itself “the most local of global brewers” (Peru’s Cusqueña, Romania’s Timișoreana, Ghana’s Chibuku priced at about 58 cents a liter to compete with home brews, Poland’s Tyskie), in contrast to Anheuser-Busch InBev selling Budweiser worldwide as “The American Dream in a Bottle.” AB InBev acquired SABMiller in 2016. VW upgraded Czech carmaker Škoda (over 866,000 vehicles in 2023); Kraft bought Cadbury for its emerging-market roots (India) and used the deal to split into grocery and snack companies. Drawbacks: the investment required, possible corporate-culture mismatch, and a longer-term commitment to the country.

The central tension

International companies must decide how extensively to adapt marketing to local conditions. At one extreme is a standardized marketing program worldwide, promising the greatest consistency. At the other is a localized program, where the company, consistent with the marketing concept, believes consumer needs vary and tailors marketing to each target group.

Oreo: Kraft kept one global positioning (“Milk’s Favorite Cookie”) and wrote a brand book in an Oreo-shaped box listing what must be common, what can change and what cannot. Research then showed taste differences — Chinese consumers found the cookie too sweet, Indian consumers too bitter — so new formulas followed (less sweet with green tea ice cream or fruit fillings in China; chocolate-and-peanut in Indonesia; banana and dulce de leche in Argentina). Some flavors moved to other countries: reverse innovation. Local campaigns also connect, such as the UK’s #OREOtwists (“there is no wrong way to eat an OREO cookie”).

Three basic strategies

Developing global product strategies requires knowing which products or services are easily standardized and which adaptation strategies fit. There are three: straight extension, product adaptation and product invention (the chapter also calls the last “product innovation”).

Straight extension and when standardization backfires

Straight extension is tempting because it needs no extra R&D, retooling or promotional change. Many high-end and luxury goods standardize because quality and prestige market similarly across countries. TAG Heuer (Swiss, founded 1860; LVMH since 1999) cut points of sale from 4,000 to 2,500, grew stand-alone boutiques, raised prices (an entry-level Formula One watch starts at $1,450), and sells the Connected smartwatch (15% of sales); China is about 10% of sales. Food and beverage standardize less easily because of varying tastes and habits. Standardization backfires when consumers differ in knowledge, preferences and usage:

Dealing with counterfeit products

As supply chains stretch farther from home, opportunities for corruption, fraud and quality-control problems rise, and factories can copy almost anything. Counterfeiting costs an estimated more than a trillion dollars a year; luxury brands (Hermès, LVMH, Tiffany) lose tens of billions. Microsoft once estimated about 90% of its Windows software in China was pirated; LVMH estimated 90% of Louis Vuitton and Christian Dior pieces on eBay were fakes and sued. Defense: online, AI-based software finds counterfeit storefronts by detecting ads similar to legitimate brands, unauthorized sites plastering trademarks and logos, keywords like cheap, discount, authentic and factory, colors the products were never made in, and prices that are far too low — and warns violators without human intervention.

Why the same product costs more abroad

The same Gucci handbag may sell for $2,000 in Italy, $3,000 in the U.S. and $4,000 in China. Transfer pricing and dumping

Market-based pricing can lead to a transfer price: one unit of a company charging another unit (e.g., a foreign subsidiary) for goods shipped. Too high a price may mean higher tariff duties (though possibly lower foreign income taxes). Too low and the company can be accused of dumping — charging either less than its costs or less than it charges at home to enter or win a market. Governments watch for abuse and often force prices similar to competitors’ for the same or a similar product.

Tough price markets: countries with overcapacity, cheap currencies and a need to export aggressively push prices and currencies down, making sales difficult. When IKEA opened in Beijing in 2002, local stores sold copies of its designs at a fraction of its prices; it slashed prices by stocking Chinese-made products, down as much as 70% below prices outside China, and keeps stores in 37 Chinese locations despite persistent knockoffs.

Distribution: channels vary by country

When multinationals first enter a country they prefer local distributors with local knowledge, but friction arises: the multinational complains the distributor does not invest in growth, follow policy or share information; the distributor complains of insufficient corporate support, impossible goals and confusing policies. The fix: choose the right distributors, invest in them and set mutually agreed performance goals.

Retail units differ too. U.S. retail is dominated by large chains; much foreign retailing is small independents. In India, millions of tiny shops and open markets mean high markups but haggling brings the real price down; incomes are low, homes lack storage and refrigeration, and shoppers buy daily (even one cigarette at a time). Breaking bulk remains an important intermediary function and perpetuates long channels, a major obstacle to large-scale retailing in developing countries. Global retailers (Carrefour, Aldi, Metro, Tesco) keep expanding, though even Walmart withdrew from Germany and South Korea after heavy losses.

The gray market

The gray market diverts branded products from authorized distribution channels, in-country or across borders. Typically some enterprising distributors buy more than they can sell at home and reship the goods to another country to exploit price differences.

Harms: a free-rider problem that makes legitimate distributors’ investments in supporting the product less productive and pushes selective distribution systems toward being more intensive; damaged distributor relationships; tarnished brand equity; undermined channel integrity; and consumer risk if products are damaged, relabeled, obsolete, without warranty or support, or counterfeit. High-priced prescription drugs are a frequent target (fake vials of Roche’s cancer drug Avastin reached U.S. doctors). Prevention: police distributors, raise prices to lower-cost distributors, and alter product characteristics or service warranties by country. One study found gray-market activity was most deterred when penalties were severe, when violations could be detected and punished in a timely way, or both.

What VOC analysis is

It uses the customer’s own words to understand their needs, how well those needs are met, and how they might respond to new features, positioning or messaging. Insights can emerge that the company never thought to ask about, and the customer’s language can be reused so benefits are described in words that resonate.

1. Needs/benefits analysis

Start from a text corpus (here, reviews). Topic modeling finds the main themes, which mostly reflect needs or benefits sought. Aspect-based sentiment analysis then scores each topic positive or negative separately, regardless of the review’s overall tone.

Example from the slides

“The camera takes beautiful pictures, but the battery life is terrible.” The customer wants image quality and battery life; the first need is met, the second is not. If many customers say the same about many products, there is a market-wide unmet need, and an opportunity.

Positive sentiment = need met; negative = need unmet. Unmet needs are openings for new features, positioning or messaging. Needs are broader than specs: performance, ease of use, fit with a usage occasion, the buying process, fit with complementary products, perceived value.

How important is each need? Two measures

By the end of the needs analysis you should know the different needs, which are met and unmet, how important each is as a driver of satisfaction, the key pain points, and where expectations and experience don’t match.

Types of needsSigns of an unmet need
Functional (what it must do); emotional (how they want to feel); social (how they want to be seen, sharing); contextual (situations of use); practical constraints (budget, skill, portability, age, technical comfort, connectivity, durability, time pressure)Repeated complaints; workarounds; trade-offs accepted reluctantly; things expected but not received; comparisons to smartphones or older cameras; gaps between specs and real value; the same frustration described without a solution

2–6. The remaining analyses

  • Segmentation: group customers by the benefits they emphasize (benefit segments, as in the L’Oréal case). Aim for a parsimonious number: homogeneous within, heterogeneous between. Too few and different customers share a segment; too many and two segments should really be one. Count customers per segment. Possible segments include travelers, family memory keepers, beginners, smartphone upgraders, older or less technical users, gift recipients, photographers wanting a small second camera, zoom-focused users, vloggers, budget buyers, and point-and-shoot replacers. Demographic guesses from AI (from words like “grandkids”) may not be very accurate.
  • Journey: trigger, research and comparison, who is involved, channel (mostly Amazon), unboxing and setup, everyday use, travel or special events, sharing photos, support and returns, long-term ownership.
  • Competitive analysis: per brand: review volume, average stars, core strengths, common complaints, differentiating themes, where each is strong or vulnerable, and where a premium manufacturer could credibly differentiate.
  • Customer language bank: short representative phrases about motivation, desired outcomes, pain points, benefits, smartphone comparisons, travel, family, beginner use, quality and reliability, value and price.
  • Opportunity map: rank opportunities by prevalence, pain intensity, impact on ratings, degree of unmet need, strategic fit, differentiation potential, feasibility, and your confidence in the evidence.

Limitations and tips (from the slides)

  • It’s hard to know how GPT reaches conclusions, and hard to keep it to the data; told the reviews are about cameras, it will name image quality as a key need almost regardless. Don’t let it be lazy, and keep it doing the work (a Pro subscription balked at all 184K reviews).
  • Review titles carry a lot of information; check that they are analyzed.
  • Talk to it with follow-ups (e.g. “Among reviewers who mention portability, are they comparing to a phone or a DSLR?”). Let AI do the volume work. Ask it to describe a VOC analysis first, check what’s missing and add guard rails (stick to the review text), then have it write the analysis prompt.
