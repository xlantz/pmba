Step 1 · Start here

A liability is a promise to pay for something that already happened

This unit answers two questions. First: does a promise go on the books (and for how much), in the footnotes, or nowhere? Second: for borrowed money, how do interest and the balance move every period?

The tab and the savings slip

The tab. You ate dinner (past event), the bill is coming (obligation), and you know roughly how much (estimable). That is a liability, so it goes on the books. A possible lawsuit from a customer who might sue is only worth a footnote, and a far-fetched one is not mentioned at all.

The savings slip. A bond is a loan from many people, written as a slip: “pay the holder $1,000 at the end, plus $60 a year.” If everyone else's savings accounts now pay 8%, nobody hands you the full $1,000 for a slip paying only 6%. They pay less, and that gap is the discount. If accounts pay only 4%, your 6% slip is a bargain and they pay extra: a premium. A mortgage is the same logic from the other side: the bank charges interest on whatever you still owe, so as the balance shrinks, so does the interest part of each payment.

Six words to know before anything else

Liability
Something the company owes because of a past event.
Accrue
Record an expense and a liability for time that has passed, even though no cash has moved yet.
Face value
The lump sum a bond repays at the end. Also called par.
Stated rate
The fixed rate printed on the bond. Cash interest = face × stated rate.
Market rate
What investors could earn elsewhere right now. It sets the bond's price.
Carrying amount
What the bond or loan is carried at on the books today: face ± unamortized premium/discount.

Two tiny examples

1. Accrued interest. A $10,000 note at 6% has been outstanding 73 days at year-end (365-day year).

Interest = $10,000 × 6% × 73/365 = $120
Dr Interest Expense 120 / Cr Interest Payable 120

2. A one-year bond. Face $1,000, stated rate 6% (investors get $1,060 in total), but the market rate is 8%.

Price = $1,060 ÷ 1.08 = $981.48 (discount $18.52)
Interest expense = $981.48 × 8% = $78.52
= $60 cash coupon + $18.52 discount amortized
Carrying amount = $981.48 + $18.52 = $1,000 = face

That is the bond half of the unit in four lines: price below face, expense above cash, the gap amortizes, carrying amount climbs to face.

The basic idea: a liability is a promise to pay, based on something that already happened

Some liabilities are simple: a company signs a loan agreement for exactly $50,000, so it records exactly $50,000 as a liability. No guessing involved. But plenty of obligations are messier — a company gets sued, or sells a product with a warranty, and has no idea yet exactly how much it'll end up owing, or even whether it'll owe anything at all. Accountants call these contingent liabilities, and there's a specific framework for deciding whether — and how — to put them on the books.

A liability like this needs three ingredients: a present obligation (the company actually owes something, or probably will), arising from a past event (something that's already happened, not something that might happen in the future), where the eventual payment is probable and can be reasonably estimated. When all three line up, it gets recorded. When they don't, the rules ask two more questions: how likely is the payout, and can it be estimated?

How likely is the payout?Can it be estimated?What the company does
ProbableYesRecord it as a liability and an expense right now
ProbableNoDisclose it in the footnotes only — describe it, but don't put a number on the balance sheet
Reasonably possible (but not probable)EitherDisclose it in the footnotes only
Remote (unlikely)EitherDo nothing — not recorded, not even mentioned

A useful way to picture this: imagine a dial running from "definitely won't happen" to "will definitely happen." Only the far end of that dial — probable and estimable — actually shows up as a number on the balance sheet. Everything in the middle gets a mention in the footnotes so a reader at least knows it exists. The far "won't happen" end gets nothing at all, because putting unlikely, remote risks on the financial statements would make them harder to read, not more informative.

Examples of the kinds of things this framework applies to: pending lawsuits, environmental cleanup obligations, employee bonus plans, and product warranties. One important exception: a signed, definite obligation — like a note payable with a stated dollar amount and due date — isn't a "contingency" at all. There's nothing uncertain about it, so it just gets recorded at its full amount; you don't run it through the probable/possible/remote test.

Warranties: expense now, pay later

A warranty is a great example of "probable and estimable": a company that sells 500,000 toasters already knows, from experience, that some percentage will come back for repair under warranty — it just doesn't know which ones yet. The matching principle says the expense belongs in the period the toasters were sold, not spread out over however many years customers trickle in with a broken toaster. So the company estimates the total cost up front and records it all at once.

This creates two separate numbers that are easy to mix up:

Warranty Expense (for the period) = Units Sold × Expected Repair Rate × Average Repair Cost
Warranty Liability (at a point in time) = Total Estimated Repair Cost − Repairs Already Performed

The expense is a one-time number booked at the moment of sale, covering everything expected to go wrong with that whole batch. The liability is a running balance — how much of that promise is still outstanding right now, after subtracting whatever's already been fixed.

Worked example: a company sells 500,000 units this year. Based on history, it expects 3% to need a repair, averaging $160 per repair. By year-end, 12,000 units have already been repaired.

  • Expected repairs = 500,000 × 3% = 15,000 units
  • Warranty expense = 15,000 × $160 = $2,400,000 (recorded immediately, at time of sale)
  • Warranty liability at year-end = (15,000 − 12,000) × $160 = $480,000 (what's still owed, since 12,000 of the expected 15,000 repairs are already done)

One subtlety worth sitting with: when a customer actually brings in a broken toaster and it gets repaired, that repair does not create a new expense (assuming the original estimate was reasonable) — the expense was already booked back at the time of sale. Servicing the claim just uses up part of the liability that's already sitting on the books, the same way paying off part of a loan doesn't create a new expense; it just reduces a balance. If claims end up running higher than expected, the company revises its estimate going forward — a change that only affects the current and future periods, not something that requires going back and restating last year's numbers.

Bonuses: accrue what employees have already earned

A bonus plan is the same idea as a warranty: the work is done this year, so the cost belongs to this year even if the check is written next year. If executives get 4% of pretax income and pretax income before the bonus is $950,000, the bonus liability is 4% × $950,000 = $38,000: Dr Bonus Expense / Cr Bonus Payable.

What is not a liability yet: executory contracts and plans

A liability needs a past event. If you owe a supplier $185,000 for inventory you have already received, that is a liability. But if you have only signed an agreement to buy $90,000 of equipment next year and nothing has been delivered, nothing has happened yet. This is an executory contract: a promise on both sides that has not been performed. It is not recorded (at most a footnote). The same goes for a planned expansion or an expected drop in sales: all future events, so no liability. Total liability in that example: $185,000, not $275,000.

Three-second test Has the thing already been delivered, used, earned or caused? If yes, it may be a liability. If it is only planned or promised, it is not.
Exam angleCheck first whether the obligation is even uncertain: a signed note with a stated amount just gets recorded. For lawsuits: probable and estimable = record; probable but not estimable = footnote; reasonably possible = footnote; remote = nothing at all. Warranty expense is booked once, at the time of sale, for the whole expected cost (units × repair rate × cost per repair); repairs later just use up the liability (liability = estimate − repairs done) and create no new expense. Bonuses are accrued when earned. Executory contracts and future plans are not liabilities.

The core lesson of this whole section

If you borrow money and agree to pay it all back — principal plus interest — in one lump sum when the loan matures, it's tempting to think nothing needs to be recorded until that day arrives. That's wrong, and it's the single most important idea in this section: interest accrues continuously, day by day, regardless of when cash actually changes hands. "No payment yet" does not mean "no expense yet." If a loan is outstanding on December 31st and a company is closing its books for the year, it owes interest for every day the loan has existed so far this year — even if the actual check won't be written until months later.

The formula for simple interest over a stretch of days:

Interest = Principal × Annual Rate × (Days Outstanding ÷ 365)

The one-line version (what most exam questions ask)

Most questions give you the days accrued and ask for one number: principal × annual rate × days ÷ 365.

NoteCalculationAccrued interest
$50,000 at 9%, 75 days$4,500 × 75/365$925
$72,000 at 6%, 45 days$4,320 × 45/365$533
$40,000 at 8%, 90 days$3,200 × 90/365$789 (a 360-day year would give $800)

Several notes? Compute each, then add them into one Interest Payable balance ($425 + $610 + $295 = $1,330). The year-end entry is Dr Interest Expense / Cr Interest Payable: expenses and liabilities both rise, cash is untouched.

Full worked example: a note that crosses a year-end

A company borrows $100,000 at a 12% annual rate on November 15, 2025. The note is due in 90 days, which lands on February 13, 2026. The company's fiscal year ends December 31st — right in the middle of the loan's life. That means this one loan touches two fiscal years, and needs an entry at three separate moments.

Step 1 — November 15, 2025 (borrowing the money):

Debit Cash $100,000   |   Credit Note Payable $100,000

Step 2 — December 31, 2025 (year-end: accrue the interest earned so far, even though nothing is due yet). From November 15 to December 31 is 46 days.

Interest = $100,000 × 12% × (46 ÷ 365) = $1,512
Debit Interest Expense $1,512   |   Credit Interest Payable $1,512

At this point, the December 31, 2025 balance sheet shows two separate liabilities related to this one loan: Note Payable of $100,000, and Interest Payable of $1,512. Both are real obligations, even though no cash has moved since the original $100,000 came in.

Step 3 — February 13, 2026 (maturity: settle everything at once). From January 1 to February 13 is the remaining 44 days of the 90-day term.

Remaining interest = $100,000 × 12% × (44 ÷ 365) ≈ $1,447

At maturity, three things need to happen in the same entry: recognize 2026's slice of interest expense (the 44 days that belong to this new fiscal year), close out the Interest Payable that was set up at the end of 2025, and pay everything — principal plus all the interest — in cash.

Debit Interest Expense  $1,447  (2026's portion only)
Debit Interest Payable  $1,512  (closing out 2025's accrual)
Debit Note Payable         $100,000
    Credit Cash                     $102,959

A good way to check your work on any problem like this: the two pieces of interest expense should always add up to the interest on the whole loan, calculated just once, start to finish. Here: $1,512 + $1,447 = $2,959, which matches $100,000 × 12% × (90 ÷ 365) ≈ $2,959. The year-end accrual doesn't change the total interest owed on the loan — it just decides which fiscal year each slice of it belongs to.

Exam angleAny time a problem gives you a note that spans a fiscal year-end, expect three separate calculations: the accrual at year-end, the remaining interest after year-end, and a combined settlement entry at maturity that includes both this year's interest and paying off last year's Interest Payable balance. Forgetting the year-end accrual entirely is the single most common mistake here.

Why the same monthly payment covers less interest every month

Think of a typical home mortgage: the same dollar payment goes out every single month for 30 years. But under the hood, that fixed payment is doing two different jobs at once — part of it pays interest, and part of it actually reduces what's owed (the "principal"). Here's the part that surprises people: interest is always calculated on whatever balance is currently outstanding — not the original loan amount. So as the balance slowly shrinks month after month, the interest portion of each payment shrinks right along with it, which means more and more of that same fixed payment goes toward knocking down the principal instead. This is why payoff accelerates the longer a loan runs — the last few years of a mortgage pay off principal dramatically faster than the first few.

Splitting a payment into interest and principal

Each period, work it in this order:

Interest portion = Current Balance × Periodic Interest Rate
Principal portion = Total Payment − Interest portion
New Balance = Old Balance − Principal portion

What's a "periodic" rate? Loans quote interest as an annual rate, but payments usually happen more often than once a year. The periodic rate just answers "how much of that annual rate applies to this one payment?" — you get it by dividing the annual rate by however many payments happen per year: monthly payments → divide by 12; semiannual payments → divide by 2; quarterly → divide by 4. A 6% annual rate paid monthly becomes a 0.5% periodic rate (6%÷12); paid semiannually it becomes 3% (6%÷2). Same annual rate, different periodic rate, depending on how often the loan is actually paid.

Worked example: a $300,000 mortgage at 6% annual interest, 30 years, paid monthly. (The fixed monthly payment for a loan like this works out to $1,799 — that number comes from a standard loan-payment formula, the same one your bank uses, and Excel's PMT function will compute it for you instantly once you know the rate, number of payments, and loan amount.)

  • Month 1: Interest = $300,000 × (6%÷12) = $1,500. Principal = $1,799 − $1,500 = $299. New balance = $300,000 − $299 = $299,701.
  • Month 2 starts from that new, lower balance: Interest = $299,701 × (6%÷12) = $1,498.51, so slightly less interest and slightly more principal than Month 1 — and this pattern just keeps compounding, month after month, for the life of the loan.

The professor's version: a semiannual mortgage, step by step

A $600,000 mortgage at 8% a year, paid semiannually, with a $45,000 payment. “Semiannual” means the rate for one period is 8% ÷ 2 = 4% (the same as × ½).

Interest (balance × 4%)Principal (payment − interest)New balance
Payment 1$600,000 × 4% = $24,000$45,000 − $24,000 = $21,000$579,000
Payment 2$579,000 × 4% = $23,160$45,000 − $23,160 = $21,840$557,160

Interest falls (24,000 → 23,160) and principal rises (21,000 → 21,840) while the payment stays level. “Carrying amount” of the mortgage = the balance after the payment.

A longer example, with a full schedule

The same logic works for any payment frequency. Here's a $4,000,000 mortgage at 10% annual interest over 15 years, paid semiannually (twice a year) — so the periodic rate is 10%÷2 = 5% per period, over 15×2 = 30 periods, giving a fixed payment of $260,206 each period:

PeriodInterest (5% × balance)Cash paidPrincipal reductionNew balance

Exam angleAlways compute the interest portion first, from the current balance (balance × annual rate × fraction of the year), then principal = payment − interest, then new balance = old balance − principal. Each payment has both interest and principal; interest shrinks over time because the balance shrinks, not because the rate or payment changes.

What a bond actually is

A bond is just a very structured IOU. Instead of borrowing from one bank, a company (or a government) borrows from many investors simultaneously, each buying a small slice of a much larger loan. In exchange, the company promises two things: regular fixed interest payments (called coupon payments) throughout the bond's life, and one lump-sum repayment of the face value (also called par value) when the bond matures.

Why bother with bonds instead of a simple bank loan or selling more stock? Three reasons: access to a much larger pool of money than any single bank could lend; locking in specific terms (a rate and a maturity date) up front, for the life of the bond; and control — borrowing money doesn't give bondholders any ownership stake or voting rights the way issuing new stock would.

Why a bond almost never sells for exactly its face value

Every bond has a stated rate (also called the coupon rate) printed right on it — the fixed rate used to calculate the coupon payments. But the actual price investors are willing to pay depends on comparing that stated rate to the market rate: the return investors could get elsewhere on an equally risky investment right now. The market rate moves around with the economy and investor sentiment; the stated rate is locked in and never changes once the bond is issued.

ComparisonResult
Stated rate = Market rateBond sells at par (exactly at face value)
Stated rate < Market rateBond sells at a discount (below face value — investors won't pay full price for a below-market coupon)
Stated rate > Market rateBond sells at a premium (above face value — investors will pay extra for an above-market coupon)

Quoted prices: 103 means 103% of face

Bond prices are quoted as a percentage of face value. 103 = 103% of face; 96 = 96% of face.

QuoteFaceCash receivedPremium / discount
103$500,000$500,000 × 1.03 = $515,000$15,000 premium
96$400,000$400,000 × 0.96 = $384,000$16,000 discount

Three facts that never change with the market

  • Cash interest = face × STATED rate (× ½ for semiannual). $800,000 of 7% bonds pay $28,000 every six months, even if the bonds sold for $747,321 at a 7.75% market yield. The market rate only affects the price and the interest expense.
  • Carrying amount = face + unamortized premium, or face − unamortized discount. $300,000 face with $12,000 unamortized premium = $312,000; $250,000 face with a $7,000 unamortized discount = $243,000.
  • Carrying amount converges to face. Premium or discount amortizes to zero, so at maturity the carrying amount equals what is repaid.

After issue: prices move opposite to market rates

The coupon is frozen, so when market rates fall, the old bond pays more than new ones and its price rises; when market rates rise, its price falls. A bond trading at a premium tells you market rates are now below its stated rate. None of this changes the cash interest the issuer owes.

Careful with the word “discount”: as a verb it means converting future dollars to today's value (every bond price does that); as a noun it means the price is below face. A bond can be “discounted” without selling “at a discount.”

Exam angleStated > market = premium; stated < market = discount; equal = par. Reason it out rather than memorizing: nobody pays full face for a below-market coupon. Cash interest always uses the stated rate on face value. Quote × face = cash received. Rates up, price down.

The running example

A company issues $1,000,000 of 5-year bonds with a 6% stated rate, paid semiannually ($30,000 every six months). The market rate is 8% (4% per half-year, 10 periods), so the bonds sell for $918,891, a discount. (How that price is computed is in Go deeper ▲1; on the exam it is given.)

Issue entries: three cases

CaseEntry
At parDr Cash / Cr Bonds Payable (both at face)
At a discountDr Cash (price received), Dr Discount on Bonds Payable (face − price) / Cr Bonds Payable at face
At a premiumDr Cash (price received) / Cr Bonds Payable at face, Cr Premium on Bonds Payable (price − face)

Bonds Payable is always credited at face value, never at the cash received. Example: $900,000 of 6% bonds issued for $862,131 → Dr Cash 862,131, Dr Discount 37,869, Cr Bonds Payable 900,000.

Issuance journal entry:

Debit Cash                       $918,891
Debit Discount on Bonds Payable  $81,109
    Credit Bonds Payable                       $1,000,000

The "Discount on Bonds Payable" account is a contra-liability — it doesn't represent a separate debt; it just sits alongside Bonds Payable on the balance sheet and reduces it down to the bond's actual current worth. Right at issuance: $1,000,000 face value minus the $81,109 discount equals exactly the $918,891 the company actually received. Over the bond's life, that discount gradually shrinks to zero, so by maturity the bond's carrying amount has grown back up to the full $1,000,000 it needs to repay.

Why interest expense doesn't equal the cash coupon payment

For a bond issued at a discount, each period's interest expense is calculated on the bond's current carrying value at the market rate — which is a bigger number than the fixed cash coupon, since the coupon was set using the lower stated rate. The gap between them isn't lost anywhere; it gets added onto the bond's carrying value, which is exactly how the discount gradually amortizes away to zero by maturity.

First-period amortization, continuing the example above:

  • Cash coupon paid (fixed, from the stated rate): $30,000
  • Interest expense (carrying value × market rate per period): $918,891 × 4% = $36,756
  • Discount amortized this period = $36,756 − $30,000 = $6,756
Debit Interest Expense  $36,756
    Credit Discount on Bonds Payable  $6,756
    Credit Cash                       $30,000

The bond's carrying value grows from $918,891 to $918,891 + $6,756 = $925,647. Next period, interest expense is recalculated on this new, slightly higher balance — the same "recalculate from the current balance every period" logic used for mortgages, just running in the opposite direction (growing toward face value instead of shrinking toward zero).

The first three periods of the running example (interest expense = carrying amount × 4%; cash = $30,000):

PeriodInterest expenseCash paidDiscount amortizedCarrying amount (end)
Issue———$918,891

Expense and discount amortization grow, cash stays level, and the carrying amount climbs toward $1,000,000. Another professor-style case: $900,000 of 6% bonds with carrying amount $862,131 and a 7% effective rate → expense = $862,131 × 7% × ½ = $30,175; cash = $900,000 × 6% × ½ = $27,000; discount amortized = $3,175 (Dr Interest Expense 30,175 / Cr Discount 3,175 / Cr Cash 27,000). Or simply: given expense $17,200 and cash $15,000, the $2,200 gap is the discount credit.

For a bond issued at a premium the logic runs the other way: expense (carrying amount × market rate) is less than the cash coupon, and the difference is a debit to Premium on Bonds Payable, shrinking the carrying amount toward face.

Paying off a bond, on schedule or early

At the scheduled maturity date, once the discount or premium has fully amortized away, the carrying value equals the face value exactly — so retiring the bond on time is simple:

Debit Bonds Payable $1,000,000   |   Credit Cash $1,000,000

But a company can also choose to retire (buy back) a bond early, before maturity — and in that case, the price it pays in the market might not match the bond's carrying amount on the books, producing a gain or loss:

Gain / (Loss) on Early Retirement = Carrying Amount at time of retirement − Cash Paid to Retire

If the company pays less than the carrying amount to buy the bond back, that's a gain (it settled the debt for less than it was "worth" on the books). If it pays more, that's a loss.

Retirement examples: face $250,000 with a $7,000 unamortized discount = carrying amount $243,000; retired for $240,000 → $3,000 gain. Paying more than carrying amount is a loss; paying less is a gain.

Not all liabilities are "debt"

Before computing any ratio, it helps to separate two overlapping ideas. Total liabilities means everything a company owes — accounts payable to suppliers, wages owed to employees, taxes owed to the government, deferred revenue, loans, bonds, all of it. Interest-bearing debt is a narrower slice: only the liabilities that actually charge interest, like notes, bonds, mortgages, and leases. An unpaid supplier invoice is a liability, but it isn't "debt" in this narrower sense — it doesn't charge interest the way a loan does.

Key ratios for reading a company's capital structure and coverage

† "Total Debt" in this particular ratio means total liabilities (everything owed), not the narrower interest-bearing slice — unless a problem tells you otherwise, treat "total debt" here the same way as "total liabilities." It's a case where everyday language ("debt") gets used loosely for what the balance sheet calls "liabilities," so watch for which one a question actually means. When a problem lists current debt and long-term debt separately, add them; never use long-term debt alone.

EBIT stands for Earnings Before Interest and Taxes — essentially, operating profit before any financing or tax effects are subtracted out. It's used specifically for times interest earned because the whole question is "can operating profit, on its own, cover the interest bill?" — a question you can't answer if interest has already been subtracted from the profit figure you're starting with.

A subtlety on averaging: unlike some ratios you may have seen elsewhere (like return on equity, which uses average equity to match against a full year's income), liabilities-to-equity and debt-to-equity are point-in-time comparisons — computed fresh at each balance sheet date, using the ending balances, not averaged across the year. That's because both the numerator and denominator are balance sheet "stock" figures (a snapshot at one moment), not "flow" figures earned gradually over a period.

A distortion worth knowing about: treasury stock

When a company buys back its own shares (called treasury stock), that repurchase directly reduces stockholders' equity on the balance sheet. If a company has been aggressively buying back stock for years, its equity balance can shrink dramatically — in extreme cases, even turn negative — which makes debt-to-equity and similar ratios look artificially alarming, even though nothing about the company's actual debt load has changed. To get a cleaner read, analysts sometimes add treasury stock back into both equity and total assets before computing these ratios, effectively asking "what would this ratio look like if the company hadn't spent years buying back its own stock?"

What each one measures: current ratio = short-term liquidity; quick ratio = liquidity using only the most liquid assets (cash, short-term investments, receivables — so it uses fewer assets than the current ratio); times interest earned = whether operating profit covers the interest bill; cash from operations to total debt = whether operating cash could pay down debt.

Exam angleUse the total debt the question implies (current + long-term), not just long-term. The quick ratio drops inventory and prepaids. Times interest earned uses EBIT, before interest. Leverage ratios use ending balances (point in time). Large buybacks shrink or flip equity, distorting debt-to-equity; analysts add treasury stock back.

How much of this loan is due next year? ("Current portion of long-term debt")

A balance sheet has to separate current liabilities (due within a year) from long-term ones. For a mortgage, that means splitting the remaining balance into "the part due in the next 12 months" and "everything after that." A very common mistake is assuming the entire remaining balance is somehow "current" just because payments are being made regularly. It isn't — only the principal portion of the payments falling in the next 12 months counts. To find it, look one year ahead in the amortization schedule (like the table above) and add up just the principal-reduction column for those periods. Everything else stays classified as long-term debt.

Exam angleFor “current portion of long-term debt,” resist using the whole remaining balance. Add up only the principal reduction for the payments falling in the next 12 months.

Not every bond is equally safe. Independent agencies assign credit ratings that estimate how likely a borrower is to actually pay everything back — not unlike a personal credit score.

Pricing a bond: present value of two separate cash flows

A bond's price is just the present value — today's worth — of everything an investor will receive from it, discounted at the market rate (never the stated rate, since the market rate reflects what the payment stream is actually worth today). There are two pieces to add together: the stream of coupon payments (an annuity, since it's the same amount paid repeatedly), and the one-time face-value repayment at maturity (a single sum).

Bond Price = PV of the coupon payments (as an annuity) + PV of the face value (as a single sum), both discounted at the market rate

A word of caution — "discount" means two different things here. This topic uses the word "discount" in two unrelated senses, and it's easy to mix them up. (1) As a verb, "to discount a cash flow" just means converting a future dollar amount into its present-day equivalent — every present-value calculation on this page is "discounting" something, whether the bond ends up selling above or below face value. (2) As a noun/adjective describing the bond's price, "a discount" specifically means the bond sold below face value (the opposite of a premium). So a bond can be "discounted" (verb, always true) without necessarily selling "at a discount" (noun, only true when stated rate < market rate). Keep those two uses separate as you read on.

Worked example: a company issues a bond with a $1,000,000 face value, a 6% stated annual rate (paid semiannually, so $30,000 every six months), maturing in 5 years. The market rate for equally risky bonds right now is 8% annually. Since payments are semiannual, the periodic rate is 8%÷2 = 4%, over 5×2 = 10 periods.

Using a financial calculator or Excel's PV function with those inputs gives a price of $918,891. Since the stated rate (6%) is below the market rate (8%), this bond sells at a discount — exactly as the table above predicted.

Where $918,891 comes from, piece by piece (4% per period, 10 periods):

PieceCalculationPresent value
Coupons (annuity)$30,000 × 8.1109$243,327
Face value (single sum)$1,000,000 × 0.67556$675,564
Bond price$918,891

Solving for the other unknowns

The same price equation can be solved for whichever variable is missing: Excel's PV gives the price, RATE the market yield, NPER the number of periods, and PMT the payment. Homework asks for the price at several market rates, then labels each par, premium or discount: the lower the market rate, the higher the price.

The engine underneath bond and mortgage pricing: present value

Every bond price and mortgage payment calculation in this unit ultimately rests on one idea: money today is worth more than the same amount of money in the future, because money today can be invested and grow. Present value (PV) answers the question "what is a future payment (or stream of payments) worth right now?" and future value (FV) answers the reverse question.

FV = PV × (1 + i)n        PV = FV ÷ (1 + i)n
PV of an annuity = Payment × [1 − (1 + i)−n] ÷ i

where i is the interest rate per period (the annual rate divided by how many times per year it compounds or pays) and n is the total number of periods (years multiplied by payments per year). A bond's repeating coupon payments are priced using the annuity formula; its one-time face-value repayment at maturity is priced using the simple single-sum PV formula; and a mortgage payment is found by rearranging that same annuity formula to solve for the payment amount instead of the price.

Reading the notation: "(1 + i)n" just means "take (1 + i) and multiply it by itself n times" — the same thing compound interest does year after year. A negative exponent like "(1 + i)−n" simply means "1 divided by (1 + i)n" — it's the same number, just flipped upside down, which is exactly what you want when you're working backward from a future amount to today's value instead of forward. In practice, nobody works these out by hand: you'll plug the numbers into Excel's PV, FV, or PMT functions (or a financial calculator), the same way the bond and mortgage examples above did — what matters for the exam is knowing which formula fits which situation and correctly identifying i and n, not grinding through the exponents yourself.

Market value from a quoted price (10-K style)

A 10-K lists $300 million face of 7.125% bonds quoted at 131.03% of face, with an unchanged credit rating.

Market value = $300M × 1.3103 = $393.09M
Annual cash interest = $300M × 7.125% = $21.375M (unchanged)

Trading far above face tells you market yields are now well below 7.125% (rates fell). The issuer still pays the same coupon; only the market value moved. The balance sheet, meanwhile, carries the bonds at their (amortized) carrying amount, not the market value.

Adjusting a buyback-heavy company (Home Depot style)

When huge treasury stock shrinks equity, add treasury stock back to equity (and total assets) before computing leverage. Illustrative numbers: liabilities $50B, equity $2B, treasury stock $30B. Raw liabilities-to-equity = 50 ÷ 2 = 25.0; adjusted = 50 ÷ (2 + 30) = 1.56. Same company, same debt: the buybacks, not the borrowing, made the raw ratio look alarming.

Traps the exam sets

  • Recording a signed agreement to buy equipment next year (executory contract) or a planned expansion as a liability.
  • Booking warranty expense when repairs happen, or treating a repair as new expense. Expense is at sale; repairs cut the liability.
  • Using 360 days instead of 365 (or a full year instead of days/365) for note interest.
  • Forgetting the year-end accrual, or the semiannual ½ on mortgage and bond interest.
  • Computing mortgage interest on the original loan instead of the current balance.
  • Cash interest from the market rate or issue price. It is face × stated rate.
  • Crediting Bonds Payable for the cash received instead of face; getting premium/discount backwards.
  • Quick ratio numerator = total current assets (that is the current ratio); CFO-to-debt using only long-term debt.
  • Calling gain/loss on retirement or interest expense a balance sheet item, or a premium/discount an income statement item.

Vocabulary bank

Search or filter by category. Every definition here uses plain language first, with the formula or accounting term right alongside it.

Contingent liability
An obligation whose existence or amount is still uncertain — arising from something that's already happened, where payment is possible or probable but not yet a sure, fixed amount.
Contingencies & Warranties
Probable + estimable = record it
When a payout is likely AND the amount can be reasonably estimated, the liability and matching expense get recorded on the financial statements right now.
Contingencies & Warranties
Reasonably possible = disclose only
If a payout is possible but not probable (or is probable but can't be estimated), it's described in the footnotes but not given a dollar figure on the balance sheet.
Contingencies & Warranties
Remote = neither recorded nor disclosed
An unlikely obligation isn't mentioned in the financial statements at all.
Contingencies & Warranties
Definite (known) liability
A liability with no uncertainty attached — like a signed note with a stated dollar amount — isn't a "contingency" at all, and skips the probable/possible/remote analysis entirely; it just gets recorded outright.
Contingencies & Warranties
Warranty expense
Units Sold × Expected Repair Rate × Average Repair Cost — the full estimated cost of fixing everything sold this period, recorded all at once, at the time of sale.
Contingencies & Warranties
Warranty liability
Total Estimated Repair Cost − Repairs Already Performed — the running balance of what's still owed under warranty at a given point in time.
Contingencies & Warranties
Servicing a warranty claim
Doesn't create a new expense if the original estimate was reasonable — it just draws down the warranty liability that was already recorded at the time of sale.
Contingencies & Warranties
"No payment yet" ≠ "no expense yet"
Interest on a loan accrues continuously, day by day — a company must record interest expense for time that's already passed even if the actual cash payment isn't due until later.
Short-Term Notes
Simple interest formula
Interest = Principal × Annual Rate × (Days Outstanding ÷ 365).
Short-Term Notes
Interest Payable
A separate liability recorded at year-end for interest that has accrued on a loan but hasn't been paid in cash yet — it sits on the balance sheet alongside the loan's own Note Payable balance.
Short-Term Notes
A note that crosses a fiscal year-end
Needs an entry at issuance, an accrual entry at year-end (even with no cash movement), and a combined settlement entry at maturity that both records the remaining period's interest and pays off the prior year-end's Interest Payable.
Short-Term Notes
Installment note / mortgage
A loan repaid through a series of fixed payments, each split between interest (on the current balance) and principal (paying down what's owed).
Installment Notes & Mortgages
Interest is computed on the current balance
Not the original loan amount — as the balance shrinks with each payment, the interest charged on it shrinks too, so more of each fixed payment goes toward principal as the loan matures.
Installment Notes & Mortgages
Splitting a payment
Interest portion = Current Balance × Periodic Rate. Principal portion = Total Payment − Interest portion. New Balance = Old Balance − Principal portion.
Installment Notes & Mortgages
Periodic rate
The annual interest rate divided by how many payments happen per year — monthly payments ÷12, semiannual ÷2, quarterly ÷4. It's the actual rate that applies to a single payment period, not the full-year rate.
Installment Notes & Mortgages
Amortization schedule
A period-by-period table showing how each fixed payment splits into interest and principal, and how the remaining balance shrinks over the life of the loan.
Installment Notes & Mortgages
Current portion of long-term debt
Only the PRINCIPAL due within the next 12 months — found by looking one year ahead in the amortization schedule and summing that year's principal payments. Never the entire remaining balance.
Installment Notes & Mortgages
Bond
A structured loan where a company borrows from many investors at once, promising regular coupon (interest) payments plus repayment of the face value at maturity.
Bonds
Face value (par value)
The lump sum a bond promises to repay at maturity — the amount coupon payments are calculated from.
Bonds
Stated (coupon) rate
The fixed interest rate printed on the bond itself, used to calculate the dollar coupon payments — set once at issuance and never changes.
Bonds
Market rate
The return investors could currently get on an equally risky alternative investment — moves with the economy, unlike the fixed stated rate.
Bonds
Par, discount, or premium
Stated = market → sells at par. Stated < market → sells at a discount (below face). Stated > market → sells at a premium (above face).
Bonds
"Discount" has two different meanings here
As a verb ("discount a cash flow"), it means converting a future amount to its present-day value — this happens for every bond, always. As a noun ("sells at a discount"), it means the bond's price is below face value — this only happens when the stated rate is below the market rate. Don't confuse the two.
Bonds
Bond pricing formula
Price = PV of the coupon payments (as an annuity) + PV of the face value (as a single sum) — both discounted at the market rate, never the stated rate.
Bonds
Discount on Bonds Payable
A contra-liability account that reduces Bonds Payable down to the bond's actual issue price — it's not a separate debt, just an offset that shrinks toward zero as the bond approaches maturity.
Bonds
Carrying amount
A bond's current book value: face value plus any unamortized premium, or minus any unamortized discount.
Bonds
Effective interest method
Each period's interest expense = carrying amount × market rate per period — always recalculated from the bond's current, evolving carrying value, not its original issue price.
Bonds
Why interest expense ≠ the cash coupon
For a bond issued at a discount, interest expense (based on the market rate) is larger than the fixed cash coupon (based on the stated rate) — the gap is the discount amortizing away, gradually pushing carrying value up toward face value.
Bonds
Gain/loss on early retirement
Carrying Amount − Cash Paid to Retire. Paying less than the carrying amount produces a gain; paying more produces a loss.
Bonds
Credit rating
An independent estimate of a borrower's default risk (AAA down to CCC and below) — lower ratings mean higher risk, which means investors demand a higher required return.
Bonds
Total liabilities vs. interest-bearing debt
Total liabilities = everything owed (A/P, wages, taxes, loans, bonds, all of it). Interest-bearing debt = only the narrower slice that actually charges interest (notes, bonds, mortgages, leases).
Debt Ratios & PV
Liabilities-to-equity
Total Liabilities ÷ Total Stockholders' Equity, both measured at the same point in time (not averaged) — how much of the company is financed by everything it owes vs. owner investment.
Debt Ratios & PV
Debt-to-equity
Interest-Bearing Debt ÷ Total Stockholders' Equity — the same idea as liabilities-to-equity, but using only the interest-charging slice of liabilities.
Debt Ratios & PV
Times interest earned
EBIT ÷ Interest Expense — how comfortably a company's operating profit covers its interest bill.
Debt Ratios & PV
EBIT
Earnings Before Interest and Taxes — operating profit before financing costs and taxes are subtracted, used so a coverage ratio isn't distorted by interest already being removed from profit.
Debt Ratios & PV
Current ratio
Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities — a basic short-term liquidity check.
Debt Ratios & PV
Quick ratio
(Cash + Receivables) ÷ Current Liabilities — a stricter liquidity check that excludes inventory, which can be slow to convert to cash.
Debt Ratios & PV
Treasury stock distortion
Large stock buybacks shrink (or even make negative) stockholders' equity, artificially inflating debt-to-equity-style ratios. Analysts sometimes add treasury stock back to equity and assets for a cleaner comparison.
Debt Ratios & PV
Present value (PV) / future value (FV)
PV asks what a future payment is worth today; FV asks what today's money grows into by a future date. FV = PV×(1+i)^n. Rests on the idea that money today is worth more than the same amount later, since it can be invested and grow.
Debt Ratios & PV
Annuity
A series of equal, repeating payments — a bond's coupon payments and a mortgage's fixed payments are both annuities, priced with the PV-annuity formula.
