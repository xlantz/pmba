Schein, E. H. (1968). "Organizational Socialization and the Profession of Management." Industrial Management Review, Winter 1968. Paraphrased study summary of the scanned text; wording of some OCR passages is garbled, so check numbers and names against your copy.

Why Schein cares

Schein began by noticing parallels between how prisoners were indoctrinated during the Korean War and what happens to graduates entering corporations. He followed panels of MIT Sloan School graduates into their early careers and also compared student groups at the school. Attitudes differed by group (Senior Executives, Sloan Fellows, Master's students, faculty) and shifted during school, but a study of Sloan Fellows after graduation found that most of the shift toward the faculty position had reversed within a year. His conclusion: attitudes and values change several times across a managerial career, and the process behind that deserves a name and attention.

Definition: organizational socialization

Organizational socialization is the process of "learning the ropes": being indoctrinated, trained and taught what matters in an organization or one of its subunits. Schein prefers "organizational" to the sociologists' "occupational" socialization to keep the focus on the setting where it happens. It recurs whenever someone enters school, takes a first job, moves to another department or rank, joins a new employer, or returns to school. The speed and effectiveness of socialization shape employee loyalty, commitment, productivity and turnover, so an organization's stability and effectiveness depend on how well it socializes newcomers.

Schein limits the term. It does not mean all learning; it covers only the values, norms and behavior patterns that the organization considers necessary for any new member to learn. He calls this the price of membership.

The five things a newcomer must learn

#What is learnedExample (invented)
1The organization's basic goalsA hospital's goal of patient safety before speed.
2The preferred means of reaching those goalsDecisions go through a weekly review meeting rather than hallway deals.
3The basic responsibilities of the role being grantedThe analyst owns month-end reconciliations.
4The behavior patterns needed to do the role wellFlag errors early and in writing.
5Rules that protect the organization's identity and integrityDo not criticize the firm in public (Schein's own examples include not criticizing the organization publicly and not wearing the wrong clothes).

Stage map: how socialization works

Schein describes a set of mechanisms. They fall naturally into the sequence below, which is this page's memory aid rather than Schein's formal model.

1. Unfreezing and "upending experiences"

If a newcomer already expects and accepts the organization's norms, socialization mostly reaffirms them through communication, the example of key people, and instructions from supervisors, trainers and informal coaches. If the newcomer's values are out of line, the first phase is a destructive or unfreezing phase. It detaches the person from former values and shows that the old self is inadequate in the organization's terms, so the person redefines himself or herself in the new role. At the extreme (religious novitiates, military basic training) the old self is symbolically destroyed by loss of clothes, names and titles. Schein argues that graduate schools and businesses use milder versions: a punishing schedule and reading load, problems too hard to solve, and statistics homework as a kind of head-shaving.

Upending experiences are deliberate or accidental circumstances that dramatically upset or disconfirm major assumptions a newcomer holds about self, company or job. Schein's panel members described assignments so easy or trivial that they signal "you are not worth anything important", assignments so hard that failure is certain, practice work, reports that nobody reads or acts on, and long periods of watching others work.

Case from the readingWhat happenedLesson
Engineering supervisor and the circuitA supervisor, wanting to deflate what he saw as unwarranted arrogance in new engineers, asked each to diagnose a complex circuit that broke several textbook principles but worked well. Each newcomer confidently said it could not work, even after being invited to double-check. The supervisor then showed that the company had sold it for years without complaints and asked the engineer to explain why it worked. None could, and all came away chastened and keen to learn what they lacked.Deliberate unfreezing: a failure that disconfirms "I already know it all" and makes the newcomer receptive. The manager said that afterwards a give-and-take relationship was easier.

2. The entrant's motivation and the organization's other holding forces

Unfreezing is unpleasant, so it works only if the newcomer is strongly motivated to join or the organization has other ways to make him or her endure it. Schein names two factors that the organization does not fully control: (1) the entrant's initial motivation to join (a fraternity pledge tolerates hell week; someone with low motivation may simply leave, which means socialization has failed) and (2) the extent to which the organization can make newcomers stay through the period (legal obligations in basic training, tuition paid in advance, vows, fear of losing face, and in business pension, stock-option or housing plans, plus the boss's reminder that it takes a year to learn a business and leaving means starting over). Whether these pressures work depends partly on the labor market.

3. The peer group of novices

Overload and threat lead newcomers to band together as a defense against the powerful organization and as a way to work out norms for what and how to study (Schein's medical school and Sloan examples). If the group's solutions support organizational norms, the peer group becomes an effective instrument of socialization; if not, it breeds counter-socialization (sabotage, rebellion). Because the gains usually outweigh the risk, organizations tend to encourage peer group formation. Schein's striking example: Sloan Fellows said that before the program they identified mainly with their company, and afterward with the other Sloan Fellows, for the rest of their careers.

4. Learning from multiple sources

Given readiness to learn, newcomers acquire the new values from five kinds of sources: official literature, the example of key models, direct instruction from a trainer, coach or boss, the example of longer-serving peers ("big brothers"), and the rewards and punishments that follow their own experiments with new behavior. Instructions from senior members are among the most potent. Schein illustrates with three incidents from his own start at Sloan in 1956.

Schein's Sloan incidentWhat he was told or refusedValue he absorbed
1. Interview with Elting MorisonBluntly: if you know what you want and can go ahead alone, Sloan is a great place; if you will look to others for guidance, do not bother.Independence.
2. Conversation with Dean Penn BrooksEach faculty member should find his own approach to management education; do not imitate the sister school up the river (the message: case-discussion leaders need not apply).Develop your own approach.
3. Doug McGregor and the course outlinesSchein asked to borrow outlines of past versions of the course; McGregor politely but firmly refused to give outlines or advice and trusted him to make up his own mind.Do not rely on history; Schein jokes that he can no longer be made to coordinate anything with anybody.

Subtle values (how the boss wants things done, what counts as heroic, what is taboo) often travel through peers acting as helpful big brothers. Note a catch: if the immediate work group's values differ from the organization's, the newcomer learns the group's values much faster, which Schein says is especially clear for hourly workers, where fellow employees have more socializing power than the boss. His example: a graduate hired into a cost-reduction group was shown by colleagues how to pad his expense account on trips; he accepted neither the company's cost-reduction values nor the peers' cost-inflating ones, and left to start his own business.

5. Building commitment and loyalty

MechanismHow it worksSchein's example
Invest in the newcomer (guilt)Spend effort and time on the person so that loyalty, hard work and fast learning are expected in return; he would feel guilty not repaying.A public relations firm told a graduate that his values would be evaluated for a couple of years, and he was often invited to lunch with senior people. Flattered, he worked hard to learn their values and felt he would be guilty if he left. Training programs, perquisites and paternalism work the same way.
Small behavioral commitmentsGet the newcomer to make a series of small commitments that can be justified only by accepting company values, so he becomes his own agent of socialization.Coercive persuasion: public confessions bound prisoners, who then justified them to themselves. In business, promoting a rebel into a position with subordinates changes how the same values look; one panel member said his ethical standards shifted so gradually over five years that he barely noticed.
Very general idealsWin acceptance of ideals such as "work for the good of the company" or "meet the competition", then cite them whenever behavior strays.An engineer who is late is told he lacks concern for the company; a panel member who found a possibly harmful, FDA-approved additive was told to forget it because worrying could raise prices.
"Learn from the ground up"The ideal that you must learn how the company really works before getting real responsibility justifies menial work and training; Schein notes elements of hazing."I had to do it when I joined, now it is someone else's turn."

6. Transition to full member

The last mechanism helps the newcomer fold the new values into identity, so they are not just lip service. Organizations signal acceptance by giving important responsibility (or power that could hurt the organization if misused), titles, status symbols, extra rights, and shared confidential information. The newcomer feels it when he is finally "identified with the company".

Example (invented). A junior consultant spends three months on photocopying and unread status reports (upending), bonds with fellow new hires over the pressure (peer group), learns from a senior colleague what the partners really care about (big brother), is invited to lunches with partners (investment), and is then handed a client call and a firm email address with client files (transition to full member).

Three responses: rebellion, creative individualism, conformity

Schein says organizations attach different importance to different norms:

Type of normMeaningExamples in the reading
PivotalNot accepting it means you will not survive as a member.Belief in getting the job done; reasonable profit; free enterprise and competition; hierarchy of authority.
RelevantNot strictly required, but considered desirable and good to accept.Standards of dress and decorum, not being publicly disloyal, living in the right neighborhood, belonging to the right clubs.
PeripheralLittle importance to the organization; rewards and punishments are small.Schein's IBM example: white-shirt norms may be pivotal in some parts, relevant in others and peripheral elsewhere. Organizations differ.

Rewards and punishments scale with a norm's importance, which gives the newcomer some freedom in how far to conform and the organization some freedom in how much conformity to demand.

ResponseWhat the newcomer doesOutcome for the organization
Type 1: RebellionRejects all values and norms.Expelled, or turns his energy to defeating the organization's goals. A socialization failure.
Type 2: Creative individualismAccepts only the pivotal values and norms, rejects the rest.The response most organizations should want. Hard to keep up, because promotions and transfers bring fresh resocialization (the engineer in sales, the staff person in production).
Type 3: ConformityAccepts all values and norms, even becoming a "carbon copy" or caricature of others.Curbs creativity and moves the organization toward sterile bureaucracy. Also a socialization failure.

Example (invented). A new product manager at a retailer fully accepts "never ship an unsafe product" (pivotal), follows the unwritten dress norm (relevant) mostly because it costs nothing, and politely ignores the tradition of eating lunch at the desk (peripheral). Rebellion would be rejecting even the safety rule; conformity would be copying every habit of her boss, including the peripheral ones.

Professional socialization versus organizational socialization

Schools socialize students toward a profession; organizations socialize new members to be effective members. Do the two supplement or conflict? Schein uses seven characteristics of a profession to ask whether management is one.

#Characteristic of a professionDoes management fit?
1Decisions rest on general principles independent of the case.Partly yes: general subjects about people, money and information are taught.
2Expertise in a specific area, not general wisdom.Left to the reader.
3Objective relations with clients, independent of sentiment.Difficult: no single client (stockholder, enterprise, customer can conflict) and decisions are not clearly sentiment-free.
4Status from accomplishment, not birth or connections.Industry is moving that way, but socialization may reward the conformist over the independent professional.
5Decisions on behalf of the client, free of self-interest.At best equivocal in manager-customer relations.
6Relates to a voluntary association of colleagues, accepting only their authority.Manager is least like a professional here: expected to accept hierarchical authority.
7Strong codes of ethics, enforced by peers.Few codes or enforcement systems for managers.

Schein's verdict: on several bases management is a profession; on several others it clearly is not yet. His crucial point is that graduate management education increasingly socializes students to professional values (general principles, long-range health of institutions, the corporation's view from the top, an ethic of rationality and emotional neutrality) that clash with what a first job expects: loyalty to one particular company, ad hoc local wisdom, evaluation by the boss rather than outside colleagues, and selling ideas from a low-power position. The organization's recruiters say they want fresh ideas, while the first boss and peers push the graduate into the traditional mold (examples: a graduate hired to introduce linear programming is told to hold back because success would make the old staff look bad; a financial analyst is denied data).

The numbers: of the panel who graduated in 1962, 73 percent had already left their first job; for 1963, 67 percent; for 1964, 50 percent. Schein reads this as many graduates being unwilling to be socialized into organizations whose values conflict with what the school teaches, even though those organizations may need creative individualists most.

What schools and companies can do

WhoSchein's suggestions
SchoolsMore apprenticeship experience early; a practicum on how to change organizations (teach students to be change agents who can influence from low power without giving up professional values and who stay creative individualists); closer involvement in career planning and placement, since a bad first job undermines the education.
Companies(1) Become aware of and understand their own socialization practices, and focus on pivotal values only, not peripheral or irrelevant ones. (2) Train the first boss: most company effort goes into training the graduate, yet the boss has the power to create the climate that leads to rebellion, conformity or creative individualism. Too many bosses push peripheral values and undermine creative individualism and improvement.

Schein's closing point: understanding and controlling socialization forces is a managerial responsibility, so a bit of "social engineering" in this area should not be shunned. He also says both schools and organizations have a right (the schools an obligation) to pursue their own socialization, and neither should be blamed.

Thomas, J. G. & Griffin, R. W. (1989). "The Power of Social Information in the Workplace." Organizational Dynamics, Autumn 1989. Paraphrased study summary of the scanned text; the scanned Figure 13.1 is only partly legible.

The puzzle: John Rogers, Adams and Woods

The article opens with a new vice-president of marketing, John Rogers, who meets two district sales managers, Bill Adams and Dick Woods. They share a background (classmates, same employer from graduation, similar promotions, comparable territories). Woods calls the company fair and the work challenging, exciting and worthwhile. Adams says the company is "ripping off" everybody and the work is a bore. How can the same job be seen so differently? The authors' answer: people respond to their jobs as they perceive them, and those perceptions are influenced by information from others, not only by the objective job. Managers who assume everyone reacts to a job description in the same way (the quoted executive-program client) are missing this.

Origin and definition

The line of research called social information processing comes from Salancik and Pfeffer (1978), who reopened the question of how objective job evaluations are. It argues that people adopt attitudes, behaviors and beliefs in light of social information provided by others. Early support: O'Reilly and Caldwell found that informational cues affected reported task characteristics more than objective task dimensions did; a study of public health nurses with identical objective jobs found that perceptions were influenced by family and professional orientations; and Thomas found that the most relevant source varied by job aspect (family helped nurses see the significance of their work, co-workers were most important for developing new job skills).

Social information means comments, observations and similar cues from people whose view of the job the employee considers relevant. It can come from people tied to the job (co-workers, supervisors, customers) or from outsiders (family and friends). Not everything is open to influence (a hot workplace stays hot whatever anyone says), but most perceived job characteristics are.

Four ways social information works

WayWhat it doesExample from the article
1. Importance (salience)Tells people which features of the job and organization others treat as important. Frequent talk raises perceived importance; rarely mentioned features fade.If the time clock or the temperature is a constant topic, workers notice it more. Miners: after a disaster at a neighboring mine, miners return to work with little visible fear because they want to believe the company and co-workers that an accident is unlikely, while outsiders see the danger as huge.
2. EvaluationOthers' views reveal how they judge the job; organizations transmit a culture (rituals, stories, symbols, language) that shapes evaluation.Military boot camp: recruits grow more accepting of authority and more committed to military values. Orientation programs aim at similar ends, with milder change. A family member who keeps reminding a miner of the dangers pushes the miner to reassess risk.
3. BehaviorOnce attitudes form, social cues suggest how to evaluate options and choose appropriate behaviors.The article describes this step in a sentence and gives no separate story.
4. JustificationSocial cues shape the explanations or rationales for behavior. When an employee's view conflicts with the information received, the employee may try to persuade the source, rationalize the difference away, or change their own perception.The Asch experiment (below).

The article's body has section headings for importance of characteristics, evaluation of job characteristics and justification of workers' perceptions; the behavior step is described in its overview paragraph.

The Asch line experiment: the numbers

In the Asch experiment, subjects judged the length of lines. When they worked alone, less than 1% misjudged. When co-workers (actually confederates) gave wrong answers, more than one-third of subjects gave the same wrong answer. If people shift their view of something as objective as line length, the authors reason, they are probably even more willing to shift views of less objective parts of a job.

Sources of social information

Information can come from anywhere (the article jokes: from bathroom graffiti to the evening news), but the likeliest sources are people met regularly.

SourceWhy it mattersRogers story link
Co-workersSpend hours with the employee and know the work, so their views on characteristics, importance and evaluation are credible; daily contact reinforces them.Co-workers told Rogers that other firms offered better benefits and advancement, so most saw the job as a stepping stone; Adams matched this view, and Rogers started to agree.
SupervisorsA dual role: management's representative (presenting the organization's view of the job) and sometimes a friend or peer in informal settings. "Company" employees may accept the supervisor's view without questioning it.Woods was a personal friend of his division manager, who kept telling him that loyalty, seniority and good performance lead to advancement, with less pressure and better security.
Friends and familyOutsiders less influenced by the company, so seen as more objective (or, alternatively, written off as naive). They offer an outside reference point (jobs elsewhere), support, a way to vent, and help with rationales, especially for new employees.Woods's family were blue-collar and he was the first to earn a degree and become a manager; a quick promotion would mean moving. He was content with slow promotion.
Customers or clientsEvaluate the company and product, which becomes part of the culture; poor customer reception raises cost and quality pressure and job-security worries; full-service cultures treat customers as an important source.The article's quip about a place that "would not be bad to work at if it were not for the customers".

Guidelines for managers

GuidelineWhat the article says
Employee experienceNewcomers (new hires, transfers and promoted employees) are more receptive to social information than people with seniority in the position, and spend a lot of time listening to many views. The number of sources used falls with experience (much of what some provide is learned quickly), although reliance on a few selected sources may increase, and those credible few gain influence.
Managing sourcesManagers can do little about family and friends or customers, but should know that those sources limit their options and that employees may know more about other firms than supervisors do. Discussing what employees hear lets managers respond and learn. Choosing the co-workers a newcomer meets can shape first impressions (a supportive group yields a more favorable initial attitude), but it is only temporary and, if later criticism seems more credible, management may look manipulative. Supervisors are the social information managers control most: for many employees the immediate supervisor is the organization, and even joking criticism can spread negativity about top management.
Griffin's field experiment (1983)Managers trained to give positive social or informational cues changed employees' perceptions of task variety, autonomy, feedback and friendship opportunities, compared with a control group whose managers had no such training, without changing the job itself.
Objective job versus social informationBe careful interpreting surveys and interviews. If responses come from the objective job, changing the job should change attitudes; if they come from social information, changing the job will have little effect as long as the social information stays the same.

Example (invented). A bank redesigns tellers' jobs to add variety, yet survey scores barely move. The branch's veteran tellers keep telling newcomers that "management does not listen anyway" (social information). The fix has to include what supervisors and co-workers say, not only the task design.

Tomaskovic-Devey, D. & Orellana, R. (HBR.org, May 12, 2022). Paraphrased summary. A fresh PDF export was compared with the earlier text: the same ten lessons and closing frame, and no figures, charts or statistics, so the diagrams below remain concept maps.

The big idea

Employers are fighting a hiring crunch and a rising risk that newly trained hires leave for better pay. The authors interviewed workforce development professionals (people who help employers find workers and young adults find employers) about what makes hires last. Their focus was young workers in core production tasks (factories, health care, administrative services) at entry level. Their conclusion: raising wages, offering full-time benefits and signing bonuses is essential, but what matters more for getting young workers to stay is the social side of hiring, especially building mutual respect and trust. This is a particular challenge for workers of color, who often expect discrimination.

The ten lessons, grouped by when they matter

The article lists ten numbered lessons. They are easiest to remember by timeline: before the hire, day one, the first weeks, and the ongoing climate.

#PracticeCore point (paraphrased)
1Create career jobsA good job is more than paying a bit over minimum wage. A career job pays a living wage, has predictable hours, shows visible skill and wage progression, and above all fosters respectful relationships with supervisors and coworkers. Bad jobs signal the employer does not care whether you stay.
2Communicate career progressionYoung workers may have had several dead-end jobs, so a "training period" can feel like more of the same. If you see the hire as the start of a long-term relationship, say so from the start.
3Build relationships before hiringHelp candidates picture themselves in the workplace: mock interviews, tours, job shadowing. Caution: if everyone shown is white or male, tours, websites and videos can signal "you do not belong".
4Positive first-day receptionDo not assume new workers will just figure it out. If nobody expects them, it signals the employer does not care. First impressions are crucial for retention; introduce them to coworkers, supervisors, support staff and the boss.
5Assign a mentorNew hires must learn job skills and the informal culture. Left to chance, some get lucky, others struggle and get labelled lazy or dumb. Mentors give information and social integration, and matter especially for young workers of color who may be ignored until they "prove" themselves. Many firms mentor managers but leave lower-level onboarding to chance.
6Explain expectations clearlyFormal and informal rules may seem obvious to supervisors but arbitrary to young workers (the article's example is cell phone rules). Explain the rules and why they make sense, or you sound like a coercive parent saying "just do it".
7A culture where questions are normalYoung workers fear failure and hide their need for help, especially where there is disrespect and impatience. People learn faster when asking is normal and safe.
8Understand non-work livesChildren, commutes on public transport, school schedules and appointments shape availability. Learning this prevents mistaking complex lives for bad work habits.
9Respect and dignity for everyoneDo not excuse "equal opportunity bullies" or toxic racist or sexist behavior. Tolerating disrespect lowers morale and productivity and encourages turnover.
10Racially equitable workplaceA color-blind approach is an insult to people with lived experience of discrimination. Check pay, shifts, hours and job-assignment gaps by race and gender, and build stable, respectful relationships across backgrounds.

Example (invented). A distribution center hires a 19-year-old on Monday, but the shift lead was never told. The new hire stands at the door for ten minutes and is then handed a vest with no introductions (breaks practice 4). The employer sees an HR-supervisor miscommunication; the new hire concludes nobody cares. A better version: the lead meets them, introduces the team, assigns a buddy for week one (practice 5), and explains why phones stay in lockers on the floor (practice 6).

The closing frame

The authors suggest treating the first few weeks as a probationary period for both sides. The employer wonders whether the hire will adapt to the workplace's rhythms and expectations, while the hire is judging whether this will be a respectful, encouraging place to build a career. Successful onboarding therefore means communicating that you value a long-term relationship and that the workplace is welcoming and respectful.

There are no statistics in this article to memorize.

Where Chapter 10 sits

The Leadership Challenge (Kouzes & Posner, 7th ed., 2023) is built on five practices and ten commitments. The authors began in 1983 by asking people to describe a "Personal-Best Leadership Experience", and have collected thousands of cases from ordinary people leading teams, departments, schools and community groups. Chapter 10 covers Commitment 9, inside the practice Encourage the Heart.

ItemSummary
Commitment 9Recognize contributions by showing appreciation for individual excellence.
Its partner (Ch 11)Commitment 10: celebrate the values and victories by creating a spirit of community. Think of 9 as individual appreciation and 10 as collective celebration.
Section: Expect the BestThe idea, at framework level: leaders who clearly hold high expectations, and show they believe people can meet them, tend to see people rise to them. Expectations are a form of encouragement, so they come before the recognition itself. Secondary summaries of the chapter describe the idea as a Pygmalion-style effect: people tend to live up to the expectations a leader holds for them, so high expectations are not just a positive attitude, they shape results. Study-aid summaries list three habits: show you believe in people, be clear about goals and standards, and give regular feedback. (Habits from secondary summaries, not verified against the 7th-edition text.)
Section: Personalize RecognitionThe idea, at framework level: appreciation should fit the individual and feel genuine rather than generic or ceremonial. Know what the person values; tie the thanks to specific excellence. Summaries also stress making recognition personal and direct (for example a handwritten note rather than a mass email), because generic praise reads as ceremony. (From secondary summaries.)

Example (invented). A manager says a blanket "great job, team" in every meeting. Later she notices that a quiet analyst caught a billing error that saved a client relationship, writes a short note naming exactly what was caught and why it mattered, and delivers it privately because the analyst dislikes public praise. That is Commitment 9: individual excellence, specific, personalized.

Why it appears in an onboarding week

The course notes tie Chapter 10 to onboarding through recognition and belonging: a new hire is deciding whether the workplace is respectful and worth a career. Early, specific appreciation and clear belief in their ability are two ways a leader tells them it is.

What the quiz may probeWhich practice and commitment Chapter 10 is (Encourage the Heart, Commitment 9); its wording ("showing appreciation for individual excellence"); how it differs from Commitment 10 (celebration, community); and applying "personalize recognition" to a scenario. How the Week 4 pieces fit together

LinkHow they connect
Schein (unfreezing) + Orellana (first day)Schein says newcomers are often put through experiences that say "you have things to learn"; Orellana says a first day where nobody expects the hire says "we do not care". Both show that early experiences send a message about the newcomer's place. The difference in tone matters: Schein describes deliberate or accidental disconfirmation; Orellana advises employers to avoid unintended signals.
Schein (first boss) + Thomas & Griffin (supervisors)Schein says the first boss has the power to create the climate for rebellion, conformity or creative individualism and should be trained. Thomas & Griffin say that for many employees the immediate supervisor is the organization and is the source of social information managers control most. Same conclusion from two angles.
Schein (peer group, big brothers) + Thomas & Griffin (co-workers) + Orellana practice 5 (mentor)All three say newcomers learn informal culture from people nearby. Orellana's mentor is a way to make that source reliable instead of leaving it to chance, which matters because (Schein) a work group can teach values that clash with the organization's, and (T&G) co-workers are highly credible.
Thomas & Griffin (social information) + Wk 3 David (person perception)Both say perception is shaped by context and cues rather than facts alone. David's correspondence bias is blaming a person's character when the situation explains behavior; Orellana warns that struggling hires are called lazy or dumb when they simply lack mentoring, and that complex lives get mistaken for bad habits.
Orellana practice 3 + Wk 3 hiring biasesHiring does not end at the offer. Similarity bias at the interview and the signals sent by tours, websites and videos both shape who gets in and who feels they belong.
Schein (pivotal norms) + Ingram & Choi (values and culture)Ingram & Choi say culture is made of values, beliefs and the norms derived from them, that shared values help alignment (they report COOs with low alignment needed about a 40% raise to be as likely to stay), and that "cultural fit" should mean fit with organizationally relevant values, not similarity on irrelevant traits. That is close to Schein's advice to focus on pivotal values and not peripheral ones.
Schein (commitment) + Christensen (culture)Christensen, citing Schein, says culture forms when a group's ways of working keep succeeding until people follow them by instinct and assumption. Schein describes how organizations deliberately build the same commitment in each newcomer.
K&P Ch 10 (summary) + OrellanaBoth are about a newcomer deciding whether they matter. Specific, personal recognition and belief in people's ability do the same through the leader's behavior as a mentor and clear expectations do through structure.

Where they differ or pull apart

  • Scope and evidence. Schein is an essay based on panels of Sloan graduates and the school's own students; Thomas & Griffin synthesize research studies (Asch, nurses, a field experiment); Orellana draws on interviews with workforce professionals about entry-level production jobs. Only Schein and Thomas & Griffin contain numbers.
  • Tone toward unfreezing. Schein treats upending experiences as normal and even useful (and notes hazing-like elements); Orellana treats unwelcoming early experiences as a cause of turnover and tells employers to remove them.
  • Who the newcomer is. Schein is about management graduates meeting the organization's values; Orellana is about young, often non-white, entry-level workers who expect discrimination; Thomas & Griffin are about any employee.
  • Pay. Orellana says pay matters but social factors matter more for retention; Schein says money invested (pensions, stock plans) can hold people in. Both treat pay as important but not the whole story.
