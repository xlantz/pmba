Week 4: Onboarding & Culture

Good onboarding tells a new hire, in the first days and weeks, whether this is a respectful place to build a career. This page covers the ten onboarding practices from Tomaskovic-Devey and Orellana, Kouzes and Posner's Chapter 10 on recognition, a clearly marked list of what is still missing, and a general-background scaffold on culture and socialization.

Tomaskovic-Devey, D. & Orellana, R. (HBR.org, May 12, 2022). Paraphrased summary.

The big idea

Employers are fighting a hiring crunch and a rising risk that newly trained hires leave for better pay. The authors interviewed workforce development professionals (people who help employers find workers and young adults find employers) about what makes hires last. Their focus was young workers in core production tasks (factories, health care, administrative services) at entry level. Their conclusion: raising wages, offering full-time benefits and signing bonuses is essential, but what matters more for getting young workers to stay is the social side of hiring, especially building mutual respect and trust. This is a particular challenge for workers of color, who often expect discrimination.

The ten lessons, grouped by when they matter

The article lists ten numbered lessons. They are easiest to remember by timeline: before the hire, day one, the first weeks, and the ongoing climate.

#PracticeCore point (paraphrased)
1Create career jobsA good job is more than paying a bit over minimum wage. A career job pays a living wage, has predictable hours, shows visible skill and wage progression, and above all fosters respectful relationships with supervisors and coworkers. Bad jobs signal the employer does not care whether you stay.
2Communicate career progressionYoung workers may have had several dead-end jobs, so a "training period" can feel like more of the same. If you see the hire as the start of a long-term relationship, say so from the start.
3Build relationships before hiringHelp candidates picture themselves in the workplace: mock interviews, tours, job shadowing. Caution: if everyone shown is white or male, tours, websites and videos can signal "you do not belong".
4Positive first-day receptionDo not assume new workers will just figure it out. If nobody expects them, it signals the employer does not care. First impressions are crucial for retention; introduce them to coworkers, supervisors, support staff and the boss.
5Assign a mentorNew hires must learn job skills and the informal culture. Left to chance, some get lucky, others struggle and get labelled lazy or dumb. Mentors give information and social integration, and matter especially for young workers of color who may be ignored until they "prove" themselves. Many firms mentor managers but leave lower-level onboarding to chance.
6Explain expectations clearlyFormal and informal rules may seem obvious to supervisors but arbitrary to young workers (the article's example is cell phone rules). Explain the rules and why they make sense, or you sound like a coercive parent saying "just do it".
7A culture where questions are normalYoung workers fear failure and hide their need for help, especially where there is disrespect and impatience. People learn faster when asking is normal and safe.
8Understand non-work livesChildren, commutes on public transport, school schedules and appointments shape availability. Learning this prevents mistaking complex lives for bad work habits.
9Respect and dignity for everyoneDo not excuse "equal opportunity bullies" or toxic racist or sexist behavior. Tolerating disrespect lowers morale and productivity and encourages turnover.
10Racially equitable workplaceA color-blind approach is an insult to people with lived experience of discrimination. Check pay, shifts, hours and job-assignment gaps by race and gender, and build stable, respectful relationships across backgrounds.

Example (invented). A distribution center hires a 19-year-old on Monday, but the shift lead was never told. The new hire stands at the door for ten minutes and is then handed a vest with no introductions (breaks practice 4). The employer sees an HR-supervisor miscommunication; the new hire concludes nobody cares. A better version: the lead meets them, introduces the team, assigns a buddy for week one (practice 5), and explains why phones stay in lockers on the floor (practice 6).

The closing frame

The authors suggest treating the first few weeks as a probationary period for both sides. The employer wonders whether the hire will adapt to the workplace's rhythms and expectations, while the hire is judging whether this will be a respectful, encouraging place to build a career. Successful onboarding therefore means communicating that you value a long-term relationship and that the workplace is welcoming and respectful.

What the quiz may probeWages versus social factors (which one the authors say matters more for staying); name the practice a scenario violates (first day, mentor, explain the why, safe to ask questions, non-work lives); the "probationary period for both sides" idea; who the authors interviewed and which workers they focus on. There are no statistics in this article to memorize.

Where Chapter 10 sits

The Leadership Challenge (Kouzes & Posner, 7th ed., 2023) is built on five practices and ten commitments. The authors began in 1983 by asking people to describe a "Personal-Best Leadership Experience", and have collected thousands of cases from ordinary people leading teams, departments, schools and community groups. Chapter 10 covers Commitment 9, inside the practice Encourage the Heart.

ItemSummary
Commitment 9Recognize contributions by showing appreciation for individual excellence.
Its partner (Ch 11)Commitment 10: celebrate the values and victories by creating a spirit of community. Think of 9 as individual appreciation and 10 as collective celebration.
Section: Expect the Best (earlier editions)The idea, at framework level: leaders who clearly hold high expectations, and show they believe people can meet them, tend to see people rise to them. Expectations are a form of encouragement, so they come before the recognition itself.
Section: Personalize Recognition (earlier editions)The idea, at framework level: appreciation should fit the individual and feel genuine rather than generic or ceremonial. Know what the person values; tie the thanks to specific excellence.

Example (invented). A manager says a blanket "great job, team" in every meeting. Later she notices that a quiet analyst caught a billing error that saved a client relationship, writes a short note naming exactly what was caught and why it mattered, and delivers it privately because the analyst dislikes public praise. That is Commitment 9: individual excellence, specific, personalized.

Why it appears in an onboarding week

The course notes tie Chapter 10 to onboarding through recognition and belonging: a new hire is deciding whether the workplace is respectful and worth a career. Early, specific appreciation and clear belief in their ability are two ways a leader tells them it is.

Schein's three levels of organizational culture

LevelMeaningExample (invented)
ArtifactsVisible structures and behaviors: space, dress, rituals, language, how things look and run.An open-plan office where leaders have no private offices.
Espoused valuesThe stated strategies, goals and philosophies; what the organization says it stands for.A poster saying "Customers first".
Basic assumptionsDeep, taken-for-granted beliefs that drive behavior and are rarely questioned.Everyone assumes bad news should be softened before it reaches the boss.

A useful test: when espoused values and basic assumptions disagree, people follow the assumptions. This echoes the Week 1 theme of the gap between stated and lived values.

Organizational socialization in a few points

  • Socialization is the process by which newcomers learn the knowledge, skills, norms and relationships they need to function as insiders.
  • It happens whether or not the organization plans it: newcomers read signals from supervisors, peers and routines.
  • Organizations can shape it deliberately through orientation, training, mentors and clear communication of expectations, which is where the Orellana article's ten practices fit.
  • Well-known outcomes of good socialization include faster productivity, stronger commitment and lower early turnover.

Treat the points above as a vocabulary scaffold; replace them with the course's own definitions once you have the Schein and Thomas & Griffin texts.

How the Week 4 pieces fit together

LinkHow they connect
Orellana practices 4, 5, 6 + K&P Ch 10Both are about a newcomer deciding whether they matter. A good first day, a mentor and clear expectations tell people they are valued; specific recognition and belief in their ability do the same through the leader's behavior.
Orellana practice 6 (explain the why) + Week 1 valuesIngram & Choi say values alignment pays off (they report COOs with low alignment needed about a 40% raise to be as likely to stay as high-alignment COOs). Explaining why a rule exists is how stated values become understood values.
Orellana practices 5 and 8 + Week 3 David (correspondence bias)The article warns that struggling hires get labelled lazy or dumb, and that complex lives get mistaken for bad habits. That is the pattern of blaming a person's character instead of their situation, the bias David describes. Mentoring and learning about non-work lives counter it.
Orellana practice 3 + Week 3 hiringHiring does not end at the offer: pre-hire outreach and the signals sent by tours, websites and videos shape who feels they belong.
K&P Ch 10 + Weeks 1-2 K&P chaptersClarify values (Ch 2) and set the example (Ch 3) are Model the Way. Recognition (Ch 10) is how a leader reinforces the values they modeled.
Schein background + OrellanaOrellana's mentor and "informal culture" language fits the idea that much of culture is learned, not announced (general background; confirm with the Schein reading).

Where they differ

  • Scope. Orellana focuses on structural employer practices for entry-level production workers; K&P focus on a leader's personal behaviors in any role.
  • Evidence. Orellana draws on interviews with workforce professionals; K&P draw on thousands of personal-best leadership cases. Neither gives you formulas, so exam questions are about concepts and application.
  • Pay. Orellana says pay matters but social factors matter more for retention; K&P are about non-monetary appreciation. Both push against "just raise wages".
