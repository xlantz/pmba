Walk the material

Chapter 12, section by section

Marketing communications is how firms inform, persuade and remind consumers, directly or indirectly, about the products and brands they sell: the voice of the company and its brands. It shows how, why, by whom, where and when a product is used; links brands to people, places, events, experiences and feelings; builds brand equity by establishing the brand in memory; and, by strengthening loyalty, contributes to customer lifetime value.

Opening example: Dove’s Campaign for Real Beauty

Prompted by a global survey in which fewer than a quarter of women felt responsible for setting their own beauty standards and only 2% considered themselves beautiful, Dove (Ogilvy & Mather) showed “typical” women instead of models. The 2006 “Evolution” video mocked photo retouching; “Real Beauty Sketches” drew over 150 million views in under a year. Dove’s sales grew from $3 billion (2004) to over $6.5 billion (2023), and its 2024 “The Code” campaign promoted a pledge never to use AI in its advertising in place of real people.

The macromodel: nine elements

The micromodel: response hierarchies

SequenceWhen it fitsExample
Learn → feel → doHigh involvement, meaningful differentiationCar, house

Learn → do → feelLow involvement, little differentiationSalt, batteries

Whatever the order, buyers pass through awareness → knowledge → liking → preference → conviction → purchase. A campaign must raise the odds of each step: the right consumer, message, place and time; correct positioning (points of parity and difference); attention and comprehension; motivation; and actual purchase and use.

Exam angleList the nine macromodel elements by type; match a product to its response hierarchy (involvement × differentiation); order the six steps from awareness to purchase.

The process (Figure 12.2): set objectives → identify the target audience → craft the message → select the channels → develop the creative → measure effectiveness. A communication objective is a specific task and achievement level for a specific audience in a specific period.

FocusWhat it involvesDetail
Create awarenessAbility to recognize or recall the brand well enough to buyRecognition (easier; matters in-store: Stouffer’s red packages) vs. recall (matters outside the store). Primary demand (the need) vs. selective demand (the brand).
Build preferencesPersuade that the offering meets a relevant needNeeds are negatively oriented (problem removal, avoidance, incomplete satisfaction, depletion) or positively oriented (sensory, intellectual, social approval). Comparative ads (Dodge Ram vs. Ford F-150) work best when they engage thinking and feeling together. Reinforcement ads reassure current buyers.
Incite actionMotivate purchase or a purchase-related stepHard, because few people are in the market at once: in a given week only about 20% of adults plan to buy detergent, 2% a carpet cleaner, 0.25% a car.

Reading the consumer state (Figure 12.3)

Benchmarks: quantitative and temporal

An actionable objective has a focus, a quantitative benchmark (how much) and a temporal benchmark (by when), e.g. “Create awareness of the new Avengers movie among 40% of Gen Z before the premiere,” or “Raise the share who believe new Colgate whitens better from 10% to 40% in one year.”

Setting the budget

Spending ranges from about 50% of sales in cosmetics to 5% in industrial equipment. (“Half my advertising is wasted, but I don’t know which half”: John Wanamaker.)

MethodHow it worksVerdict
Objective-and-taskDefine objectives → tasks to reach them → cost of each; the sum is the budget. Spend until the marginal profit of a communication dollar equals that of other marketing dollars.Recommended
Competitive parityMatch competitors’ spendingCompetitors aren’t wiser; situations differ; doesn’t prevent ad wars
AffordableSpend what you think you can affordIgnores communication as an investment; unstable; over- or under-spends
Percentage of salesFixed % of current or expected salesEasy, but detached from the task

Seven budget factors: product life-cycle stage (new products need more), differentiation (less-differentiated categories need more), market share (high-share brands need less as a % of sales), message complexity (more repetition), reach (hard-to-reach audiences cost more), competitive communication (clutter), and available resources. Note: advertising is expensed in the year it is spent even though its benefits (brand equity) can last for years.

Exam angleClassify an objective as awareness, preference or action; recognition vs. recall; read Brand A/B; write an objective with focus, quantitative and temporal benchmarks; name the four budgeting methods and why objective-and-task is best.

The target audience can be potential buyers, current users, deciders, influencers, groups or the public, and it shapes what to say, how, when, where and to whom. A useful profile is by usage and loyalty: new to the category or current users? Loyal to us, to a competitor, or switching? Heavy or light?

The target audience can differ from the target market: cereal ads aimed at kids though adults buy; milk ads aimed at adults though kids drink. When a group decides, communications may target different members of the decision-making unit.

Covonia (chapter example)

With a smaller budget, the UK cough medicine used an AI “cough predictor” to find areas with outbreaks and trigger online ads there, reaching the right people at the right time with 95% accuracy. Penetration and share grew by double digits and seasonal sales rose despite lower spending.

Crafting the message

  • Marketers seek the “big idea”: it resonates rationally and emotionally, distinguishes the brand, and is flexible across media, markets and time.
  • A good ad focuses on one or two core selling propositions. Research which appeal works, then write a creative brief: key message, target audience, objectives (to do, to know, to believe), key benefits, support for the brand promise, media. (Compare the six-question brief in Unit 6.)
  • Explore many messages (own team, agency, or crowdsourcing): more options, better odds of an excellent one.
  • Appeals can be about performance (quality, economy, value) or extrinsic (contemporary, popular, traditional). Jersey Mike’s mixes rational (“fresh sliced, made to order”) and emotional appeals (its annual Day of Giving, when shops donate all sales to local charities).
Exam angleWhy the target audience can differ from the target market; what goes in a creative brief; one or two core selling propositions.

Companies allocate their budget across eight major modes, and firms in the same industry differ (Avon: personal selling; Revlon: advertising). Tools can substitute for each other, which is why they must be coordinated.

Paid, owned, earned and shared media

  • Paid: TV, magazine and display ads, paid search, sponsorships.
  • Owned: brochures, packaging, website, blog, the brand’s own social pages.
  • Earned: consumers, press or outsiders talk about the brand voluntarily (WOM, buzz, viral).
  • Shared: content the company posts on social channels (X, Instagram, Facebook, TikTok).

Example of a full mix: Volvo backs its “safety” point of difference with R&D and ads, and sponsors golf, the Volvo Ocean Race and cultural events to keep a contemporary point of parity. Cadbury’s 2022 UK “Secret Santa Postal Service” combined QR-code posters (over 280), a 30-second TV, radio and cinema ad, an online hub, pop-ups and donations, and increased sales by over 5% in a stagnant market.

AI in the communications mix

Virtual influencers (Lil Miquela for Burberry, Prada, Givenchy), though they may not bond as strongly as humans; AI smart carts showing personalized in-store ads; AI-inserted product placement (M&M’s added to a scene in Bosch); fan co-creation (Coca-Cola’s “Create Real Magic”); and experiences like WWF Germany’s AI paintings of climate change in 2100.

Exam angleName the eight modes and one distinguishing trait each; define paid, owned, earned and shared media; mobile is timely, pervasive and customized.

Choose the most cost-effective vehicles. 30 seconds of network TV can cost $30,000 on a new show, $120,000 on a popular prime-time show, or over $7 million at the Super Bowl. Planners compute cost per thousand persons reached (CPM), then adjust for audience quality (a baby-lotion ad has more value to young parents than to teens), attention probability, editorial quality (prestige and believability), and placement policies and extra services.

Reach, frequency, impact

TermMeaning
Reach (R)Number of different people or households exposed at least once in the period
Frequency (F)Average number of times a person or household is exposed in the period
Impact (I)Qualitative value of an exposure in a medium (a food ad in Bon Appétit vs. Fortune)
Exposures / GRPsE = R × F. Reaching 80% of homes 3 times on average = 240 GRPs. (300 GRPs is more weight, but you can’t tell its reach/frequency split.)
Weighted exposuresWE = R × F × I
The trade-off, worked (chapter numbers, checked)

A $1,000,000 budget at a $5 CPM buys 1,000,000 ÷ ($5/1,000) = 200,000,000 exposures. At a frequency of 10, that reaches 20,000,000 people. Higher-quality media at a $10 CPM gives 100,000,000 exposures, so only 10,000,000 people at the same frequency, unless you accept lower frequency.

  • Reach matters most for new products, extensions of known brands, infrequently bought brands, or an undefined target.
  • Frequency matters most with strong competitors, a complex story, high consumer resistance, or a frequent purchase cycle; the higher the forgetting rate, the more repetition.
Super Bowl math

The 2024 game drew 123.7 million viewers. At $7 million for 30 seconds, that is $7,000,000 ÷ 123,700,000 ≈ 5.7 cents per viewer, before the free PR and social reach (Tubi’s “Rabbit Hole” spot nearly doubled brand awareness and raised viewing time 38%).

Timing: four scheduling patterns

  • Continuity suits expanding markets, frequently bought items, tightly defined buyers. Concentration suits one selling season or holiday. Flighting suits limited funds, infrequent purchase cycles, seasonal items. Pulsing is the compromise; fans say audiences learn the message more thoroughly at lower cost.
  • Communication should be more continuous the higher the buyer turnover, purchase frequency and forgetting rate.
  • Macroscheduling follows seasons and the business cycle (follow, oppose or ignore the seasonal pattern); microscheduling spreads spend within a short period (concentrated, continuous, or intermittent).
  • Space: national buys, spot buys (a few markets, called areas of dominant influence), local buys. Disney matches ads to more than 2,000 audience segments across its platforms.

How you say it can matter more than what you say. If a communication fails, the wrong message was used or the right one was poorly expressed.

Rules of thumb from the research

  • Two-sided messages (mentioning shortcomings) can beat one-sided ones, especially with more educated audiences, audiences initially opposed, or when negative associations must be overcome.
  • Order: one-sided message to a non-captive audience, put the strongest argument first; captive audience, a climactic order may work better; two-sided to an opposed audience, start with the other side and end with your strongest point.
  • Fear appeals work best when not too strong, the source is credible, and the product believably relieves the fear. Messages persuade most when they moderately disagree with the audience’s beliefs.
  • “Borrowed interest” devices (babies, puppies, music, sex appeal) grab attention but can hurt comprehension, wear out fast, or overshadow the product.

The message source

Credibility rests on expertise, trustworthiness (friends over strangers; unpaid over paid) and likability (candor, humor, naturalness). Doctors are credible for drugs; Charles Schwab fronted “Talk to Chuck.” Snickers’ “You’re Not You When You’re Hungry” turned the celebrity idea around (Betty White in its Super Bowl launch) and drove sustained double-digit growth.

Celebrity endorsements
Work whenHigh recognition, high positive affect, high fit (the Q score measures familiarity and likability); credible or personifying a key attribute (Dennis Haysbert for State Farm)
MisfiresCeline Dion added neither glamour nor sales to Chrysler and was let go early from a $14 million deal; Ozzy Osbourne for “I Can’t Believe It’s Not Butter”
Risks and fixesFee demands, scandals, lost popularity; use several endorsers, create a brand character (Dos Equis’s “Most Interesting Man in the World”), or use real people (Dove)
CongruityIf a liked celebrity praises a disliked brand, attitudes move toward each other: the brand rises a little, the celebrity falls a little

Derek Rucker’s (Kellogg) ADPLAN framework helps a strategist review a storyboard or finished ad on six dimensions. Each can be measured empirically, so it also points to where testing is needed. It complements, rather than replaces, a good creative brief and a check of the ad’s purpose, media and budget.

DimensionQuestionChapter example
AttentionDoes it capture and hold interest?Skipped YouTube ads fail; Geico made unskippable five-second ads
DistinctionDoes it stand apart from other ads?Apple’s “1984” (aired once) vs. look-alike local car ads
PositioningRight category, strong benefit, a reason to believe?Old Spice as the most masculine body wash, proven by its spokesperson
LinkageWill people remember the brand and benefit, not just the ad?Ameriquest’s loved Super Bowl ads that nobody could link to the brand
AmplificationAre viewers’ thoughts positive or negative?Pepsi’s widely panned Kendall Jenner ad
Net equityDoes it fit the brand’s heritage and associations?BMW guards its high-performance equity
Exam angleName the six ADPLAN letters and diagnose an ad: a memorable ad with the wrong brand recalled fails linkage; a backlash fails amplification; an off-brand ad fails net equity.

Senior managers want outcomes and revenue; communication managers too often report inputs (clippings, ad counts) and expenses. Intermediate outputs include reach and frequency, recall and recognition scores, persuasion changes and CPM.

Supply sideDemand side
Measures media coverage: seconds the brand is visible on screen, column inches of press. Quantifiable, but ignores the message content. (Some PR people claim positive editorial coverage is worth 5–10 times equivalent advertising; sponsorship rarely gets that treatment.)Measures the effect on the audience: do they recognize or recall it, how often did they see it, what do they remember, how did they feel, and how did attitudes change? Plus behavior: who bought, liked it, talked about it.
  • Posttests judge a finished campaign: if the goal was to lift awareness from 20% to 50% and it reached 30%, the company is under-spending, the ads are poor, or something else was missed.
  • Pretests let ads be fixed before launch, but critics say ads can be designed to test well and still fail in market; use them for diagnosis, not as the only criterion (Nike does very little pretesting).
  • Communication effect (awareness, knowledge, preference) vs. sales effect. Sales impact can be estimated with the historical approach (correlating past sales with past spending) or with experiments; tracking studies (Kantar Millward Brown) follow brand health over time.

Sprite shows measurement in action: its first global platform, “Heat Happens” (2022), made it the fastest-growing FMCG brand worldwide that year per Kantar, adding 34 million households.

Exam angleSupply-side (coverage) vs. demand-side (audience effect) measurement; the share-of-voice chain; pretest pros and cons; historical vs. experimental sales-impact methods; diagnose a posttest shortfall.

Both end-of-chapter cases connect to the professor’s slides: Red Bull is the laddering example in Unit 6, and Levi’s is the differentiation example in Unit 1.

Red Bull: building a category with a non-traditional mix

  • Launched in Austria in 1987, creating the “energy drink” category. One slim 250 ml can priced up to 300% more per ounce than soft drinks, because a cola-level price would undercut belief in the benefit. Broad positioning: “Vitalizes body and mind.”
  • Market entry sequence: “premarketing” with events before launch → a seeding program in “in” clubs, bars and shops plus sampling at moments of fatigue (libraries, highway rest stops) and with opinion leaders → branded coolers to own the point of purchase → only then mass media. Word of mouth drove awareness early.
  • Consistent cartoon ads (“Red Bull gives you wiiings”) for three decades; more than 1,000 owned events a year (Flugtag); sports sponsorship became its largest marketing cost (over €1 billion in 2022); Red Bull Media House content.
  • Monster passed it in U.S. share in 2012 (35% vs. 30%) with many flavors at about half the price per ounce; Red Bull answered with Zero and fruit “Editions” and led again in 2023 (40% vs. 30%).

Levi’s: staying relevant for 170 years

  • Revenue reached $7.1 billion in 1996, then fell to $4.3 billion by 2001 as the brand “got lost in the middle” between cheap jeans (Old Navy, Target) and $300 premium denim (Diesel, 7 For All Mankind). (The Unit 1 slides quote $4.2B for 2003.)
  • Responses: the Signature sub-brand for mass retailers (2002), later Denizen; the “Go Forth” (2009) and “Live in Levi’s” (2014) campaigns. Revenue passed $5.7 billion in 2019 and topped $6 billion in 2022 and 2023, with direct-to-consumer sales near half of revenue by 2024.
Exam angleRed Bull’s staged entry (premarketing → seeding → point of purchase → mass media) and premium-price logic; Levi’s “stuck in the middle” problem and sub-brand response.

Check your creative brief against this chapter

  • Does it focus on one or two core selling propositions, and is the top rung of your ladder (Unit 6) the insight behind them?
  • Is the communication objective written with a focus, a quantitative benchmark and a temporal benchmark?
  • Is the appeal informational, transformational or both, and why does that fit your VOC segment?
  • Who is the message source, and is it expert, trustworthy and likable to that segment?
  • Run your ad concept through ADPLAN: attention, distinction, positioning, linkage, amplification, net equity.
