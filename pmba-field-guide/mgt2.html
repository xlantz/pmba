Building the team: contracts, psychological safety, and modern conditions

Unit 1 established what a real team is and why teams underperform by default. Unit 2 is the practical layer on top: how to actually launch a team with a written social contract, a deeper look at psychological safety straight from Amy Edmondson, how to diagnose a struggling team (virtual or not), how Hackman's conditions evolve for modern "4-D" teams, and the concrete mechanics of running one — team charters, conflict types, and resolution.

Unit 2, source by source

Riordan, C. M., & O'Brien, K. (2012, April 17). "For Great Teamwork, Start with a Social Contract." Harvard Business Review.

Big idea

This is the direct academic backbone for a Team Contract assignment. A social contract is an explicit, team-authored agreement laying out ground rules for how members will work together, decide, communicate, share information, and support each other. Norms already exist in every team whether stated or not — a social contract just makes the good ones explicit and gives the team a tool to override the bad ones.

Where social contracts come from

Rooted in social capital theory: information exchange and reciprocity between people, mutual trust and fairness, and shared values/expectations. The DaVita example ("village" culture, "One for all, and all for one!") shows a social contract operating at company scale, not just team scale.

How to build one — four starting questions

  • What expectations do team members have of each other?
  • What is working well within the team?
  • What is not working well?
  • What should the team keep doing, start doing, and stop doing?

What goes in one

  • Positive behaviors to encourage — e.g. be honest and transparent with no hidden agendas; help each other; have forums to discuss tough issues; cooperate rather than compete.
  • Negative behaviors to explicitly rule out — e.g. "Don't speak negatively about other employees in their absence."
  • Violation handling, established up front — how the team gives candid constructive feedback and knows when to bring in outside help.

Key conditions for a social contract to actually work

  • It cannot be mandated by the leader alone — all members must collectively form and buy into it.
  • The leader must model the behavior the contract calls for.
  • High-performing teams revisit and update their social contract over time.
  • Violations are linked in research to employee dissatisfaction, lower trust, and intent to leave.
Exam angleExpect questions asking you to design or critique a team's ground rules — anchor your answer in the "keep doing / start doing / stop doing" question set and the requirement that the whole team, not just the leader, authors and buys into the contract.

Gallo, A. (2023, February 15). "What Is Psychological Safety?" Harvard Business Review (interview with Amy Edmondson).

Big idea

A deep-dive follow-up to the psychological safety concept from Duhigg/Google (Unit 1) — same underlying construct, but straight from Edmondson herself, with a sharper definition, an actual measurement tool, concrete how-to tactics, and two common misconceptions worth knowing cold.

Team psychological safety: "A shared belief held by members of a team that it's OK to take risks, to express their ideas and concerns, to speak up with questions, and to admit mistakes — all without fear of negative consequences." Edmondson's shorthand: "felt permission for candor."

Origin story

Edmondson's PhD research on hospital teams expected that better teams would make fewer errors. Instead, better-rated teams reported more errors — because they felt safe admitting them.

Why "team," not individual

Psychological safety is a group-level, emergent property — not a personality trait. People on the same team tend to report similar levels of it, even though it's experienced individually.

Three benefits (mechanism, not just outcome)

  • Engagement/motivation — people feel their contributions matter and won't be punished for speaking up.
  • Better decision-making — more perspectives get voiced and considered.
  • Continuous learning — mistakes get shared and learned from instead of hidden.

A nuance Unit 1's Duhigg article didn't cover

Psychological safety matters more when work is discretionary — creative, novel, or truly collaborative — than when work is fully prescribed/routine. It also interacts with diversity: on teams with high psychological safety, expertise diversity was positively associated with performance.

Try it: Edmondson's 7-item survey

Rate a real team you're on using the tool below — it's the actual instrument from Edmondson's research, not a simplified version.

Caveat Edmondson herself gives: scores are relative to each person's own expectations, so what matters is the variance/pattern across a team, not treating any single score as an absolute number.

How to actively build it (four tactics)

  1. Make clear why voices matter — explicitly explain why you need input and how it affects outcomes.
  2. Admit your own fallibility — a leader modeling vulnerability normalizes it for everyone.
  3. Actively invite input — don't assume people will volunteer it.
  4. Respond productively — replace blame with curiosity. Quote worth remembering: "If team members sense that you're trying to blame them for something, you become their saber-toothed tiger."

Two myths to know cold

  • Myth: "It's all about being nice." Reality: polite workplaces can have zero psychological safety if politeness suppresses candor.
  • Myth: "You must feel comfortable." Reality: it's about taking risks safely, not never feeling uncomfortable. Edmondson: "Candor is hard but non-candor is worse."
Exam angleIf asked to diagnose whether a team has psychological safety, use the 7-item survey framework rather than a vague gut read. If asked to build it, cite the four tactics rather than just saying "be supportive."

Mortensen, M., & Hadley, C. N. (2020, May 22). "How to Prepare Your Virtual Teams for the Long Haul." Harvard Business Review.

Big idea

A practical three-stage model — triage, stabilization, long-term care — for diagnosing and fixing virtual/hybrid team problems, plus a cross-team (not just within-team) lens that's new to this unit.

The "recipe for team disasters" (five ingredients)

Unclear missions, inconsistent social norms, low common identity, unclear roles, and unstable membership. Any team — virtual or not — showing several of these is at risk.

Case examples

  • Global executives survey (pre-pandemic): 76% reported difficulty feeling connected to teammates across multiple teams.
  • Medical device manufacturer: responded to a business shock by categorizing every team project as keep/accelerate/disband/start.
  • Key stat: the team launch period accounts for up to one-third of a team's ultimate success — reinforcing Hackman's "first few minutes" point from Unit 1, now with a magnitude attached.
Exam angleGood source for scenario questions describing an organization (not just one team) under strain — structure your answer as triage → stabilization → long-term care rather than jumping straight to a fix.

Haas, M., & Mortensen, M. (2016, June). "The Secrets of Great Teamwork." Harvard Business Review.

Big idea

Explicitly updates Hackman's classic five conditions (Unit 1) for "4-D teams" — diverse, dispersed, digital, and dynamic (frequent membership changes). Three of Hackman's original conditions still hold and matter more than ever; 4-D teams are newly vulnerable to two problems — "us vs. them" thinking and incomplete information — requiring a fourth condition: shared mindset.

The four conditions in this article's terms

  1. Compelling direction — challenging but not dispiriting, and consequential. 4-D risk: far-flung members can silently hold different interpretations of the same stated goal.
  2. Strong structure — right mix/number of members, well-designed tasks (autonomy, ownership, real feedback), clear norms. Bigger is not better.
  3. Supportive context — reward systems, information access, training, resources. 4-D risk: resources vary drastically by location.
  4. Shared mindset (new) — counters "us vs. them" subgrouping and incomplete information. Built via structured unstructured time (e.g., the first 10 minutes of a meeting reserved for open conversation) and deliberately building bridges between subgroups.

Case examples

  • World Bank teams: blending "cosmopolitan" members (multi-country experience) with "local" members (deep on-the-ground knowledge) beat either type alone.
  • Santa Clara/Bangalore software team: giving Bangalore developers ownership of a whole module (instead of disconnected overnight handoffs) fixed motivation and quality.
  • Takeda Pharmaceuticals: a "share the pain" policy alternating late-night calls didn't actually share the pain equally, due to unstated cultural/logistical differences — a vivid incomplete-information example.

Evaluating a team: 3 classic criteria + 4 conditions

Rate a team on Hackman's three effectiveness criteria — output, collaborative ability, individual development — then check which of the four conditions is weakest, since weak conditions and weak outcomes are usually linked.

Exam angleExpect the course to test whether you can map old-condition-name → new-condition-name and explain why "shared mindset" got added. Also good for diversity questions: diversity helps (World Bank) but also creates subgrouping risk (Takeda-style examples) — the resolution isn't "avoid diversity," it's "actively build shared mindset."

Barrett, D. (2013). Leadership Communication. McGraw-Hill Higher Education. Chapter 10.

Big idea

The most operational, how-to-actually-run-a-team source in the course. Restates Katzenbach & Smith's team basics as a foundation, then gives concrete mechanics for team launch (the team charter), document production, conflict types and resolution, and virtual team management.

The Team Charter — four parts

  1. Project purpose and goals — written down, specific/measurable, linked to purpose.
  2. Team member roles and responsibilities — leader, facilitator, timekeeper, note taker, plus a "meeting minder" and work-plan manager.
  3. Ground rules — see table below.
  4. Communication protocol — which channel for which message type, response-time norms, who speaks for the team externally.

Ground rule topics with example language

TopicExample ground rule
LeadershipOne leader for the overall project; topic leads run meetings on their own topics
ParticipationAll members participate equally and actively in all team activities
Conflict managementHealthy debate about content is encouraged; the facilitator addresses disruptive conflict
AttendanceAttendance required at all meetings; missing 3+ risks removal
Decision-makingDecisions by consensus; facilitator calls a vote at an impasse

Types of team conflict (genuinely new vocabulary this unit)

  1. Analytical — constructive disagreement over substance. Generally good if kept separate from personality.
  2. Task — someone not delivering a work product or missing a deadline. Often rooted in unclear expectations.
  3. Interpersonal — personality, cultural, or values differences.
  4. Roles — unclear or contested responsibilities, especially around what "the leader" is supposed to do.

Three approaches to resolving conflict

  • One-on-one — best for task-based conflict, less reliable for personality conflict.
  • Facilitation — a neutral third party mediates.
  • Team — the whole team addresses it, with a designated spokesperson, not group pile-on.

Virtual team specifics

Nonverbal cues carry an estimated 80–93% of face-to-face meaning — almost entirely lost over voice/text. Virtual teams need shared beliefs, a "storehouse of credibility and trust," and a shared workspace. Recommends discussing five things explicitly at a team's first meeting: position/responsibilities, past team experiences, personal expectations, personality/working style, and cultural differences.

Exam angleExtremely likely source for a case-based question: given a described team conflict, first classify it (analytical/task/interpersonal/roles), then recommend a resolution approach and justify the choice based on conflict type.

Key takeaways — Unit 2

Before the exam

Likely exam & discussion angles

  • Explain what makes a social contract actually work (whole-team authorship, leader modeling it, periodic revisiting) versus just posting a list of rules.
  • Reproduce Edmondson's psychological safety definition and explain "felt permission for candor" in your own words.
  • Correct a common misconception: does psychological safety mean a team is always comfortable and non-confrontational? Why not?
  • Diagnose an organization's team problems using triage → stabilization → long-term care, distinguishing within-team from across-team issues.
  • Map a described team's weaknesses onto Haas & Mortensen's four conditions, and explain what "shared mindset" adds beyond Hackman's original five.
  • Build a team charter for a given scenario using Barrett's four parts and the ground-rules table.
  • Given a described team dispute, classify the conflict type (analytical/task/interpersonal/roles) and justify a resolution approach.
  • Cross-unit synthesis: explain how a Team Contract assignment (Unit 1), the social contract concept, and the team charter are three names for closely related tools.
