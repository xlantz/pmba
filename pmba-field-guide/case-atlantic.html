Atlantic Computer: A Bundle of Pricing Options

A new product manager must price a server-plus-software bundle against a low-cost online rival, inside a company that prices on cost and gives software away. This page lays out the facts, the numbers, the people and the pricing frameworks.

Case brief only: the analysis and recommendation are yours (course policy: no AI for case analyses). This is the graded written case, due at the start of class on Tuesday 11/24.

Atlantic Computer, section by section

Who, what, when

Jason Jowers, the youngest product manager at Atlantic Computer, four months out of his MBA, is handed the pricing question for the Atlantic Bundle: the new low-end Tronn server sold together with PESA (Performance Enhancing Server Accelerator), a software tool that can make the Tronn run up to four times faster. The scene opens in Amberville, New York, in early October 2000. A management presentation is set for October 15, and the pricing structure is wanted before the SME (Small and Medium-Size Enterprise Systems Solutions) trade show in early November.

The decision facing Jowers (as the case frames it)

  • Compute a price for the Bundle for one sample customer, DayTraderJournal.com, under four named approaches, then pick one for that customer and explain how it would be implemented (pp. 5 to 6).
  • Sketch for sales director Cadena how a hardware-minded sales force could learn to charge for the tool instead of giving it away (p. 7).
  • Prepare a view on how Ontario Computer's Zink team might respond (p. 7).
  • A rough three-year revenue comparison across the four approaches is also mentioned (p. 6).

Two segments of the server market

Servers are shared computers that run software for many users at once. The case separates High Performance servers (complex applications such as supply chain, ERP, business intelligence) from Basic servers (simple, repeatable jobs such as showing a website's pages). Basic servers grew out of the Internet era: one customer can spread a website across several small servers. Matzer believed customers would not treat the two kinds as substitutes (p. 3).

Why this segment is different on price

The industry convention described in the case is cost-plus pricing for servers, and the widely held belief is that software tools (monitoring, performance) are simply part of the server and should be free (footnote 2). Application software (financial systems, CRM) is different: customers do pay for it, and it is licensed per server.

What the professor will probeWhich segment is the Bundle aimed at, how big and fast-growing is it, who leads it, and why is the 'tools are free' norm part of the pricing problem?

Atlantic Computer

Described as the largest player in the overall computer industry, in servers for 30 years, selling high-end Radia servers to large enterprises. It is known for reliable products and responsive post-sales support; its overall strategy is described as customer intimacy and product differentiation. The Server Division has focused on hardware, set server prices by cost-plus, and has historically given software tools away. Sales run mainly through a high-touch direct channel; third-party business partners are also mentioned for reaching small and medium customers (p. 5).

Ontario Computer (Zink line)

Low-end only. 50% revenue share of basic servers, Zink performs at about the same level as the Tronn, and most sales are online. Business model: operational excellence; it has pushed out non-value-adding costs and competes largely on price. Its CEO, Allen Capps, is quoted saying the aim is not to lead in product technology but to deliver leading technology through a flexible, innovative supply chain.

Product bundling: pure and mixed

Bundling means selling two or more products together at one price. Pure bundling: items are only available as the bundle. Mixed bundling: items are sold separately and also as a bundle (usually at a price below the sum of the parts). Ch 11 treats bundling under product-mix pricing; check your edition's wording. Why firms do it: raise sales of a weaker item alongside a strong one, simplify the purchase, or position the combination around value rather than parts.

Invented example (not the case): a coffee-machine maker sells Machine at $100 and Descaler kit at $40.

Cost-plus versus value-based pricing

Cost-plus: unit cost (direct, indirect and a share of fixed cost) times (1 + mark-up). Easy to compute; it starts from the seller's costs and ignores what the customer gets. Value-based (value-in-use): start from what the offer is worth to the customer compared with the next-best alternative, then set a price that shares that value. Footnote 3 of the case defines cost-plus; footnote 4 cites the 2006 HBR article (Anderson, Narus, van Rossum) and its 'value word equation' that converts performance differences into dollars.

Economic value to the customer (EVC)

EVC = price of the reference (next-best) alternative + value of differences (savings and benefits) that the new offer brings, minus any extra costs it adds. The price a seller can ask lies between the reference price and EVC; the split is the seller's choice, shaped by negotiation and strategy. Cost is a floor, not a basis.

Same invented example, in order: (1) identify the reference alternative ($3,000); (2) list differences in dollars: maintenance +$300, energy +$200, extra cleaning -$100, net +$400; (3) EVC = $3,400; (4) pick a share (40%), price = $3,000 + 0.40 x $400 = $3,160; (5) cost-plus with cost $2,200 and a 25% mark-up gives $2,750, a number that says nothing about value.

Price discrimination / differentiated pricing

Charging different customers or segments different prices for similar offers, based on willingness to pay. It requires distinguishable segments, limits on resale or leakage, and customers who see the offers as different enough. Ch 11 lists segment, product-form, image, location and time as common bases. Related case ideas: different applications benefit differently from PESA (Exhibit 2), and the case says the savings logic could be generalized to similar customers (p. 7).

Channel conflict and sales-force alignment

Channel conflict arises when sellers or channels pursue the same customers or have incentives that pull apart. The case does not describe an open conflict. Its relevant facts are: a direct sales force on 70/30 pay, third-party partners mentioned, a rival that sells mostly online, and a habit of giving software away. The generic concept to apply: what a person is paid and trained to do determines what they emphasize.

Other Ch 11 ideas the case touches

  • Steps in setting a price: objective, demand, costs, competitors, method, final price.
  • Pricing methods: mark-up, target-return, perceived-value, going-rate.
  • Reference prices and the habit that a product class has a 'normal' price (here, tools being free).
  • Initiating and responding to price changes and competitor reactions.

1. "What price should Jowers charge DayTraderJournal.com for the Atlantic Bundle (i.e., Tronn servers + PESA software tool)?"

Where to look: pp. 5 to 6 (four routes, footnotes 3 to 6); Exhibit 3 (p. 10); Exhibit 2 (p. 9); Exhibit 4 (p. 11).

  • Name the reference alternative and the server counts you compare, with reasons.
  • Show the price each of the four routes produces, with the formula and inputs for each.
  • Settle the annual-versus-one-time and per-server-versus-per-order questions and write them as assumptions.
  • Say which route you pick and why that fits the customer's ranked criteria (cost first).
  • Cover implementation issues the case flags, including breaking the 'tools are free' norm.

2. "Approximately how much money would be left on the table if Atlantic were to give away the software tool for free?"

Where to look: p. 6 (back-of-envelope revenue idea, three years); footnote 5 (shares 4%, 9%, 14%; 50% attach rate); Exhibit 1 (segment units).

  • Define the baseline you compare against (free) and the alternative (your price).
  • Build units: segment units x Atlantic share x attach rate, year by year.
  • Multiply by the per-unit amount from your chosen route; decide whether to discount or just sum three years.
  • State what 'approximately' means for your rounding, and what is excluded.

3. "How is Matzer likely to react to your recommendation?"

Where to look: pp. 1 to 4 (his statements, tool-pricing belief, cost-plus habit); p. 5 (conservative).

  • List what Matzer has said he values or believes, with page cites.
  • Say which part of your recommendation fits those views and which part does not.
  • Offer a way to address any gap (evidence, pilot, framing). Do not rely on guesses about personality beyond the case.

4. "How is Cadena's sales force likely to react to your recommendation?"

Where to look: p. 4 (giving tools away), p. 5 (70/30 pay, high-touch direct, third-party partners), p. 7 (role-play idea, hardware orientation).

  • Link pay structure and habits to the behavior your recommendation asks for.
  • Name specific enablers you would propose (training, sell sheets, incentives, scripts); connect each to the trade-show learning the case mentions.
  • Consider the third-party business partners as well as the direct force.

5. "How are customers in the target market likely to react to your recommended pricing strategy? What response can be provided to overcome any objections?"

Where to look: footnote 2 (tools seen as free); Exhibit 4; p. 7 ('We can save you money!' as a generic claim).

  • Identify the target segments and why (Exhibit 2).
  • List the objections the case suggests and how evidence could meet each.
  • Show how the savings story would be made specific and credible for the customer's own criteria.

6. "How is Ontario likely to react to your proposed pricing for the Atlantic Bundle?"

Where to look: p. 3 (50% share, online, operational excellence, CEO quote); Exhibit 3 (Zink price and cost); p. 7.

  • Describe Ontario's strategy, capabilities and cost position using case facts.
  • List its possible responses and what each would cost it.
  • Consider whether your pricing choice changes its options, and how Atlantic would respond in turn.
