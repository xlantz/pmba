From segmentation to positioning

Effective targeting has three steps: (1) segmentation — identify distinct groups of buyers with different needs; (2) targeting — pick which segment(s) to serve; (3) positioning (Chapter 7) — establish, communicate, and deliver the right benefit(s). Targeting itself splits into strategic targeting (which customers to serve, guided by target compatibility and target attractiveness) and tactical targeting (how to reach the strategically chosen customers, guided by effectiveness and cost efficiency). Opening case: Gillette used new lines (King C Gillette, GilletteLabs) to re-target bearded/high-end men without abandoning its core.

Mass, niche, and one-to-one

ApproachDefinition and example
Mass (undifferentiated) marketingOne offer for the whole market; ignores segment differences. Lowest costs (R&D, production, advertising) but vulnerable to market splintering. Classic example: Ford Model T, "any color as long as it's black."
Targeted (differentiated) marketingDifferent products for different segments. Estée Lauder: flagship brand for older consumers, Clinique/M·A·C for younger, Aveda for aromatherapy fans, Origins for eco-conscious buyers.
One-to-one marketingThe ultimate level: each segment = one customer. Works best when the firm collects lots of individual customer data, sells cross-sellable/repeat-purchase/high-value products. Example: Wagner Custom Skis (from $1,995, precisely fitted).
Mass customizationIndividually designed products/services on a mass basis. MINI configurator; Coke Freestyle (100+ flavors); politicians micro-targeting ads on Facebook.

Strategic targeting: two key questions

Strategic targeting requires a deliberate tradeoff: give up market breadth to better serve chosen customers. A manager must answer two questions: can the company create superior value for these customers? (answered by target compatibility) and can these customers create superior value for the company? (answered by target attractiveness).

Target compatibility reflects the company's ability to outdo competitors in fulfilling target-customer needs. Six resource types matter: business infrastructure (manufacturing, service, supply-chain, management); access to scarce resources (unique natural resources, prime locations, a memorable web domain); skilled employees (especially R&D, education, consulting); technological expertise (proprietary processes, patents, trade secrets); strong brands (create value beyond the product's attributes, especially in commoditized categories); and collaborator networks (vertical — suppliers/distributors — and horizontal — R&D/manufacturing/promotion partners). A core competency has three traits: it's a source of competitive advantage/significant customer benefit, it applies across many markets, and it's hard for competitors to imitate. Example: Athletic Brewing built target compatibility around flavor and brand appeal to crack the non-alcoholic beer market.

Target attractiveness: monetary and strategic value

Value typeComponents
Monetary valueCustomer revenues (market size/growth, buying power, loyalty, price sensitivity, competitive intensity) minus costs of serving them (tailoring, communicating, delivering, acquisition, retention, support).
Strategic value — socialInfluence on other buyers: opinion leaders, trendsetters, mavens who promote the offering via their networks.
Strategic value — scaleBenefits from the scale of operations; a company may target low-margin or unprofitable customers to build a user base (airlines, hotels; Uber, Airbnb, Instagram, TikTok in early growth).
Strategic value — informationThe worth of the data/feedback customers provide, including early-adopter "lead users" who help refine the offering before mass-market launch.

Tactical targeting: building the customer profile

Tactical targeting links the (unobservable) value-based segment to observable characteristics — the customer profile — so the company can actually reach people. Four profile categories:

Profile factorExamples
Demographic (firmographic for B2B)Age, gender, income, occupation, education, religion, ethnicity, employment status, social class, life-cycle stage. B2B equivalent: size, structure, industry, growth, revenue.
Geographic (geolocation)Physical location — permanent address (enduring) or real-time location (dynamic, mobile-driven).
BehavioralPrior experience (current/competitor's/new-to-category), purchase frequency/quantity, price and promotion sensitivity, loyalty, decision-journey stage, decision role (initiator/influencer/decider/buyer/user).
PsychographicPersonality, values, attitudes, interests, lifestyle — often inferred rather than directly observed (e.g., sports interest inferred from magazine subscriptions, club membership).

Two principles guide tactical targeting: effectiveness (reach all strategically viable customers) and cost efficiency (reach only those customers, minimizing waste). Example: a new travel-rewards credit card links customer value (wants travel benefits, pays on time) to observable profile traits (reads travel magazines, watches travel shows, has a strong credit score) to decide where and how to advertise.

Personas

Personas are detailed hypothetical-customer profiles (demographic, psychographic, behavioral) with photos/names/bios that help marketers keep a concrete target customer in mind. Example: Best Buy's Geek Squad used five personas ("Jill," "Charlie," "Daryl," "Luis," "Nick") to redesign its service. Caution: a persona is a representative individual, not the whole segment — companies often build multiple personas to capture real variation.

Exam angleDistinguish target compatibility (can we create value for them?) from target attractiveness (can they create value for us?). Many companies focus only on monetary value because it's easy to quantify — but a customer with near-zero direct revenue can still be highly attractive for social, scale, or information value. Also know that strategic targeting answers "who," while tactical targeting answers "how to reach them."

Single-segment vs. multi-segment strategies

StrategyDescription / example
Single-segment (concentrated) marketingServe one segment only. Deep expertise + operating economies, but concentrated risk. Porsche (sports-car enthusiasts); Enterprise (insurance-replacement rentals, an overlooked niche).
Selective specializationPick several unrelated-but-attractive segments — diversifies risk, little synergy needed. Crest Whitestrips initially targeted brides-to-be and gay men.
Product specializationSell one product to several segments (e.g., a microscope maker selling to university, government, and commercial labs). Risk: the product line could be disrupted by new technology.
Market specializationServe many needs of one customer group (e.g., selling an assortment only to university labs). Builds a strong reputation with that group. Example: Hallmark's many sub-lines (Mahogany, LGBTQ+, charity tie-ins) all serve card-buyers broadly.

What attractive niches look like

Distinct needs, willing to pay a premium, decent size/profit/growth potential, unlikely to attract competitors, and gains from specialization. Niche marketers win on margin; mass marketers win on volume.

Multi-segment pitfall: if a company builds offerings around its own production capability rather than each segment's actual needs, its offerings end up competing for the same customers while other segments go unserved, and customers get confused.

The Long Tail

Chris Anderson's Long Tail: lower distribution costs + better recommendation engines let companies profit from many low-volume niche products (the "tail"), shifting some markets from an 80/20 rule toward 50/50 — though critics note this doesn't apply everywhere (autos, aircraft still rely on mass-produced hits), and poor recommendation systems can leave long-tail items too obscure to sell.

Exam angleDistinguish the four multi-segment strategies by what varies and what stays fixed: selective specialization varies both product and segment (several unrelated bets); product specialization fixes the product, varies the segment; market specialization fixes the segment, varies the product. Also remember niche marketers win on margin, mass marketers win on volume.

Demographic segmentation

Popular because demographics correlate with needs and are easy to measure. Key variables and examples:

  • Generation/age: Silent Gen (1925–45), Baby Boomers (1946–64), Gen X (1965–81), Millennials (1982–96), Gen Z (1997–2012), Gen Alpha (2013–). Example: Centrum vs. Centrum Silver (50+).
  • Life stage (not the same as life-cycle stage!): divorce, remarriage, new home, elder care. Example: the $30,000 average U.S. wedding; Singles Day in China (11/11) targets single young adults — the world's largest shopping event.
  • Gender: traditionally communal (women) vs. self-expressive/goal-directed (men), though the gap is narrowing. Example: Gillette Venus razor engineered specifically for women's shaving needs; some firms now go gender-neutral (CK EVERYONE, Bic Made For YOU).
  • Income: doesn't always predict best customers (blue-collar workers were early color-TV buyers). Markets are increasingly hourglass-shaped — growth at both the discount and premium ends, with middle-market brands "trapped in the middle" (Levi's response: premium Made & Crafted line + budget Signature line).
  • Race and culture: multicultural marketing requires tailored messages/media, e.g., McDonald's "I'm Lovin' It" (hip-hop roots, broad appeal); Ford's Kevin Hart Explorer ad (Black-market-first, then general-market crossover).

Geographic segmentation

Divides the market into nations, regions, cities, or neighborhoods; can be combined with demographics for richer profiles (geoclustering, e.g., Claritas's PRIZM — 68 segments by income, education, occupation, home value, urbanization, age, kids at home). Geoclustering answers: which zip codes hold our best customers? How penetrated are they? Which channels/media work best there? Example: Yelp built its business on hyper-local reviews and ads.

Behavioral segmentation

VariableDetail
User statusNonusers, potential users, first-timers, regulars, ex-users. Potential users tied to a life stage (e.g., parents-to-be) are especially valuable.
Usage rateLight / medium / heavy. Heavy beer drinkers = 87% of consumption, ~7× light drinkers — but heavy users can be either fiercely loyal or price-driven brand-switchers, and have less room to grow purchases; light users may respond better to new appeals.
Buyer-readiness stageUnaware → aware → informed → interested → desirous → intends to buy. Marketing must match the mix of stages (e.g., a Pap-test awareness campaign shifts from simple awareness ads to benefit-dramatizing ads to a free-exam offer).
Loyalty statusHard-core loyal (one brand always), split-loyal (2–3 brands), shifting-loyalty, switchers (no loyalty).
OccasionsPurchase/use triggers differ (air travel: business vs. vacation vs. family; wine: drinking vs. cooking) — different occasions create different value.

Psychographic segmentation

Groups buyers by psychological traits, lifestyle, or values — useful because demographics don't always reflect underlying needs (Honda's Element: targeted 21-year-olds but attracted 42-year-old "psychologically young" Boomers, and the model was discontinued). The VALS framework classifies U.S. adults by motivation (ideals, achievement, or self-expression) and resources (personality traits + demographics). Psychographic segmentation is also used for the LGBTQ+ market (~7% of the population, ~$1T in buying power) — companies like American Airlines, Nike, and Target have built dedicated initiatives.

Segmenting business markets

B2B markets share some consumer variables (geography, benefits, usage rate) plus these business-specific ones:

CategoryExample questions
Demographic (firmographic)Which industries? Which company sizes? Which locations?
Operating variablesWhich technologies? Heavy/medium/light users or nonusers? How many services needed?
Purchasing approachesCentralized or decentralized buying? Engineering- or finance-dominated? Existing relationship or best-available deal? Leasing vs. buying? Quality/service/price priority?
Situational factorsNeed immediate delivery? One application or many? Large or small orders?
Personal characteristicsBuyer-seller similarity? Risk attitude? Supplier loyalty?

Example: Timken discovered some customers generated revenue but little profit; it shifted focus from autos to heavy-processing, aerospace, and defense, and even told a low-margin tractor maker to look elsewhere (while still serving its large-tractor line) — becoming a global bearings leader by segmenting on profitability, not just revenue.

Exam angleIdentify the four segmentation bases (demographic, geographic, behavioral, psychographic) from an example, and distinguish the five behavioral variables (user status, usage rate, buyer-readiness, loyalty status, occasions). Recognize a niche marketing scenario (small, high-margin, low competitive interest) vs. mass marketing (high-volume, low-margin).

The Ansoff growth matrix

Igor Ansoff's growth matrix crosses product (existing vs. new) with market (existing vs. new) to produce four growth strategies:

StrategyProduct × marketTypical tactics
Market penetrationExisting product, existing marketIncrease usage frequency/quantity, win competitors' customers, convert nonusers. Lowest risk.
Market developmentExisting product, new marketNew geography, new customer segment, or new use/application for the current offering.
Product developmentNew product, existing marketNew products or line extensions sold to the already-served customer base.
DiversificationNew product, new marketNeither existing product expertise nor existing relationships to lean on — the riskiest of the four.

Growth through mergers & acquisitions

Rather than growing organically, a company can acquire its way into a strategy. Three integration directions:

TypeDescription
Backward integrationAcquire a supplier — control costs, inputs, and supply reliability.
Forward integrationAcquire a distributor or other customer-facing channel — control how the product reaches customers.
Horizontal integrationAcquire a direct competitor — gain share, scale, or capabilities quickly.

Innovation vs. imitation

Not every growth strategy requires inventing something new. A company can grow by imitating a pioneer instead, at three levels of fidelity:

  • Cloner — copies the pioneer's offering and positioning almost exactly.
  • Imitator — copies some features but keeps differentiation in pricing, packaging, or promotion.
  • Adapter — takes the pioneer's product and adapts or improves it, often for a different market.

Imitation trades away first-mover advantages for lower R&D cost/risk, faster time to market, and the ability to learn from the pioneer's mistakes.

Exam angleGiven a scenario, identify which Ansoff cell applies by asking: is the product new or existing, and is the market new or existing? Also be ready to classify an integration move (backward/forward/horizontal) by which part of the value chain is being acquired.

Three types of market position

Position typeWhat it measures
Share of marketSales/volume share relative to competitors.
Share of mindTop-of-mind brand awareness — which brand customers think of first.
Share of heartPreference and loyalty — which brand customers say they'd most like to buy.

A company can grow or defend its position three ways: expand the total market (grow the pie for everyone), defend current share, or expand share by taking it directly from competitors.

Pioneer advantage

Being "first" actually breaks into three distinct roles, which need not be the same company: the inventor (first to develop the underlying technology), the product pioneer (first to turn it into a commercial product), and the market pioneer (first to successfully market it to customers at scale). Pioneer advantage — brand association with the category, customer switching costs, and a head start on the scale/learning curve — is real but not guaranteed: many pioneers are overtaken by fast followers who learn from the pioneer's mistakes.

Defense strategies (for the market leader)

StrategyDescription
Position defenseReinforce the current, dominant position directly.
Flanking defenseProtect weak sides before a competitor attacks there.
Pre-emptive defenseAttack first, before a competitor can establish a foothold.
Counteroffensive defenseStrike back directly after being attacked.
Mobile defenseExpand into new markets/categories to broaden the base being defended.
Contraction defenseGive up weaker territory to concentrate resources on the strongest positions (a planned retreat, not weakness).

Attack strategies (for the challenger)

StrategyDescription
Frontal attackMatch the leader head-on across the board.
Flank attackTarget the leader's weak points or underserved segments.
Encirclement attackAttack from multiple fronts simultaneously.
Bypass attackAvoid head-to-head competition; move into new markets/technologies/segments the leader hasn't addressed.
Guerrilla attackSmall, intermittent attacks (price cuts, promotions, legal challenges) to harass and weaken rather than win outright.

Responsive, anticipative, and creative marketing

Responsive marketing reacts to needs customers already express; anticipative marketing looks ahead to needs customers will soon have; creative marketing uncovers and offers solutions customers hadn't thought to ask for — the highest level of market leadership. Niche markets remain a growth-context option too: a defender or challenger can grow profitably in a small, specialized space rather than fighting for the mass market directly.

Exam angleMatch a described company action to the correct defense or attack strategy by name. Know that pioneer advantage is not automatic, and that the inventor/product pioneer/market pioneer roles can belong to three different companies.

The four stages

StageSalesProfitTypical focus
IntroductionLow, slow growthNegative or minimalBuild awareness; high launch/promotion cost.
GrowthRising rapidlyImprovingBuild distribution/share as volume spreads costs; competitors enter.
MaturitySlows, flattensPeaks, then may erodeDefend share; price/promotion competition; differentiation.
DeclineFallingFallingHarvest (minimize investment, maximize remaining cash) or divest (exit).

Three phases within maturity

Growth maturity (sales growth is slowing but still positive), stable maturity (sales flatten on a per-capita basis), and decaying maturity (absolute sales begin to decline). Causes of decline include new technology, shifting consumer tastes, and increased competition.

Alternative PLC patterns

PatternShape
Growth-slump-maturityRapid initial growth, a sharp drop, then a long, stable plateau.
Cycle-recycleA second growth bump, usually driven by a relaunch or renewed promotion.
Scalloped patternRepeated new-growth cycles, each sparked by new features, uses, or user groups.

Fads vs. trends

A fad is a short, often emotion-driven surge in popularity that fades quickly and unpredictably. A trend has durability and momentum, shaped by broader shifts in society, technology, or culture — and is far more useful to plan a sustained marketing strategy around.

Exam angleGiven a sales curve description, identify the PLC stage and the right strategic response (build awareness vs. defend share vs. harvest/divest). Distinguish the three alternative patterns from the standard S-curve, and distinguish a fad from a trend by durability, not just speed of rise.

Why innovation matters

New-offering failure rates run around 95% — yet innovation remains essential as markets, technology, and customer needs keep shifting. The risk of not innovating (irrelevance, disruption) typically outweighs the risk of a given new offering failing.

Organizational approaches to managing innovation

Companies rarely rely on a single mechanism. Common approaches include: a dedicated innovation department or lab; cross-functional project teams; internal innovation committees or idea tournaments/competitions; acquiring startups for their technology or talent; open innovation (partnering with outside firms, universities, or customers); and intrapreneurship (internal venture units that let employees pursue new ideas with some startup-like autonomy).

The Stage-Gate framework

A widely used five-stage process for developing new offerings, with a gate — a go/no-go decision — between each stage. Gates control risk: a weak idea is screened out (or sent back for more work) before the company commits more time and money to it. At each gate, three validation criteria are assessed:

CriterionQuestion it answers
DesirabilityDo customers actually want this?
FeasibilityCan we actually build and deliver it?
ViabilityCan we make money doing it?

Screening errors

Error typeDescription
Type 1 errorRejecting a good idea (false negative) — killing something that would have succeeded. Often invisible: a killed idea never gets a real market test, so the company may never learn what it lost.
Type 2 errorAccepting a bad idea (false positive) — advancing something that goes on to fail, usually visibly, after launch.
Exam angleBe ready to label a scenario as a Type 1 or Type 2 screening error, and to identify which of desirability/feasibility/viability a described failure violates.

Top-down vs. bottom-up idea generation

Top-down: leadership sets a strategic direction (e.g., "expand into wearables") and solicits ideas to fit it. Bottom-up: ideas originate from employees, customers, or partners and surface without top-down direction. Strong innovation programs usually draw on both.

Idea-generation research tools

Customer surveys and interviews, ethnographic/observational research (watching how people actually use products), focus groups, lead-user research (studying sophisticated customers who face needs ahead of the mass market), crowdsourcing, and mining complaints or support data for unmet needs.

Concept development & validation

ToolPurpose
PrototypeAn early physical or digital version used to test and refine a concept before committing to full-scale production.
Alpha testingInternal testing within the company (e.g., with employees) before any external release.
Beta testingTesting with real external customers under real-world conditions, ahead of full commercial launch.
A/B testingComparing two versions of an offering, message, or feature with real customers to see which performs better.
Conjoint analysisAsks customers to make tradeoffs among attributes, revealing how much each attribute (and level) contributes to overall preference.

Adoption: Rogers' categories and Moore's chasm

Everett Rogers' diffusion-of-innovation model sorts adopters into five groups, in order of adoption speed: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards. Geoffrey Moore's technology-adoption model highlights "the chasm" — a gap between early adopters (visionaries, comfortable with risk) and the early majority (pragmatists, who want proven value) — where many promising innovations stall and fail to reach the mainstream.

Exam angleMatch a testing method (alpha, beta, A/B, conjoint) to the scenario described. Place a described customer group correctly among Rogers' five categories, and explain why "the chasm" specifically sits between early adopters and the early majority.

Designing the business model

A new offering's business model should be designed using the same frameworks used for strategic planning in Chapter 2: the 5 Cs (context, company, customers, collaborators, competitors), the 3 Vs (value, segment, channel), and the 7 Ts. This keeps the new offering's go-to-market plan coherent with the rest of the business, rather than being designed around the product alone.

Business-model validation: three failure patterns

ExampleWhat failed, and why
Crystal PepsiDesirability trap: strong initial curiosity-driven trial, but no sustained value proposition customers wanted to repeat-purchase.
Perpetual motion machineFeasibility trap: can be highly desirable in theory, but infeasible because it violates fundamental (physical) constraints.
Pets.comViability trap: real desirability and feasibility, but the cost of shipping heavy, low-margin items made the business model unprofitable.

The lesson across all three: validating the product is not the same as validating the business model — a company needs evidence on desirability, feasibility, and viability before committing to full-scale launch.

Offering implementation & commercial deployment

Offering implementation is the operational work of actually producing, distributing, pricing, and supporting the offering at scale once the business model has been validated. Commercial deployment typically follows one of two paths:

StrategyDescription
Selective market deploymentLaunch in a limited market or segment first, to test and refine before a wider rollout.
Market expansionBroaden distribution and target segments more aggressively once the offering is validated.

Worked example: the Instant-Breakfast-Drink case

The textbook walks a hypothetical instant-breakfast-drink idea through the full Stage-Gate process: an initial concept screen (quick desirability/feasibility/viability check), prototype development and testing (refining taste, packaging, and positioning with real consumers), business-model validation (confirming the pricing and cost structure can be profitable at scale), and finally full commercial launch — with the investment and evidence required increasing at each successive gate.

Exam angleGiven a short failure story, identify whether it's a desirability, feasibility, or viability failure (use Crystal Pepsi/perpetual-motion-machine/Pets.com as your mental anchors). Distinguish selective market deployment from market expansion.

Two prices, one product

The retail price is what the shopper pays. The manufacturer’s selling price is what the retailer pays the manufacturer. The gap is the retailer’s margin.

Contribution margin (CM) = manufacturer’s selling price − variable cost (VC). It is each unit’s contribution toward covering fixed costs.

The trap: margins are a percent of price, not cost

Rule: retail price = selling price ÷ (1 − margin %). Multiplying by 1.40 treats the 40% as a markup on cost and understates the price.

Break-even formulas (from the slides)

QuestionFormula
How many units cover fixed costs?BEP = FC / CM
What price breaks even at a given volume?BESP = (FC / volume) + VC
How many extra units pay for a new fixed cost (e.g. an ad campaign)?Additional FC / CM
How many extra units are needed after a variable-cost increase (upgraded materials, a price promotion)?FC / (CM − new per-unit cost) − FC / CM: recalculate the (higher) BEP with the new cost and subtract the old BEP
Worked example (made-up numbers, arithmetic checked)

Selling price $10, VC $6, so CM = $4. Fixed costs $500,000.

BEP = 500,000 / 4 = 125,000 units. Break-even price at 100,000 units = 500,000 / 100,000 + 6 = $11.00. A $100,000 ad campaign needs 100,000 / 4 = 25,000 more units. Upgrading materials by $0.50 per unit: 500,000 / 3.50 − 125,000 = 142,857 − 125,000 = about 17,857 more units.

Exam angleConvert between retail and manufacturer price with the margin as a % of price (divide by 1 − m). Then run BEP, BESP and the two incremental break-evens; the variable-cost version is “new BEP minus old BEP.”

Costs decline by a fixed percent every time cumulative production doubles (1,000 → 2,000 → 4,000 → 8,000 …). The slides write the cost of the nth unit as

cn = c1 · n−a   so   ln cn = ln c1 − a · ln n

Solving for a (the slides’ example)

First unit costs c1 = 110; the millionth costs 60. Then ln 60 = ln 110 − a · ln 1,000,000, so a = ln(110/60) / ln(1,000,000) = 0.6061 / 13.8155 = 0.0438 (verified). Each doubling multiplies cost by 2−0.0438 = 0.970: about a 3% drop per doubling (a “97% curve”, much flatter than Markstrat’s 85%).

Break-even with an experience curve

When unit cost keeps falling, each unit contributes a little more than the last, so you add up contributions until they equal fixed cost: break-even is the N where Σn=1..N CMn = FC.

The slides’ console example, priced at $199: CMn = $121 − $26 − 110 · n−0.0438, where $121 is the net selling price, $26 the non-direct costs (not affected by experience), and the last term the direct cost on the curve. The slide gives break-even at 32.009 million units.

Source note: the fixed-cost figure sits on a garbled line in our copy. Summing the formula above, 32.009 million units produce about $1.31 billion of cumulative contribution, so that is the fixed cost the slide’s answer implies (inferred, not read). At that volume the last unit contributes about $43.40.

Exam angleSolve for a with logs given two points on the curve; convert a to a per-doubling percentage (2−a); explain why break-even with learning is a sum of rising contributions, not FC/CM.

Sales usually respond to marketing spend (e.g. advertising GRPs) in an S-shape: little effect at first, then increasing returns, then diminishing returns as the market saturates.

The rule

  • Add units of spend until MR = MC, i.e. marginal return (MR − MC) / MC = 0.
  • At that point one more dollar of advertising generates one more dollar of sales; beyond it, a dollar brings back slightly less, and profit declines.
  • The curve can also hit MR = MC on its rising part; that point is not optimal, because returns are still increasing there.

Several mix elements and a limited budget

Real budgets run out before every element reaches MR = MC. The intuition from the slides: spend the next dollar where it returns the most; as you spend more on one element it returns less, so you switch; when the money runs out, the last dollar in each element earns about the same return, and you are indifferent about where the next dollar would go.

Illustration: sales per $1k (made up)1st $1k2nd3rd4th
Advertising$3.0k$2.0k$1.4k$1.0k
Sales force$2.4k$1.8k$1.4k$1.0k

With $6k: fund A1 (3.0), S1 (2.4), A2 (2.0), S2 (1.8), A3 (1.4), S3 (1.4). Total sales $12.0k, and the last $1k in each element returns the same $1.4k.

Exam angleOptimal spend is where MR = MC on the diminishing part of the S-curve. Across mix elements, allocate the next dollar to the highest marginal return until marginal returns are equal.

Why is the upper right empty?Either it isn’t technologically feasible, or consumers won’t believe a gentle product is also effective. Brands that did combine “opposites”: BMW (luxury + performance), Range Rover (luxury + off-road), and L’Oréal in our case (technologically advanced + everyday).
Bufferin?Good, but stuck in the middle: gentle-seekers choose Tylenol, effectiveness-seekers choose Advil/Nuprin. Other stuck-in-the-middle examples: JC Penney, “family” restaurants, traditional American sedans.
Bayer?Tylenol beats it on both dimensions, so there is no reason to buy it other than price. Bayer must find a position it can win: heart health.

Attribute-basedSimilarity-based (MDS)
InputCustomer ratings of each product on attributes, often a semantic differential (Slow 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 Fast)Ratings of how similar or different pairs of products are

Trade-offMore up-front work writing questions; axes are easy to readLess up-front work, but can be hard to interpret

Manufacturer’s selling price minus variable cost: each unit’s contribution toward fixed costs.
Unit 2 · Margins & Break-Even
Margin vs. markup
Margins are a % of price: retail = selling price ÷ (1 − margin). $6 at a 40% margin = $10, not $8.40.
Unit 2 · Margins & Break-Even
Break-even point (BEP)
FC / CM: units needed to cover fixed costs.
Unit 2 · Margins & Break-Even
Break-even selling price (BESP)
(FC / volume) + VC: the price that breaks even at a given volume.
Unit 2 · Margins & Break-Even
Break-even on added fixed cost
Additional FC / CM (e.g. extra units an ad campaign must sell).
Unit 2 · Margins & Break-Even
Break-even on added variable cost
FC / (CM − new per-unit cost) − FC / CM: new BEP minus old BEP.
Unit 2 · Margins & Break-Even
Experience (learning) curve
Unit cost falls a fixed percent each time cumulative production doubles: cn = c1·n−a.
Unit 2 · Experience Curve
Learning exponent a
a = ln(c1/cn) / ln n; with 110 and 60 at one million, a = 0.0438 (about 3% per doubling).
Unit 2 · Experience Curve
Break-even with learning
The volume N where the sum of each unit’s (rising) contribution equals fixed cost.

S-shaped response function
Sales respond slowly, then with increasing returns, then diminishing returns as spend rises.

MR = MC
