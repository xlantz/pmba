L’Oréal of Paris: Bringing “Class to Mass” with Plénitude

A prestige beauty company tries to win in drugstore skincare. Plénitude grew fast, then plateaued and lost $12.5MM in 1995, while a new product sells well and research shows confusion about the line. This brief restates the case and its exhibits, explains the frameworks the prep questions use, and ends with an optional short companion case.

Walk the case

Plénitude, section by section

Who, what, when

The case is set in April 1996 at L’Oréal’s U.S. Retail Division in New York. Carol Hamilton, Senior VP of Marketing for the division (haircare, skincare and cosmetics), took responsibility for the Plénitude skincare line in early 1996. Her boss, Joseph Campinell, is division president. The author is Prof. Robert Dolan (HBS 9-598-056, dated October 1997). Plénitude was a 1982 French success that arrived in the U.S. in 1988 under a “class to mass” idea: bring department-store-quality, technology-led skincare into drugstores and mass merchants.

After a strong start and share gains, the brand has been on a sales plateau for about four years, has not earned a profit in the U.S., and lost $12.5MM pre-tax in 1995. A new product, Revitalift, is selling very well, but Hamilton worries about what it does to the brand’s image. She is preparing a plan to revitalize the line and bring it to profitability.

L’Oréal in 1995-96

Founded in 1907 by chemist Eugène Schueller around a hair-color process, L’Oréal sold 53.4 billion French francs in 1995 (one franc was $0.2039 at end-1995, so roughly $10.9 billion) from over 2,000 products under about 500 brand names in 150+ countries. Cosmetics were 81% of revenue. The family (Liliane Bettencourt) and Nestlé jointly controlled the holding company Gesparal (51% / 49%). R&D spending was $300 million in the last year and the firm registered 300 new patents.

U.S. sales were about $1.5 billion in 1995. Cosmair had been L’Oréal’s U.S. licensee (Nestlé the majority owner) until L’Oréal bought Nestlé out in 1994. Salon businesses (Technique Professionelle, Salon Classics, Redken) sit alongside mass lines. L’Oréal bought Maybelline in 1996 as its mass color-cosmetics vehicle, and in February 1996 merged the haircare and cosmetics/fragrance divisions into the Retail Division. Campinell calls the strategy “trickle down and fire up”: prestige-brand R&D (Lancôme, Biotherm) is leveraged in volume mass outlets, which funds the next round of technology. CEO Lindsay Owen-Jones frames it as essential for any luxury player to survive. In self-service outlets, the case notes, technology claims must be backed by advertising, merchandising and promotion. Préférence hair color (“Because I’m worth it”) is the cited success in mass.

The French launch and the “formula”

Plénitude launched in France in 1982 as “high end, superior performance but accessible.” Competitors in French mass skincare were few: Beiersdorf’s Nivea (big blue jar) and Henkel’s Diadermine led. The first product, a general-purpose moisturizer, carried a 30% price premium, used mostly print advertising (via Publicis/Bloom) and reached 1 million units in 1983. From 1984-87 special-purpose products were added, a cleanser came in 1986 (delayed because cleansers offer less room for technological differentiation), and TV joined print. By 1987 Plénitude held a 19.6% dollar share of French mass moisturizers (Diadermine 13.0%, Nivea 8.8%). Later, “Action Liposomes” (technology from Lancôme’s Niôsome) was priced at three times a basic moisturizer and received about a 25% share of voice in the category.

Element of the French formulaWhat it means
1. Technologically superior productsProduct technology is the primary engine of sales
2. Concentrate on the newest “star product”Most resources go to one hero item meant to lift the entire line’s brand equity
3. “Golden Rules” of advertisingFeature the star, give evidence of technological superiority, show an up-to-date, assertive executive woman (“I live with the times”)

The U.S. launch (1988) and what followed

Plénitude was test-marketed in Atlanta and Dallas in 1988 (moisturizer share reached as high as 14% in test markets). Research led L’Oréal to soften the benefit from “Delays the signs of aging” to “Reduces the signs of aging,” and some products were made lighter and less perfumed. Unlike France’s sequential build, the U.S. launch was the whole line at once: 14 SKUs (basic moisturizers, treatment moisturizers, cleansers). Sales director Marianne Coll explains why: most skincare sells in the Health and Beauty Aids aisles (Olay, Pond’s, Neutrogena, Nivea) rather than the cosmetics wall, so L’Oréal needed new buyers and wanted to recreate a department-store experience, educating shoppers at point of purchase and mapping products to skin types without a salesperson. Retailers had to take all 14 SKUs, a one-linear-foot unit, and eye-level space beside Oil of Olay. The L’Oréal name’s trade reputation (honoring spending commitments, profitable lines for retailers) won acceptance. Year 1 brought $32.5MM of advertising and 10,000 in-store events, and sales targets were hit.

The star-system pattern then ran year by year: full line (1989), Action Liposomes (1990), Eye Defense Creme (1991), Hydra Renewal and an “Advanced” Wrinkle Defense upgrade (1992-93), Excell-A3 (1994), Revitalift Face (1995). Plénitude became #2 behind Oil of Olay quickly, grew through 1991, then plateaued; Hamilton says it lost #2 to Pond’s and had just regained #2 in moisturizers when she took over. The line had been unprofitable throughout; she says the brand was expected to need heavy investment because of Procter’s position with Olay.

  • The 1995 marketing plan’s pricing rationale was to maintain premium pricing to reinforce quality and performance.
  • The team believes it would be hard to get appreciable price increases through the trade, so simplifying price points would probably mean cuts on average.

StepCalculationWhat the case gives you
A. Net sales (or gross revenues)From the case1990 $31.7MM; 1995 about twice that
B. Cost of goods soldNet sales × COGS %25% (footnote 4 says apply 25% to trade prices)
C. Gross marginA − BYou compute it

F. Other brand-level costsNot itemizedReported pre-tax result is the only check
G. Pre-tax resultE − F-$25.4MM; -$12.5MM

Two things to notice when you build it yourself: first, whether your computed subtotal through D reconciles to the reported pre-tax result, and what that implies about costs the case never lists; second, that the case uses “net sales” for the doubling statement and “gross revenues” for the 25% COGS rate. State your assumption about whether they are the same thing. The case also warns that mass merchants take generally smaller margins than drug stores, though relative margins across manufacturers are similar in all outlet types.

1. Reading a brand income statement

A brand P&L walks from what customers (or the trade) pay down to what the brand actually earns. Each subtotal tells you something different:

LineMeaningQuestion it answers
Net salesRevenue after trade discounts and allowances (the case may call these net sales or gross revenues; know which you are using)How big is the business at the price the company actually receives?
− COGSCost to make the goods sold (here usually a percent of trade price)What does each dollar of sales cost to produce?
= Gross marginSales less COGSHow much is left to pay for marketing and everything else?
− Advertising and promotionMedia, consumer promotion, trade promotion, in-store programsHow heavy is the marketing investment relative to the margin available?
= Contribution after marketingGross margin less marketingDoes the brand cover its own marketing before other costs?
− Other brand costsSales force, overhead, allocated R&D, etc.What remains for the corporation?
= Pre-tax profitThe bottom lineIs the brand self-sustaining?

What the statement says about marketing options. Marketing dollars come out of gross margin, so the margin percentage sets the room for spending. Three useful tools, all generic:

  • Marketing as a percent of sales: A&P ÷ net sales. If it is near or above the gross-margin percentage, there is little or no room to add spending without more volume.
  • Break-even sales: (costs you must cover) ÷ (contribution margin percent). Example (made-up): if a brand has $30 of spending to cover and a 60% margin, break-even sales are $30 ÷ 0.60 = $50.
  • Price-cut breakeven: required volume lift = price cut ÷ (margin% − price cut), with both as a percent of the original price. Example (made-up): margin 60%, price cut 10% means 10 ÷ (60 − 10) = 20% more volume just to stand still.
  • Fixed versus variable spending: some marketing is committed (retail displays, trade promises), some is flexible. The flexible part determines what can be changed quickly.
Exam angleA strong answer builds the statement line by line, states assumptions (what counts as sales, what COGS applies to), flags what is missing, then connects the arithmetic to what marketing can and cannot do. It does not stop at “the brand loses money.”

2. Interpreting marketing research

Match the evidence to the question.

Evidence typeWhat it can tell youLimits
Quantitative survey (large, random)How many people think or do something; segment sizes; differences between brands; projectableTells you what, not always why; wording and sampling matter
Focus groups / acceptor-rejectorWhy people use or stop; language they use; reactions to packagingSmall and not statistically representative; vivid quotes can mislead
Shelf or concept testHow people behave when faced with the real shelf or packMock setting; stated reactions can differ from purchases
Market tracking (e.g., ACNielsen)Actual sales, share, price, distribution, trade featuresShows what happened, not motivation

Awareness, trial and usage. Three numbers per brand, plus two ratios:

  • Conversion = % ever tried ÷ % aware.
  • Retention = % currently using ÷ % ever tried.
  • Worked example (made-up): Brand K has 80% awareness, 40% trial and 20% current use. Conversion is 40 ÷ 80 = 50%; retention is 20 ÷ 40 = 50%. If a rival with the same awareness had 60% trial but only 15% current use, it is better at getting trial and worse at keeping users. The funnel shows where a brand leaks: awareness, getting people to try, or keeping them.

Brand equity and image. Brand equity is the extra value a name carries. It is typically assessed through awareness (do people know it) and associations (are they strong, favorable and unique), and by looking at loyalty and willingness to pay. Look for which name carries the equity when there is a corporate or endorsing brand and a product brand: the research may show one without the other.

Market share. Share is a ratio: brand sales ÷ category sales (dollar or unit). Trend matters as much as level. Remember that a rising category can hide a flat share, and that dollar and unit share differ when price differs. Share of voice is a brand’s ad spending ÷ category ad spending; comparing share of voice with share of market is a standard check on advertising efficiency.

Perceptual maps. Brands are placed in two dimensions based on attribute ratings. Closer brands are seen as more alike and therefore compete more closely. An arrow is an attribute; arrows pointing the same way are highly correlated. To read where a brand stands on an attribute, drop a perpendicular from the brand onto the arrow (extending the arrow backward through the origin if needed).

A map is an average view across respondents and an approximation (two dimensions summarize many). Two maps can tell different stories: a brand map shows perceptions of the product, a user map shows who people imagine uses it.

Exam angleDistinguish what each study can and cannot show. For every finding, ask who was asked (users, rejectors, all women), how many, and whether the result is a perception or a behavior.

3. Competitive set analysis

A competitive set is the group of brands a consumer treats as substitutes. Build it, then judge strength:

  1. Define the set by who competes for the same occasion: same channel, similar price tier, similar benefits, and close in perceptual space.
  2. Measure each rival’s position: share and share trend; awareness, trial, retention; price position; image; breadth and type of line.
  3. Measure staying power: advertising level and share of voice; distribution; the parent company’s resources and strategy.
  4. Look for gaps: unmet benefits, segments underserved, vulnerable rivals, trends that favor or hurt each competitor.
  5. Ask whether your brand can win: do your strengths line up with the gap, and can you fund it?

Worked example (made-up):

“Is there an opportunity to score?” asks for a judgment that weighs the size of the prize against competitors’ strength and your own ability to execute. No single number answers it.
