How to work it

How to work it
  1. Ending assets = beginning assets + change. AMD: 2,900 + 650 = 3,550. Intel: 95,000 + 3,200 = 98,200.
  2. Ending liabilities = beginning liabilities + change. AMD: 2,400 + 210 = 2,610. Intel: 41,000 − 540 = 40,460.
  3. Stockholders' equity = Assets − Liabilities. AMD: 3,550 − 2,610 = 940. Intel: 98,200 − 40,460 = 57,740.

  2. b. AMD average assets $3,225M; Intel average assets $96,600M.
  3. c. Intel — its equity is 58.8% of assets, versus AMD's 26.5%.

U2-Q5Reading and Interpreting Audit Opinions

Unit 2

Amazon.com, Inc.'s financial statements include the following audit report (real report, unchanged).

The Board of Directors and Shareholders Amazon.com, Inc. Opinion on the Financial Statements We have audited the accompanying consolidated balance sheets of Amazon.com, Inc. (the Company) as of December 31, 2025 and 2024, the related consolidated statements of operations, comprehensive income, stockholders' equity, and cash flows for each of the three years in the period ended December 31, 2025, and the related notes (collectively referred to as the "consolidated financial statements"). In our opinion, the consolidated financial statements present fairly, in all material respects, the financial position of the Company at December 31, 2025 and 2024, and the results of its operations and its cash flows for each of the three years in the period ended December 31, 2025, in conformity with U.S. generally accepted accounting principles. We also have audited, in accordance with the standards of the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (United States) (PCAOB), the Company's internal control over financial reporting as of December 31, 2025, based on criteria established in Internal Control-Integrated Framework issued by the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission (2013 framework) and our report dated February 5, 2026 expressed an unqualified opinion thereon.
a. To whom is the audit report addressed?
b. True or False for each statement:
1. Auditors express an opinion on whether the statements present fairly and are free from material misstatement
2. Auditors express an opinion that there was no fraudulent activity at the company
3. Auditors' opinion is that there are no misstatements in the related notes
4. Auditors separately audited internal controls over financial reporting
5. Auditors examined most of the company's financial transactions individually to ensure they were accurate
c. What sort of opinion did the auditor provide on the effectiveness of internal control over financial reporting?
d. Which best describes the scope of the two opinions in the report?
How to work it
  1. The greeting line of the report names exactly who it's addressed to — read the first line: "The Board of Directors and Shareholders, Amazon.com, Inc."
  2. Audits assess fair presentation and material misstatement, not fraud detection or line-by-line transaction verification — that's why statements 2, 3, and 5 are false, and 1 and 4 are true (a separate internal-controls opinion is explicitly mentioned in the second paragraph).
  3. The report says the internal controls opinion was "unqualified" — stated directly in the last sentence ("expressed an unqualified opinion thereon"). Worth noting: this is a genuinely clean bill of health on controls, unlike some companies (e.g., newer public companies) that sometimes receive an adverse ICFR opinion while still getting a clean opinion on the financial statements themselves.
  4. The report contains two distinct opinions: one on the fairness of the financial statements (first paragraph), one on internal control effectiveness (second paragraph) — they are not the same scope, even though both came back clean here.
  1. a. Addressed to the Board of Directors and Shareholders of Amazon.com, Inc.
  2. b. 1. True — 2. False — 3. False — 4. True — 5. False.
  3. c. Unqualified opinion on internal control effectiveness.
  4. d. One opinion covers the fairness of the financial statements; the other covers the effectiveness of internal controls.

U2-Q7Applying Financial Statement Linkages to Understand Transactions

Unit 2

For each transaction (a–g), select + for an increase, − for a decrease, or n/a if the line item isn't affected.
a. Issued common stock for cash and property & equipment   b. Paid cash for rent of office furnishings/facilities   c. Performed services, received cash immediately   d. Performed services, billed client (due in 60 days)   e. Paid cash salary to an employee   f. Received partial cash payment on the receivable from (d)   g. Paid cash dividends

How to work it
  1. Balance Sheet: Cash moves with every cash transaction (a in part, b, c, e, f cash-in, g). Noncash assets move when property or receivables change (a's property portion, d's new receivable, f's receivable collected). Contributed capital only moves with stock issuance (a). Retained earnings moves with anything hitting net income (b, c, d, e) or dividends (g) — not with pure asset swaps like (f).
  2. Cash flow statement: Only cash transactions show up. Financing CF: the cash portion of the stock issuance (a) and the dividend (g). Operating CF: cash rent (b), cash service revenue (c), cash salary (e), and the cash collected on the receivable (f). Investing CF is untouched here — the property in (a) was acquired via stock, not cash, so it's a noncash transaction.
  3. Income statement: Revenue is recognized when services are performed (c and d), regardless of when cash arrives — that's why (f), which is just collecting cash on an existing receivable, has no income statement effect. Expenses hit when incurred (b, e), reducing net income.
  1. a. Issued stock for cash and property: increases Cash, Noncash assets, Contributed capital, and Financing cash flow. Nothing decreases.
  2. b. Paid cash for rent: decreases Cash, Retained earnings, Operating cash flow, and Net income; increases Expenses.
  3. c. Performed services, received cash: increases Cash, Retained earnings, Operating cash flow, Revenues, and Net income. Nothing decreases.
  4. d. Performed services, billed client: increases Noncash assets, Retained earnings, Revenues, and Net income. Nothing decreases.
  5. e. Paid cash salary: decreases Cash, Retained earnings, Operating cash flow, and Net income; increases Expenses.
  6. f. Collected cash on the receivable: increases Cash and Operating cash flow; decreases Noncash assets. No income statement effect.
  7. g. Paid cash dividends: decreases Cash, Retained earnings, and Financing cash flow. Nothing increases.
  8. Total liabilities, Other equity, and Investing cash flow are n/a across every transaction in this set.

Prepare journal entries for each accounting adjustment.

a. Unrecorded depreciation on equipment is $2,340.
AccountDebitCredit
b. The supplies account has a balance of $9,875. Supplies still available at the end of the period total $2,610.
AccountDebitCredit
c. On the date for preparing financial statements, an estimated utilities expense of $1,340 has been incurred, but no utility bill has yet been received or paid.
AccountDebitCredit
d. On the first day of the current period, rent for four periods was paid and recorded as a $9,600 increase to prepaid rent and a $9,600 decrease to cash.
AccountDebitCredit
e. Nine months ago, a one-year service policy was sold to a customer, and the insurance company recorded the cash received by increasing (crediting) unearned revenue for $6,072. No accounting adjustments have been prepared during the nine-month period. The insurance company is now preparing annual financial statements.
AccountDebitCredit
f. At the end of the period, employee wages of $3,015 have been incurred but not paid or recorded.
AccountDebitCredit
g. At the end of the period, $940 of interest has been earned but not yet received or recorded.
AccountDebitCredit
How to work it
  1. (a) Depreciation always debits Depreciation Expense and credits Accumulated Depreciation — it never touches the asset account directly.
  2. (b) Supplies used = beginning balance − ending available: $9,875 − $2,610 = $7,265.
  3. (c) An incurred-but-unbilled cost is accrued: debit the expense, credit a payable.
  4. (d) One of the four periods has now elapsed: $9,600 ÷ 4 = $2,400. The adjusting entry never re-touches Cash — that already happened in the original transaction.
  5. (e) Nine of twelve months have elapsed on the policy: 9/12 × $6,072 = $4,554 of the unearned balance has now been earned.
  6. (f) Wages earned but unpaid is a classic accrued expense: debit the expense, credit a payable.
  7. (g) Interest earned but not yet received is accrued revenue — debit a receivable (Accounts Receivable, since this list has no separate Interest Receivable line), credit Interest Income.
  1. a. Debit Depreciation Expense $2,340; Credit Accumulated Depreciation $2,340.
  2. b. Debit Supplies Expense $7,265; Credit Supplies $7,265.
  3. c. Debit Utilities Expense $1,340; Credit Utilities Payable $1,340.
  4. d. Debit Rent Expense $2,400; Credit Prepaid Rent $2,400.
  5. e. Debit Unearned Insurance Premium Revenue $4,554; Credit Insurance Premium Revenue $4,554.
  6. f. Debit Wages Expense $3,015; Credit Wages Payable $3,015.
  7. g. Debit Accounts Receivable $940; Credit Interest Income $940.

Unit 3

Ashcroft Consulting recorded service revenues of $610,000 during the most recent fiscal year, of which $505,000 were on credit and $105,000 were for cash. Of the $505,000 credit sales, Ashcroft collected $65,000 cash on those receivables before year-end. The company also paid $78,000 cash for wages for the year. Its employees also earned another $46,000 in wages during the year, which were not yet paid at year-end.

a. Compute the company's net income for the fiscal year.
$
b. How much net cash inflow or outflow did the company generate during the year? (enter inflow as positive, outflow as negative)
$
Explain why Ashcroft's net income and net cash flow differ (do not use a negative sign below):

Cash inflow from services rendered will be $ than service revenue per the income statement because Ashcroft only collected $ of revenues in cash but reported $610,000 as revenue.

Cash outflow for wages paid will be $ than wages expense on the income statement because $ remained unpaid at year-end.

The combined effects of these two items yields an overall difference of $ between net income and net cash .

How to work it
  1. Net income = total revenue earned − total wages incurred (regardless of cash timing) = $610,000 − ($78,000 + $46,000) = $486,000.
  2. Net cash flow = cash actually received ($105,000 cash sales + $65,000 collected on receivables = $170,000) − cash actually paid ($78,000 wages) = $92,000 inflow.
  3. Revenue gap: $610,000 revenue − $170,000 cash collected = $440,000 — cash inflow is $440,000 less than revenue, because $440,000 of sales are still sitting in receivables.
  4. Wage gap: $124,000 wages expense (78,000 + 46,000) − $78,000 cash paid = $46,000 — cash outflow is $46,000 less than expense, exactly the amount still owed (wages payable).
  5. Combined: $440,000 − $46,000 = $394,000, matching $486,000 net income − $92,000 net cash inflow = $394,000.
  1. a. Net income = $486,000.
  2. b. Net cash inflow = $92,000.
  3. Explanation: cash inflow from services was $440,000 less than revenue, because only $170,000 of the $610,000 revenue was collected in cash. Cash outflow for wages was $46,000 less than wages expense, because $46,000 remained unpaid at year-end. Combined, that's a $394,000 overall difference between net income and net cash inflow.

Prepare entries to close these accounts in journal entry form. Then post the closing entries in the T-accounts below and give the post-closing balance of Retained Earnings.

1. Close revenue accounts
AccountDebitCredit
2. Close expense accounts
AccountDebitCredit

How to work it
  1. Closing revenues: debit the revenue account for its full balance, credit Retained Earnings — this zeroes Service Fees Earned out.
  2. Closing expenses: total expenses = $52,600 + $118,900 + $14,750 + $25,300 = $211,550. Debit Retained Earnings for that total, credit each expense account for its own balance to zero it out.

Since expenses exceeded revenue, Prescott actually had a net loss for the period ($203,400 − $211,550 = −$8,150), which is why Retained Earnings went down overall despite the revenue-closing credit.
  4. All four temporary accounts (Service Fees Earned, Rent Expense, Salaries Expense, Supplies Expense, Depreciation Expense) end at exactly $0 after closing — that's the whole point of the closing process.

Unit 3

Fairview Logistics closes its accounts on December 31 each year. The company works a five-day work week and pays its employees every two weeks. On December 31, Fairview accrued $14,850 of salaries payable. On January 9 of the following year, the company paid salaries of $42,000 cash to employees. Prepare entries using the financial statement effects template to (a) accrue the salaries payable on December 31, and (b) record the salary payment nine days later on January 9.

How to fill this out: every cell in the template needs an entry, even the ones a transaction doesn't touch. For a column a transaction doesn't affect, enter 0 in the dollar row and select N/A in the account row below it — don't leave it blank. (Blank cells are graded the same as 0/N/A here, but your real homework platform may not be this forgiving, so it's worth building the habit.)
Cash AssetNoncash AssetsLiabilitiesContributed CapitalEarned CapitalRevenuesExpensesNet Income
a. December 31: Accrue salaries expense
b. January 9: Pay salaries
How to work it
  1. (a) At year-end, Fairview owes $14,850 for days already worked — a standard accrual: liability up, expense up.
  2. (b) The $42,000 payment on January 9 does two jobs at once: it pays off the $14,850 already accrued (just settling a debt, no new expense), and it pays for new days worked in January that were never accrued. That new portion is $42,000 − $14,850 = $27,150 — a brand-new January expense.
  3. Check the balance: Cash down $42,000 = Liabilities down $14,850 + Earned Capital down $27,150. $14,850 + $27,150 = $42,000. ✓
  4. This is the classic "payment spans a period boundary" trap — the full cash amount is never the same as either the liability or the new expense alone.
  1. a. Increase Wages Payable (Liabilities) by $14,850; decrease Retained Earnings by $14,850; increase Wages Expense by $14,850; decrease Net Income by $14,850.
  2. b. Decrease Cash by $42,000; decrease Wages Payable (Liabilities) by $14,850; decrease Retained Earnings by $27,150; increase Wages Expense by $27,150; decrease Net Income by $27,150.
