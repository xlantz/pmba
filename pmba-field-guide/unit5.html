MGT 6050 · Unit 5

Team creativity & innovation

Every earlier unit was about getting a team to function well together. Unit 5 asks a sharper question: what actually makes a team's output creative? A research survey lays out what's known (and what's still genuinely unsettled) about team innovation. A real 2024 experiment tests what generative AI actually does to team ideation — and the answer isn't what most people assume. And Pixar's president lays out, in concrete detail, the peer-driven process behind one of the most creatively consistent studios in film history.

Unit 5, source by source

Schwarz, R. (2015, December 15). "What the Research Tells Us About Team Creativity and Innovation." Harvard Business Review.

Big idea

Creativity and innovation are among the least settled areas in team research. Innovation involves two stages — generating new ideas (creativity) and then implementing them — and while researchers understand a fair amount about factors that help, some of the more complex relationships are only starting to be understood, and a few are genuinely counterintuitive.

What we know helps (a comprehensive meta-analysis)

  • A compelling vision — teams innovate more when members share a common understanding of, and commitment to, team objectives.
  • Goal interdependence — the extent to which members can only meet their own goals by helping others meet theirs. An Olympic hockey team has high interdependence (the team wins or loses together); an Olympic gymnastics team has low interdependence (members can win individual medals regardless of the team outcome). High interdependence pushes members toward mutually beneficial behavior.
  • Support for innovation — teams innovate more when managers expect and approve of it, support members through failed attempts, and recognize new ideas and their implementation. This means genuinely encouraging risk and expecting some failures.
  • A task orientation — a shared concern for excellence: setting high performance standards, monitoring performance, and giving each other feedback.
  • A cohesive team — commitment to the team and desire to be part of it, which creates a psychologically safe environment for challenging each other and the status quo.
  • Strong internal and external communication — internal communication enables sharing knowledge/ideas and safe feedback; external communication (with people outside the team) brings in new information and learning from others.

What's still being worked out (and can be counterintuitive)

  • What helps creativity can hinder innovation, and vice versa. Idea generation (divergent thinking/exploration) and implementation (convergent thinking/exploitation) require different skills, structures, and processes. Unless a team hands off its ideas to someone else, it needs to operate in both modes and switch between them.
  • Diversity is a benefit that can have costs. One meta-analysis found cultural diversity increases creativity — but also increases task conflict and reduces group cohesion, with no net effect on team performance. Separately, adding more functional areas to a team showed no significant innovation benefit. Realizing diversity's benefit seems to require members actively taking the perspective of those who differ from them — the benefit isn't automatic, and neither is the cost.
  • The right level of conflict matters, at the right time. One study of project teams found a curvilinear relationship: creativity was highest at moderate levels of task conflict — but only early in a team's life cycle. Later on, task conflict showed no relationship to creativity at all.
Exam angleDon't treat "diversity helps creativity" as a simple, unconditional finding — be ready to name the specific costs (task conflict, reduced cohesion) that came with the creativity gain in the meta-analysis Schwarz cites, and the condition (perspective-taking) required to capture the benefit without the cost.

Stasik, J. (2024, March–April). "Don't Let Gen AI Limit Your Team's Creativity." Harvard Business Review. Based on research by Kian Gohar and Jeremy Utley.

Big idea

A real-world experiment across four companies found that teams using ChatGPT for a creative problem-solving task did not produce dramatically better ideas — and in one specific way, they did slightly worse. The technology isn't the problem; how teams use it is.

The experiment

Small teams at four companies (two in Europe, two in the U.S.) worked on a real business problem for 90 minutes. Control-group teams got no AI help; experimental-group teams got an open-source ChatGPT to use during individual and group ideation. Each team's ideas were graded A ("highly compelling") through D ("not worth pursuing") by the person who'd actually have to implement the solution — without knowing which ideas came from AI-assisted teams.

The results upended the researchers' own expectations

AI-assisted teams produced only 8% more ideas on average, and 7% fewer D's — but also 8% more B's ("interesting but needs development") and 2% fewer A's. Generative AI helped teams avoid truly awful ideas, but it also nudged them toward more average ones. Meanwhile, AI-assisted teams gained 21% more confidence in their own problem-solving ability — confidence the actual grades didn't support.

Why this happens

Large language models are built to produce statistically average answers — the most probable continuation of a prompt. A vague problem statement gets a generic, "safe" response. Many teams also fell for the Einstellung effect: a cognitive bias toward accepting the first familiar-seeming solution rather than exploring further, which is exactly the pattern behind the surge in B-grade ideas.

Five practices that actually work

  • Be precise about the problem. A highly specific problem statement (with concrete detail and a target metric) produces far better output than a broad, generic question.
  • Brainstorm individually before using the tool. 15–30 minutes of solo ideation first protects against groupthink and against anchoring on whatever the AI suggests first.
  • Rigorously train the AI on your specific context — feed it real detail (customer information, past successes/failures, industry benchmarks) before relying on it.
  • Treat it as an ongoing conversation, not an oracle. Teams that iterated repeatedly with the tool, pushing back and asking for more, got the A-grade ideas. Teams that accepted the first answer got B's.
  • Have an outside facilitator guide the final decision — someone (ideally AI-ideation-savvy) to consolidate suggestions, prompt the AI to critique its own output, and help prioritize.

A real example: EMC Insurance

Joe Riesberg, EMC's CIO, described being initially impressed by ChatGPT's output for a customer-experience brainstorm — until the actual grading showed his team had simply accepted the tool's first "sensible but generic" answers rather than pushing it further. His takeaway: "The more you question generative AI, the better its answers will be" — and that kind of direct, unsparing follow-up doesn't always come naturally to people.

Exam angleThe single most testable finding: AI-assisted teams got fewer A-grade ("highly compelling") ideas despite gaining significantly more confidence. Be ready to explain both halves of that finding and why they aren't a contradiction — the confidence gain came from quantity and avoiding bad ideas, not from genuinely superior creative quality.

Catmull, E. (2008, September). "How Pixar Fosters Collective Creativity." Harvard Business Review.

Big idea

Ed Catmull, Pixar's co-founder and president, argues that a movie studio's central problem isn't finding good ideas — it's building an environment where talented people can work together effectively. His clearest summary of the whole philosophy: "If you give a good idea to a mediocre team, they'll screw it up. But if you give a mediocre idea to a great team, they'll fix it or throw it away and come up with something that works."

The Toy Story 2 crisis

During production of Toy Story 2, the film's original team wasn't up to the task, and Pixar's proven creative leaders had to take over with only 8 months left on an already-aggressive schedule. They saved the film by adding an emotional throughline (the "Jessie's Story" sequence) that gave the audience real reason to believe the protagonist might make a different choice. The crisis became formative: it taught Pixar that a good idea in the hands of a mediocre team fails, while a strong team can rescue even a mediocre premise — and it hardened an internal, non-negotiable standard that everything Pixar touches must be excellent.

The Brain Trust

Pixar's directors periodically convene a peer group of fellow filmmakers (the "brain trust") to review a film in progress and give candid, unsparing feedback. The crucial design choice: the brain trust has no authority to mandate any changes. The director decides what to do with the advice. This is what makes candid feedback possible at all — members can give their honest opinion without it becoming a power struggle, and the director can seek help without fear of losing creative control. Pixar learned this the hard way: when it tried exporting the same review model to its technical departments with some authority attached, the dynamic broke down immediately.

The dailies

Borrowed from practices at Disney and Industrial Light & Magic, Pixar's daily review process has the whole crew look at unfinished, in-progress work and give feedback constantly. Showing incomplete work normalizes imperfection, lets people learn from and inspire each other in real time, and means there are no surprises when a scene is finally "done."

Pixar's three operating principles

  1. Everyone must have the freedom to communicate with anyone — decision-making hierarchy and communication structure are treated as two separate things.
  2. It must be safe for everyone to offer ideas — achieved partly through constant showings of in-progress work, staggered so fresh eyes always see it, with an explicit effort to make honest, explained criticism feel safe.
  3. Stay close to innovations happening in the academic community — technical staff are encouraged to publish and attend conferences, reinforcing that people (and ideas from outside) matter more than any single idea the company already has.

Pixar also deliberately designs its building (a large central atrium holding the cafeteria, mailboxes, and meeting rooms) to maximize inadvertent cross-department encounters, and runs "Pixar University," an internal set of courses mixing job-relevant and unrelated skills specifically to get people from different disciplines mixing and learning together.

Staying honest with success: postmortems and fresh blood

Catmull is candid that postmortems are hard to do well — people naturally want to talk about what went right, not what went wrong, and will game the format if it becomes predictable. Techniques that help: varying the postmortem's format each time, asking each group for their top five "would do again" and top five "wouldn't do again" items, and grounding the discussion in actual data rather than only personal impressions. He's equally direct that bringing in "fresh blood" is essential to avoid organizational blindness, but requires managing two specific risks: the well-known not-invented-here syndrome (rejecting outside ideas) and the less obvious awe-of-the-institution syndrome (new hires being too intimidated to speak up) — the second of which Catmull addresses directly by speaking candidly about Pixar's own mistakes at new-hire orientation.

Exam angleThe signature synthesis prompt for this unit: the brain trust's "no authority" design is a direct, concrete application of psychological safety (Units 1–2) to a creative-feedback setting specifically. Be ready to explain why giving that group real authority would have undermined the exact dynamic that makes candid feedback possible in the first place — and connect this to why AI-ideation-facilitators in the Gohar/Utley reading are advised to prioritize and critique, not dictate.

Key takeaways — Unit 5

Before the exam

Likely exam & discussion angles

  • Given a team scenario, identify which of the six research-backed innovation factors is present or missing, and predict the likely effect.
  • Explain why diversity's creativity benefit in the research came bundled with real costs (task conflict, reduced cohesion), and what condition is needed to capture the benefit without the cost.
  • Walk through the gen AI experiment's design and results, and explain precisely why "more confidence" didn't mean "better ideas."
  • Apply the five effective gen-AI practices to a described brainstorming scenario and identify which one is missing.
  • Explain the Brain Trust's "no authority" design choice and why removing that constraint (as Pixar found when testing it in technical departments) breaks the whole dynamic.
  • The signature synthesis prompt: connect the Brain Trust's design directly to psychological safety (Units 1–2) and to the AI-facilitator advice in the Gohar/Utley study — all three rely on the same underlying principle about where authority should (and shouldn't) sit during a feedback process.
  • Cross-unit synthesis: compare Catmull's "postmortems get gamed unless you vary the format" observation to Unit 4's fairness-climate research on consistent, explained process — both are about designing a process that resists being quietly hollowed out over time.
