Chapters 1, 2, 3 and 7 and the Unit 1 slides

Two definitions, one shared idea

The social definition of marketing is a societal process by which individuals fulfill their needs by creating and freely exchanging products and services of value with others. The managerial definition is the art and science of identifying, attracting, and retaining customers through creating, delivering, and communicating superior customer value. They sound different, but both hinge on the same idea: value. Drucker put it sharply: "the aim of marketing is to make selling superfluous." If you understand the customer well enough, the product sells itself — the Nintendo Wii, Apple iPad, and Toyota Prius were all swamped with orders because of marketing homework, not great retail selling.

Ten things that get marketed

Marketing isn't just physical goods. The textbook lists ten domains: goods (cars, food), services (airlines, consultants — a growing share of advanced economies, roughly two-thirds of U.S. output), events (the Olympics, trade shows), experiences (Disney's Magic Kingdom), persons (Oprah, Michael Jordan as brands), places (Las Vegas's "What Happens Here, Stays Here"), properties (real estate, stocks — intangible ownership rights), organizations (museums, nonprofits), information (Nielsen, Gartner), and ideas (social causes like Coca-Cola's "World Without Waste").

Companies that failed to update their business models — Yahoo!, Blockbuster, Barnes & Noble — were overtaken by Google, Netflix, and Amazon. Fresh ideas tend to come from three underrepresented groups: employees with contemporary or diverse perspectives, employees far from headquarters, and employees new to the industry.

Four forces reshaping every market

Four forces are pushing companies toward holistic marketing:

ForceWhat's changing
TechnologyE-commerce, mobile, cloud, and AI enable new business models (Netflix, Airbnb, Uber) and one-to-one interaction at scale; analytics and AI have been democratized so smaller companies can use them too.
GlobalizationFriedman's "the world is flat" — countries are more multicultural, and most of the next billion middle-class consumers will likely be Asian. Outsourcing cuts costs but risks jobs, IP, and supply-chain disruption.
Physical environmentClimate change (extreme weather, rising seas) and health conditions (pandemics) are reshaping where and how companies operate.
Social responsibilityMarketers must weigh the ethical, environmental, legal, and social context of their work — satisfying target markets while preserving or enhancing consumers' and society's long-term well-being.

Those four forces shape three new market outcomes: new consumer capabilities (buy anywhere after researching in-store, or "showrooming"; share opinions on social media; reject unwanted marketing), new company capabilities (mass personalization, richer customer data, more efficient reach), and a new competitive environment (deregulation, retail transformation, and a tug-of-war between disintermediation and reintermediation).

Exam angleDisintermediation and reintermediation are opposites, and it's an easy pair to flip under time pressure. Disintermediation is a direct-to-consumer startup (Warby Parker, Casper, Away) cutting out the middleman. Reintermediation is a traditional firm fighting back by blending online and physical — "brick-and-click."

Holistic marketing's four components

ComponentWhat it is
Relationship marketingBuilding mutually satisfying long-term relationships with four constituents — customers, employees, marketing partners, and the financial community — to earn and retain their business. The end result is a marketing network.
Integrated marketingCoordinating every activity so it delivers consistent value and message; "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts." A GE MRI sale comes bundled with installation, maintenance, and training.
Internal marketingHiring, training, and motivating employees who want to serve customers well — needs both vertical alignment (with senior management) and horizontal alignment (with other departments).
Performance marketingUnderstanding the financial and nonfinancial returns to business and society — brand equity, customer lifetime value, and ROMI. Ben & Jerry's went from a "double" to a "triple bottom line" (people, planet, profits).

The market-value concept favors relationships over transactions, integrated marketing over piecemeal manual actions, a strong culture over disengaged employees, and science over intuition.

Five philosophies, from inside-out to outside-in

Companies tend to operate from one of five philosophies, moving from a company-centered ("inside-out") view toward a customer-centered ("outside-in") one:

PhilosophyCore beliefWhen it fits / the risk
ProductionConsumers want products that are widely available and inexpensive.Fits developing markets or an expanding market; focuses on efficiency and mass distribution (Lenovo, Haier in China).
ProductConsumers want the highest quality, performance, or innovative features.Risk: the "better-mousetrap fallacy" — believing a better product sells itself without pricing, distribution, and promotion.
SellingLeft alone, buyers won't buy enough, so push hard.Used for unsought goods (insurance, cemetery plots) or overcapacity; risky as a general strategy.
MarketingCustomer-centered; sense and respond (mid-1950s)."Find the right products for your customers, not customers for your products" (Dell's build-your-own platform). Levitt: selling focuses on the seller's need to turn product into cash; marketing focuses on the buyer's needs.
Market-valueHolistic; every functional area creates value for customers, company, and collaborators.Define the business as a customer-satisfying process, not a product — products are transient, needs endure.

Concrete illustration: Pepsi. With a product view, its market is carbonated soft-drink drinkers and its rivals are other soda companies. With a market-value view, its market is anyone who is thirsty, so its rivals include water, juice, tea, and coffee. The same shift shows up across Table 1.1 in the book: Union Pacific went from "we run a railroad" to "we move people and goods"; Xerox from "we make copying equipment" to "we help improve office productivity"; Carrier from "we make air conditioners and furnaces" to "we provide climate control in the home."

Exam angleWatch for a scenario describing a company that just built a better product and assumed it would sell itself — that's the product-concept trap (the "better-mousetrap fallacy"), not the marketing concept. And don't confuse the marketing concept (sense and respond to the customer) with the broader market-value concept (every function in the company creates value, not just the marketing department).

Flipping the org chart

A traditional org chart puts top management at the top and customers at the bottom. A customer-oriented chart inverts it: customers on top, then frontline people who deal with them directly, then service (middle) managers who support the frontline, and top management at the bottom, whose job is to hire and support good managers. Bezos: customer focus drives proactivity — "we invent before we have to."

Low customer-centricityHigh customer-centricity
Product drivenMarket driven
Mass market focusedCustomer focused
Process orientedOutcome oriented
Reacting to competitorsMaking competitors irrelevant
Price drivenValue driven
Hierarchical organizationTeamwork

Being customer-oriented is necessary but not enough: the firm must also be creative, since rivals copy advantages fast. Even strong companies miss trends this way — Nestlé missed the coffeehouse trend (Starbucks), and Coca-Cola was slow on Snapple-type drinks and Gatorade.

Organizing the marketing department

StructureHow it works
FunctionalSpecialists report to a CMO. The most common form. Simple to administer, but groups compete for budget and status.
GeographicSales (sometimes marketing) organized by region, with area market specialists for key markets.
Product / brandAn extra layer on top of functional, hub-and-spoke with the brand manager at the center. Fast response and a focused advocate for small brands, but little authority, little functional expertise, and short tenures that push toward short-term, market-share-driven thinking.
Category managementA variation on brand management: manage by product category (P&G, Diageo). Reduces internal brand-vs-brand rivalry and matches how powerful retailers like Walmart and Target actually think.
MarketOrganized around distinct user groups (Canon: consumer, business, government). Market managers are staff, not line. Xerox, IBM, and HP all reorganized by industry.
MatrixBoth product and market managers. Costly and prone to conflict over authority.

The CMO sits in the C-suite, usually reports to the CEO, and leads product development, brand, communication, research, sales, promotion, distribution, pricing, and service. Their single most important role: bringing the customer perspective into every touch point. David Packard's line still holds: "Marketing is far too important to leave to the marketing department" — CMOs coordinate through persuasion, not authority, and partner with the CFO on ROI measures.

Exam angleP&G's shift from brand management to category management is a favorite scenario: know why firms make that switch (retailers manage shelf space by category, not by brand, and category management cuts internal brand-vs-brand competition for resources) even though brand management gives a single product a more focused advocate.

Three nested levels

Planning happens at three levels, each nested inside the last: the corporate plan (mission & culture, define strategic business units, allocate resources across them), the business-unit plan (each SBU's own mission, strategy, and funding), and the market-offering plan — the actual marketing plan for one offering, built with G-STIC.

At the corporate level, a company does three planning activities: (1) define the corporate mission and culture, (2) establish strategic business units, (3) assign resources to each SBU.

Mission and culture

A mission is a clear, concise, and enduring statement of an organization's core purpose. Drucker's questions frame it: What is our business? Who is the customer? What is of value to the customer? What will our business be? What should our business be? At its best, a mission reflects a vision — an "impossible dream" giving direction for 10 to 20 years (Sony's "personal portable sound," FedEx's "by 10:30 AM"). Missions evolve too: Amazon went from the largest online bookstore to the largest online store, and Dunkin' went from doughnuts to coffee.

Good mission statements…Example
Focus on a limited number of specific goalsGoogle: organize the world's information and make it universally accessible and useful
Stress major policies and valuesTesla: accelerate the world's transition to sustainable energy
Define the major markets servedMicrosoft: empower every person and organization on the planet to achieve more
Take a long-term viewIKEA: a better everyday life for the many people
Are short, memorable, meaningfulMeta, Starbucks: community and human connection

Corporate culture — the shared experiences, stories, beliefs, and norms of an organization — is much harder to change than a structure or policy. As Frei & Morriss put it, "Culture guides discretionary behavior… it tells us what to do when the CEO isn't in the room." Southwest Airlines ranks employees first, customers second, shareholders third, and has had 47 consecutive profitable years.

Strategic business units and resource allocation

An SBU has three characteristics: it's a single business (or group of related businesses) that could stand on its own, it has its own competitors, and it has a manager responsible for strategic planning and profit. Portfolios can be specialized (Ferrari, GoPro — narrow product lines) or diversified (Microsoft, Nestlé — broad assortments, capturing growth where the company has no presence yet). Resource allocation weighs each SBU's competitive advantage against market attractiveness, and the decision per unit is grow, harvest, or hold. The BCG matrix — a 2×2 of market share vs. growth rate — is widely used but the textbook calls it "oversimplified and subjective." Some firms split units growing at very different rates: Kellogg became WK Kellogg Co. (cereal) and Kellanova (snacks, about 80% of revenue) in 2022.

Exam angleKnow the three SBU characteristics cold — a distractor answer often invents a fourth ("shares a single brand with the parent company") that isn't one of them.

Strategy vs. tactics

Strategy is choosing a well-defined target market (the 5 Cs) and the value proposition the company will create there (the 3 Vs). Tactics is the marketing mix that makes that strategy a market reality — the 7 Ts. The two are intertwined: you can't set features, price, or channels without knowing the target market's needs and the competing options.

Target market: the 5 Cs

CDefinition
CustomersIndividuals or organizations whose needs the company plans to fulfill. They must get superior value vs. competitors, and they must create value for the company in return.
CollaboratorsWork with the company to create value for customers — chosen for complementary resources the company lacks (suppliers, distributors, agencies).
CompetitorsAim to fulfill the same needs of the same customers. Avoid a myopic, category-based definition.
CompanyThe business unit that develops and manages the offering.
ContextSociocultural, technological, regulatory, economic, physical forces surrounding the market.

The key point: the choice of target customers determines everything else — change the target customer and you usually change the competitors, collaborators, required resources, and context. Unlike Porter's Five Forces, which defines the market by industry, the 5-C framework defines it by customer need — which is why "substitutes" isn't a separate category here: a substitute that meets the same need simply is a competitor.

Value proposition: the 3 Vs

The optimal value proposition balances three kinds of value: customer value (why customers choose this over alternatives), collaborator value (why partners work with this company instead of others), and company value (why invest in this rather than other opportunities) — balanced in a way that still lets the company reach its own goals. Starbucks example: customers get a functional benefit (coffee variety) plus a psychological one (expressing personality with a custom drink); coffee growers get payment and steady demand; Starbucks gets revenue, profit, brand, and market footprint.

Tactics: the 7 Ts

TacticWhat it does
ProductA marketable good; purchase gives ownership rights.
ServiceCreates value without ownership.
BrandIdentifies and differentiates; creates value beyond the product and service itself.
PriceThe monetary charge customers and collaborators pay.
IncentivesTools that enhance value by cutting costs or adding benefits — discounts, coupons, contests.
CommunicationInforms customers, collaborators, and stakeholders about the offering.
DistributionThe channels that deliver the offering.

The 7 Ts replaced the older 4 Ps (product, price, promotion, place) because the 4 Ps had three limitations: they didn't separate product from service; they treated brand as part of the product even though many firms outsource manufacturing and focus purely on the brand; and they lumped incentives and communication together as "promotion," even though incentives enhance value while communication only informs.

Exam angleIncentives vs. communication is the classic trap: a coupon (incentive) changes the value; an ad (communication) changes awareness. And remember why the 5-C framework has no "substitutes" category — it defines the market by customer need, not industry, so a substitute already counts as a competitor.

The five steps

StepKey content
GoalThe ultimate criterion of success: a focus (metric) plus performance benchmarks. Focus can be monetary (net income, ROI) or strategic (volume, awareness, culture). Benchmarks are quantitative (e.g., +5% share) and temporal (by when).
StrategyTarget market (5 Cs) + value proposition (3 Vs), often paired with a positioning statement.
TacticsThe 7 Ts that make the strategy a market reality.
ImplementationResource development (secure competencies and assets), offering development (turn strategy and tactics into the actual good), and commercial deployment (selective launch in a primary market, or a large-scale rollout).
ControlOne function: tell the company whether to stay the course, modify, or abandon. Two components: evaluate performance and monitor the environment.

A goal breaks into three parts — focus + quantitative benchmark + temporal benchmark. Example: net income (focus) of $40 million (quantitative) in one year (temporal). Amazon's Kindle illustrates a strategic, non-monetary goal: the company may break even on the device itself because it strengthens the broader retail platform.

The marketing plan

A marketing plan is narrower than a business plan (which also covers finance, operations, HR, technology). It has three functions — it describes the goal and course of action, informs stakeholders, and persuades decision makers — and four parts: the executive summary (the "elevator pitch"), a situation overview (company and market), G-STIC (the core), and exhibits (technical detail, tables, appendices). Firms often write specialized plans too — McDonald's writes separate plans for kids and parents, teens, and business customers.

Updating the plan and the marketing audit

Plans get updated whenever a G-STIC element must change, most often because one of the 5 Cs changed — Subway's "Subway Series" menu responded to changed customer preferences; Netflix's ad-supported tier made advertisers new collaborators.

A marketing audit is a comprehensive examination meant to find overlooked opportunities and problems. A good audit is:

CriterionMeaning
ComprehensiveCovers all major marketing activities, not just trouble spots — a narrower "functional" audit can miss root causes.
SystematicFollows G-STIC in order: goal, strategy, tactics, implementation, controls.
UnbiasedOften done by an outside party, since self-audits are too subjective.
PeriodicDone regularly, not only when there's a problem — healthy firms benefit too.
Exam anglePlan vs. audit is a favorite distinction: the plan looks forward and plots a course of action; the audit examines past and present performance to set the present course and secure the future. Also be ready to split any goal a question gives you into its three parts: focus, quantitative benchmark, temporal benchmark.

Culture: the broadest, deepest influence

An individualistic-vs-collectivistic study of 30,000 fashion shoppers across 30 countries found sharp differences: individualistic cultures (U.S., Australia) buy for themselves, follow trends, use many channels to find deals, and return items more often; collectivistic cultures (Portugal, Mexico, Turkey) follow the crowd, value long-term retailer reputation, shop for family, and prefer physical stores. Below culture sit subcultures (nationalities, religions, racial groups, regions) and social classes — relatively homogeneous, enduring, hierarchically ordered divisions whose members share values and behavior (rigidity varies; Brazil uses A–E segments).

Social factors

FactorKey points
Reference groupsAll groups with a direct or indirect effect on beliefs and behavior — including aspirational groups (hope to join) and dissociative groups (values you reject).
Opinion leadersInformal advice givers — confident, socially active, heavy users. With a large social following, they're influencers.
Family of orientationParents and siblings — shapes orientation toward religion, politics, ambition, self-worth.
Family of procreationSpouse and children — the more direct influence on everyday buying. Kids influence purchases directly ("I want McDonald's") and indirectly (parents know their preferences).

Personal factors

Personality is the set of distinguishing psychological traits leading to consistent, enduring responses. Self-concept comes in three flavors: consumers pick brands matching their actual self-concept, their ideal self-concept, or how they think others see them — the effect is strongest for publicly consumed products. Lifestyle is a pattern of activities, interests, and opinions (AIO): money-constrained consumers respond to low-cost offers (Walmart); time-constrained consumers respond to multitasking products. A "convenience involvement segment" wants convenience plus some involvement — Hamburger Helper is designed around needing at least one pan and about 15 minutes of prep, not zero effort.

Exam angleFamily of orientation (grew up in) vs. family of procreation (created) is an easy pair to swap under time pressure — procreation is the one with the more direct effect on day-to-day buying. Likewise, aspirational groups are ones you hope to join; dissociative groups are ones whose values you reject — don't mix them up.

Need → want → demand

A need is a basic requirement ("I'm hungry"). Shaped by society, it becomes a want directed at a specific object ("deep-dish pizza"). Add the ability to pay, and it becomes demand ("I can buy the $25 pizza"). Marketers don't create needs — needs exist before marketers do. Maslow's hierarchy orders needs from the ground up: physiological → safety → social → esteem → self-actualization. Satisfy the most pressing need first, then move up — a starving man ignores art until he's fed.

Perception's three processes

ProcessWhat it means
Selective attentionScreening out most stimuli, noticing what relates to a current need, is anticipated, or represents a large deviation ($100 off registers more than $5 off). There's no evidence subliminal messages control consumers.
Selective distortionInterpreting information to fit preconceptions — branded vs. blind taste tests differ; students called white wine dyed red "red." Helps strong brands.
Selective retentionRemembering the positives of liked brands and forgetting competitors' good points.

Memory: three types, one network

Long-term memory splits into episodic (events, with context), semantic (general facts and concepts), and procedural (how to do things, automatic). The associative network model treats memory as nodes joined by links of varying strength — a brand is a node, and brand associations are everything linked to it. Activation spreads outward from node to node when one is triggered; the strongest links get recalled first.

Behavioral economics: mental shortcuts

ConceptMeaning / example
Availability heuristicJudging likelihood by ease of recall (people think homicide kills more people than emphysema does).
Representativeness heuristicJudging by similarity to a stereotype, leading to base-rate neglect.
Conjunction fallacyBelieving two events together are more likely than one alone ("Linda is a bank teller and a feminist").
Decision framingHow choices are presented changes the decision — "pennies a day" instead of an annual cost.
Mental accountingTreating money as belonging to separate mental "accounts" (losing a $100 ticket feels different from losing $100 cash).
Choice architecture / nudgeThaler & Sunstein: designing the choice environment itself (Nabisco's 100-calorie packs).

The two routes of persuasion (ELM)

The Elaboration Likelihood Model asks whether the audience has motivation, ability, and opportunity to think about a message. If yes, they take the central route: careful, rational thought about key product information, producing a durable attitude. If any of the three is missing, they take the peripheral route: little thought, relying on cues like a celebrity or an emotional tone, producing a weaker, cue-dependent attitude.

Exam angleSelective attention, distortion, and retention sound similar but happen at different stages: attention is about what gets noticed, distortion is about how it gets interpreted, and retention is about what gets remembered afterward. A scenario about a consumer misremembering only a liked brand's strengths is retention, not distortion.

The five stages

StageKey content
1. Problem recognitionTriggered by internal stimuli (a need rises to a threshold) or external stimuli (an ad, a friend's purchase). Drivers: natural depletion, dissatisfaction, lifestyle changes, social influence.
2. Information searchFour sources: personal (family, friends), commercial (ads, salespeople), public (media, ratings), experiential (handling, using). Commercial sources give the most information; personal, experiential, and independent public sources are often the most effective.
3. Evaluation of alternativesDriven by beliefs and attitudes; a product is seen as a bundle of attributes. Too much choice can cause choice overload.
4. Purchase decisionUp to five sub-decisions: brand, dealer/channel, quantity, timing, payment method — shaped by intervening factors.
5. Postpurchase behaviorDissonance, satisfaction, postpurchase actions, use and disposal.

Consumers narrow choices through a successive sets funnel: total set (every brand that exists) → awareness set (brands they know) → consideration set (meet initial criteria) → choice set (the few strong contenders) → decision.

Scoring alternatives: the expectancy-value model

Consumers form attitudes by combining brand beliefs weighted by importance:

Perceived value = Σ (importance weight × belief score)

Worked example: weights are quality 50%, price 30%, design 20%. Brand X scores 6 on quality, 8 on price, 5 on design. Perceived value = 0.5(6) + 0.3(8) + 0.2(5) = 3.0 + 2.4 + 1.0 = 6.4. This model is compensatory — a strength on one attribute can offset a weakness on another — unlike non-compensatory rules, where attributes are judged in isolation and don't net out.

What can derail a purchase — and what happens after

Between evaluating alternatives and actually buying, two things can intervene: attitudes of others and unanticipated situational factors. Perceived risk can also make a buyer modify, postpone, or avoid a purchase entirely:

Risk typeThe fear that…
Functionalthe product won't perform as expected
Physicalit threatens health or well-being
Financialit isn't worth the price paid
Socialit causes embarrassment in front of others
Psychologicalit affects the user's mental well-being
Opportunityfailure means spending more time/money to find a better one

After the purchase, satisfaction is how closely perceived performance matches expectations: performance below expectations produces disappointment, performance that meets expectations produces satisfaction, and performance that exceeds expectations produces delight.

Exam angleKnow the order of the successive-sets funnel cold (total → awareness → consideration → choice → decision), and be ready to compute an expectancy-value score from given weights and belief scores — that's a near-certain calculation question.

The customer value equation

Customer value = (Total customer benefit − Total customer cost) vs. the same calculation for perceived alternatives

Total customer benefit is functional, psychological, and monetary benefits drawn from the product, service, and brand image. Total customer cost is the functional, psychological, and monetary costs of evaluating, obtaining, using, and disposing of the offering. Customer value isn't absolute — it's how much better the net benefit is than the alternatives'. To raise it, a company can add benefits or cut costs (price, ownership hassle, risk — e.g., via a warranty).

Three value domains

DomainDefinitionWhere it dominates
FunctionalBenefits and costs tied directly to performanceUtilitarian goods — office and industrial equipment (performance, reliability, durability, ease of use)
PsychologicalEmotional and self-expressive benefits and costsLuxury and fashion — the joy and status of a high-performance car
MonetaryFinancial benefits and costsCommoditized categories — price, fees, discounts, rebates, financing

Customer value analysis (4 steps)

(1) Identify the attributes and benefits customers value; (2) assess their relative importance — if ratings diverge widely, cluster respondents into segments; (3) assess the company's and competitors' performance on each one — winning on everything means the company can charge more, or charge the same and gain share; (4) monitor value over time.

Value proposition vs. positioning

The value proposition is the whole cluster of benefits a brand promises — Volvo promises safety, performance, design, and environmental responsibility. Positioning zeroes in on the key benefit(s) — for Volvo, that's safety alone: the single compelling reason to choose. A value proposition is also customer-specific — different segments may need distinct value propositions from the same brand.

Exam angleValue proposition vs. positioning is a classic trap: value proposition is the whole promised bundle; positioning is the narrower, key benefit used to win the comparison. Also know the three value domains and where each dominates — a luxury handbag question is testing psychological value, a bulk-industrial-supplies question is testing functional value.

The positioning statement

"For [target customers], [brand] offers [key benefits]."

Examples: "For busy professionals, Hertz offers a fast, convenient way to rent the right car at an airport." / "For safety-conscious families with higher income, Volvo offers the safest, most durable automobile." A fuller version adds supporting attributes, but the formula is incomplete on its own — it ignores weaknesses a brand must still neutralize. Positioning rests on two decisions: (1) the frame of reference, and (2) the points of parity and points of difference.

Choosing a frame of reference

The frame of reference is the benchmark used to judge value — it's tied to the target market and defines category membership, the close substitutes a brand competes against. Companies can choose a more advantageous frame: the UK Automobile Association positioned itself as the "fourth emergency service" (with police, fire, ambulance) for credibility and urgency; the International Federation of Match Poker framed poker as a "mind sport" like chess, not gambling.

The three Ds — criteria for a true point of difference (POD)

CriterionDetails
Desirable to consumersPersonally relevant, plus a compelling reason to believe
Deliverable by the companyFeasible and profitable to create and keep; ideal PODs are preemptive, defensible, hard to attack
Differentiating from competitorsDistinctive and superior

All three must overlap for a real POD — e.g., Splenda's "made from sugar" beat Equal and Sweet'N Low on differentiation; Monster's 16-oz can and broad line differentiated it from Red Bull.

Points of parity (POPs) — three types

TypeDefinitionExample
CategoryAttributes essential to being a legitimate, credible category member — necessary but not sufficient (the "greens fees")A travel agency must be able to book air and hotel
CorrelationalA potential negative arising from your own positive attribute"Inexpensive" implies not "highest quality"
CompetitiveDesigned to overcome a perceived weakness vs. a competitor's POD; find by role-playing rivals' positioningVisa adds gold/platinum cards for prestige; AmEx expands merchant acceptance

A POP does not mean "equal to competitors" — it means "good enough" that the attribute stops counting against you. Often the key to winning positioning is achieving parity, not difference: Miller Lite paired "tastes great" (a POP vs. regular beer) with "less filling" (its real POD).

The 80-20 rule: positioning doesn't have to fit every product

A single positioning statement is meant to fit roughly 80% of a brand's products — not all of them. Trying to write one positioning that accurately covers 100% of a broad product line forces it toward the lowest common denominator: so vague and hedge-everything that it no longer says anything distinctive. It's normal, and expected, for a brand's positioning to fit its core lineup well while a handful of edge-case products sit a little outside it.

Exam angleCorrelational POPs come from your own strengths (a side effect you must neutralize); competitive POPs come from rivals' strengths (an attack you must blunt). Also know straddle positioning: BMW's POD vs. Corvette is luxury (its POP there is performance); vs. Cadillac its POD is performance (its POP there is luxury) — the POD in one frame becomes the POP in the other. The risk of straddling badly: Palm Pilot and Apple Newton tried to straddle pagers and laptops and ended up credible in neither. And remember the 80-20 rule: a positioning statement that tries to fit 100% of the product line usually ends up watered down.

The brand positioning bullseye (center out)

(1) Brand mantra at the center → (2) POPs and PODs, stated as customer benefits → (3) Substantiators / reasons to believe, the facts backing them up → (4) Brand values, personality, character (intangible) and executional properties / visual identity (tangible — name, logo, colors). Hypothetical Starbucks bullseye: mantra "Rich, rewarding coffee experience"; PODs = fresh high-quality coffee, variety of espresso drinks, fast personalized service; POPs = convenient locations, fair prices, socially responsible; substantiators = integrated supply chain, barista training, generous benefits; personality = contemporary, thoughtful, caring; visual identity = name, siren logo, green & white.

Crafting the brand mantra

A brand mantra is 3–5 words, the internal "heart and soul" of the brand — distinct from a slogan, which is external and consumer-facing. Nike's mantra is "authentic athletic performance" (its slogan is "Just Do It"); Disney's is "fun family entertainment." Three criteria for a good mantra: it communicates what's unique (may define the category or set boundaries), simplifies the brand's essence (short, crisp, vivid), and inspires (meaningful to customers and employees alike). For a mantra to work, no other brand should excel on all its dimensions.

The core idea

Successful products are differentiated from competitors in one or more ways that customers value and that are hard to copy. A product with no real difference has only one lever left: price. Competing on price means low margins and weak bargaining power with the trade (the retailers and distributors who decide what goes on the shelf).

The slides add a warning: a brand can look strong because it is selling, while its margins are quietly collapsing.

Worked example: Levi’s (from the slides)

How should management respond? The slides’ answer: go back to the core brand values and authenticity no one else could claim. A historically focused line, LVC, priced at $250+, “supports” the whole brand. Few people buy it, but it builds an emotional connection and “pulls” the brand image upward, especially when digital influencers wear it. Think of it as a halo product: its job is image, not volume.

Why “me-too” products fail

The alternative to differentiation is a head-to-head “me-too” challenge. These often fail even with big budgets; new-product failure rates run as high as 90%. The slides’ example: Pepsi’s Mountain Dew against Coke’s string of challengers (Mellow Yellow, Surge, Vault).

Work with something that is already in the consumer’s mind, so your message gets noticed and remembered despite the clutter. Two routes from the slides: influencer campaigns (borrow someone else’s audience and credibility) and positioning against the market leader (define yourself relative to a brand people already know). Exam angleUndifferentiated = compete on price = low margins and weak trade leverage. Product features and price are easy to copy; brand image is hard to copy but needs frequency and consistency. Low budget? Use influencers or position against the leader.

Very few products appeal to everyone. Successful companies focus on particular segments or niches so their product can be the very best for that group, instead of average for everyone.

“Make what will sell versus sell what you make.” The slides illustrate this with GM and BMW both wanting a younger buyer. The point is that wanting a segment is not enough: the product and message have to be designed for what that segment values, not pushed onto it.

Retargeting (the slides show a Facebook sponsored post) is target marketing at the level of one person: someone who has already shown interest, for example by visiting your site, is shown your ads again elsewhere.

Exam angleBe able to list the benefits of targeting (less competition, higher margins, loyalty and repeat purchase, focused design, focused communications, lower marketing cost) and explain “make what will sell.”

Marketers need to understand both how consumers should behave (the rational model) and how they actually behave (with predictable biases).

From the slides: a clearly dominated option “attracts” interest toward the option that beats it, in the slide’s example toward the high-end choice. Adding an option nobody should pick changes what people do pick.

The five-stage buyer decision process

We often assume buyers move through a “rational” five-stage process. The value of the model is that it lets you target buyers at different stages with different messages.

Consideration set: consumers often decide hierarchically, narrowing to a few brands to limit how much information they must process. If your brand is not in the consideration set, nothing else about it matters.

Base cost and the 85% experience curve

Base cost is the average transfer price (your cost of goods sold) for the first 2.5 million units. Produce more and unit cost falls; produce fewer and it rises. On an 85% experience curve, cost falls to 85% of its previous level every time cumulative production doubles.

Budget, production and lost sales

  • Budget: you allocate a limited budget across advertising (media and research), the commercial team (salaries and merchandising), R&D, and market research. Next period’s budget is 40% of net contribution (EBT), with a floor and a ceiling. For example, a net contribution of $10M would give a $4M budget, if that falls between the floor and ceiling (the slides do not give their values).
  • You don’t pay for production until units are sold.
  • Production is automatically adjusted ±20% against your plan. Plan 1.0M units and the system can produce between 0.8M and 1.2M to follow demand; demand beyond that is lost.
  • Purchase intent vs. market share: if a brand’s share of purchase intentions is well above its market share, the gap hints at lost sales, from stock-outs or weak distribution, that went to competitors, future orders, or out of the industry.

Marketing research studies

Consumer survey (awareness, purchase intentions, shopping habits); consumer panel (sales, market share, new vs. repeat sales, which indicates loyalty and satisfaction); distribution panel (sales and coverage by channel; %ACV = volume-weighted percent of outlets carrying your brand); semantic scales (the perceptual map: brand perceptions, each segment’s ideal point, attribute importance); multidimensional scaling (a similarity-based map that reduces many dimensions to a few); market forecast; competitive advertising, commercial team and merchandising estimates; advertising, commercial-team and merchandising experiments; conjoint analysis; industry benchmarking. The slides note there are two ways to reposition a brand on the perceptual map; the slide content for that is an image not in our copy (in practice: change the product through R&D, or change perceptions through advertising’s perceptual objectives, the Unit 3 decision).

A preparation routine using this week’s frameworks

  1. Situation: who is the decision maker, what decision is due, and by when?
  2. Market: the 5 Cs (customers, company, collaborators, competitors, context) from Chapter 2.
  3. Customer: where in the five-stage process or journey is the target, and is it a high- or low-involvement purchase?
  4. Offering: what is the value proposition and positioning (frame of reference, points of parity, points of difference from Chapter 7)?
  5. Options and numbers: list two or three real alternatives and the arithmetic each needs (margins, break-even; see Unit 2).
  6. Recommendation: pick one, say why, and say what would change your mind.

Ch 3 · Buying Decision Process
Five purchase sub-decisions
Brand, dealer/channel, quantity, timing, payment method.
Ch 3 · Buying Decision Process
Six types of perceived risk
Functional, physical, financial, social, psychological, opportunity (time/cost of finding a better alternative).
Ch 3 · Buying Decision Process
Satisfaction model
Performance below expectations → disappointment; meets expectations → satisfaction; exceeds expectations → delight.
Ch 3 · Buying Decision Process
Postpurchase behavior
Covers cognitive dissonance, satisfaction, postpurchase actions, and product use/disposal.
Ch 3 · Buying Decision Process
Product as a bundle of attributes
In the evaluation stage, consumers see a product as a bundle of attributes, driven by their beliefs and attitudes about each.
Ch 3 · Buying Decision Process
Customer value equation
(Total customer benefit − total customer cost), compared to the same calculation for perceived alternatives.
Ch 7 · Value Proposition
Total customer benefit
Functional, psychological, and monetary benefits drawn from the product, service, and brand image.
Ch 7 · Value Proposition
Total customer cost
Functional, psychological, and monetary costs of evaluating, obtaining, using, and disposing of an offering.
Ch 7 · Value Proposition
Three value domains
Functional (performance-driven), psychological (emotional/self-expressive), monetary (financial).
Ch 7 · Value Proposition
Functional value
Benefits and costs tied directly to performance; dominates utilitarian goods like office and industrial equipment.
Ch 7 · Value Proposition
Psychological value
Emotional and self-expressive benefits and costs; dominates luxury and fashion.
Ch 7 · Value Proposition
Monetary value
Financial benefits and costs (price, fees, discounts, financing); dominates commoditized categories.
Ch 7 · Value Proposition
Customer value analysis (4 steps)
Identify attributes/benefits, assess their relative importance, assess company vs. competitor performance, monitor value over time.
Ch 7 · Value Proposition
Customer value proposition
The whole cluster of benefits a brand promises (Volvo: safety + performance + design + environment).
Ch 7 · Value Proposition
Positioning
The key benefit(s) a brand emphasizes — the compelling reason to choose it (Volvo: safety alone).
Ch 7 · Value Proposition
Value proposition vs. positioning
Value proposition = the whole promised cluster; positioning = the key benefit(s) that provide the compelling reason to choose.
Ch 7 · Value Proposition
Positioning statement
"For [target customers], [brand] offers [key benefits]."
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Two positioning decisions
(1) Choose a frame of reference; (2) choose the points of parity and points of difference.
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Frame of reference
The benchmark used to judge a brand's value; tied to the target market and defining category membership.
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Category membership
The close substitutes a brand competes against within its chosen frame of reference.
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Point of difference (POD)
A strongly held, positive, and unique brand association versus competitors.
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
The three Ds (POD criteria)
Desirable to consumers, deliverable by the company, differentiating from competitors.
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Preemptive & defensible POD
The ideal deliverable POD — feasible, profitable, and hard for competitors to attack.
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Point of parity (POP)
A shared association where the brand just needs to be "good enough" — not necessarily equal to competitors.
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Category POP
Attributes essential to legitimate category membership — necessary but not sufficient (the "greens fees").
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Correlational POP
A potential negative arising from your own positive attribute (e.g., "inexpensive" implies not "highest quality").
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Competitive POP
Designed to overcome a perceived weakness against a competitor's POD (Visa adds prestige cards; AmEx expands acceptance).
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Miller Lite example
"Tastes great" = POP vs. regular beer; "less filling" = the real POD.
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Straddle positioning
The POD in one frame of reference becomes the POP in another (BMW: luxury vs. Corvette, performance vs. Cadillac).
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Risk of straddling
Done poorly, a brand ends up credible in neither category (Palm Pilot, Apple Newton straddling pagers and laptops).
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Brand substitution test
Swap a rival's brand into your positioning statement; if it still reads true, your positioning is too weak or generic.
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
The 80-20 rule (positioning)
A positioning statement should fit about 80% of a brand's products; forcing it to cover 100% waters it down to the lowest common denominator.
Ch 7 · Frame, POPs & PODs
Brand mantra
A 3–5 word internal statement of the brand's "heart and soul" — for employees and partners, not consumers.
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
Mantra vs. slogan
Mantra is internal (Nike: "authentic athletic performance"); slogan is external, consumer-facing ("Just Do It").
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
Three criteria for a good mantra
Communicates what's unique, simplifies the brand's essence, inspires customers and employees.
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
Brand positioning bullseye (4 layers)
Center-out: brand mantra → POPs/PODs (as benefits) → substantiators/reasons to believe → values/personality (intangible) and visual identity (tangible).
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
Reason to believe (substantiator)
The facts that back up a POP or POD claim (e.g., Mountain Dew's caffeine content, NIVEA's coenzyme Q10).
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
Brand personality (5 dimensions)
Sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, ruggedness.
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
Three ways to convey category membership
Announce category benefits, compare to exemplars, or rely on the product descriptor.
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
Claiming a non-member category
Borrowing a category's cachet while distinguishing the brand from it ("It's Not Delivery, It's DiGiorno!"; "It's Not TV, It's HBO").
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
Five solutions to conflicting benefits
(1) A product that performs well on both — strongest; (2) branding; (3) two separate campaigns; (4) link to a person/place/thing with the right equity; (5) reframe the negative relationship as positive.
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
GORE-TEX example
Breathable and waterproof in one product — the strongest of the five conflicting-benefits solutions.
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
Perceptual (positioning) map
A visual, quantitative picture of how consumers see brands on key dimensions; overlaying preferences reveals unmet-need "holes."
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
Primal branding
Treating brands as belief systems with a "primal code" (Google, MINI, the Marines, Apple).
Ch 7 · Mantra & Bullseye
Differentiation
Being different from competitors in ways customers value and rivals find hard to imitate; the escape from price competition.
Unit 1 · Branding & Differentiation
Undifferentiated product
Must compete on price: low margins and weak bargaining power with the trade.
Unit 1 · Branding & Differentiation
“Me-too” product
Head-to-head copy of a leader; often fails even with a big budget (e.g. Coke’s Surge, Vault, Mellow Yellow vs. Mountain Dew).
Unit 1 · Branding & Differentiation
Levi’s LVC line
Historically focused $250+ line that “supports” and pulls up the whole brand image; authenticity no one else has.
Unit 1 · Branding & Differentiation
Positioning to market leaders
Low-budget route: define your brand relative to one already in the consumer’s mind so the message cuts through clutter.
Unit 1 · Branding & Differentiation
Target marketing
Focusing on particular segments or niches so the product can be the very best for them.
Unit 1 · Target Marketing
Benefits of targeting
Less competition, higher margins, higher loyalty and repeat purchase, focused design, focused communications, lower marketing costs.
Unit 1 · Target Marketing
Retargeting
Showing ads again to a person who already showed interest (e.g. visited the site), as with a Facebook sponsored post.
Unit 1 · Target Marketing
Attraction (decoy) effect
A clearly dominated option draws choice toward the option that dominates it, often the high-end one.
Unit 1 · Decision Biases & Involvement
Consideration set
The few brands a buyer seriously evaluates; a hierarchical shortcut that limits information processing.
Unit 1 · Decision Biases & Involvement
Selective attention / retention / distortion
Noticing, remembering, and interpreting information in line with existing attitudes.
Unit 1 · Decision Biases & Involvement
Mental accounting
Treating money in separate “accounts” (e.g. “I haven’t spent on entertainment lately”), which can justify a purchase.
Unit 1 · Decision Biases & Involvement
Satisficing
Choosing a good-enough option with minimal effort; typical of low-involvement purchases (vs. optimizing).
Unit 1 · Decision Biases & Involvement
Low involvement
Minimal effort, in-store decision, 1–2 brands, brand critical, ads emphasize frequency, intensive distribution.
Unit 1 · Decision Biases & Involvement
High involvement
Considerable effort, 4–6 products considered, optimizing, ads emphasize reach (print, search), selective distribution.
Unit 1 · Decision Biases & Involvement
Clinites
Markstrat’s B2C cosmetics market: frequently purchased; five segments, four channels, six attributes.
Unit 1 · Markstrat Basics
Base cost
Average transfer price (COGS) for the first 2.5 million units.
Unit 1 · Markstrat Basics
85% experience curve
Unit cost falls to 85% of its prior level each time cumulative production doubles.
Unit 1 · Markstrat Basics
Markstrat budget rule
Next period budget = 40% of net contribution (EBT), with a floor and a ceiling.
Unit 1 · Markstrat Basics
Production adjustment
Production is automatically adjusted ±20% against your plan; you pay for units only when sold.
Unit 1 · Markstrat Basics
Distribution margin (Markstrat)
Channel’s cut as a % of actual retail price: 30% mass, 35% specialized and portals, 40% department stores.
Unit 1 · Markstrat Basics
Unit gross contribution
Selling price to the channel minus transfer cost (e.g. $9.45 − $4.75 = $4.70 in mass merchandisers).
Unit 1 · Markstrat Basics
%ACV
Volume-weighted percent of outlets that carry your brand (distribution panel).
Unit 1 · Markstrat Basics
Semantic scales study
Gives the perceptual map: brand perceptions, each segment’s ideal points, attribute importance.

Case method routine
Situation → market (5 Cs) → customer → offering → options with numbers → recommendation.
