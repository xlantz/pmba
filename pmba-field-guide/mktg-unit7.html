Walk the material

Chapter 13, section by section

Integrated marketing communications (IMC) manages a campaign through the coordinated use of different communication tools that work in concert and reinforce one another to reach the company’s strategic goals, consistently and cost-efficiently. Without it, communication becomes a random set of unrelated (even conflicting) messages from creative teams working in isolation, which confuses rather than informs and persuades.

Opening example: as post-pandemic sales slid, Quaker launched its global “You’ve Got This” campaign in February 2024, built on research into breakfast routines. A father and son share oatmeal before life’s milestones. The campaign ran across TV, out-of-home, print, digital, mobile, social and cinema, with one platform adapted to local cultures (in Mexico the oats went into a smoothie).

Four levels of integration

Six criteria for a truly integrated program

CriterionQuestion it asks
CoverageWhat share of the audience does each option reach, and how much do the options overlap?
ContributionHow much does one option produce the desired response on its own, without exposure to any other?
CommonalityDo the options reinforce the same associations (shared meaning, a cohesive image)?
ComplementarityDo the options emphasize different associations that together build the full position? (Cause sponsorship for trust; TV and print for performance.)
ConformabilityDoes the option work both for people who have seen the other communications and for those who haven’t?
CostWeigh all of the above against cost to get the most effective and efficient program.
Examples from the chapter

Heinz “Ketchup A.I.”: an AI image generator, prompted with anything from “Renaissance ketchup bottle” to “ketchup tarot card,” kept drawing Heinz bottles. Fans suggested prompts; the images ran on social, in a long video, in a real and metaverse art gallery, on custom bottles and in print and out-of-home. The 18 OOH boards delivered over 55 million impressions, and social engagement was 38% higher than past campaigns. Giant Spoon (agency): a takeover of an entire New York Times edition for GE’s split (22 full-page ads) and a Stranger Things projection on the Empire State Building with more than 200 million impressions.

Source note: the extract says Heinz earned “over 115 billion” impressions, which looks like an OCR error (possibly 1.15 billion), so it is not used here. Check the book.

The caveat: marketers only have so much control. Consumers have a voice in what a brand means. Timberland’s rugged work boot became part of early-1990s hip-hop culture through none of its own marketing.

Exam angleMatch a description to horizontal, vertical, internal or external integration. Tell commonality (same associations) from complementarity (different associations that add up), and know conformability (works for new and repeat viewers).

Advertising is any presentation and promotion of ideas, goods, services or brands by an identified sponsor using paid media (time on TV and radio, space in print).

AI and advertising

  • Agencies (WPP, Publicis, Omnicom) use AI for insights, copy, campaign execution, new-business briefs and routine tasks like parts of media buying.
  • Clients see it as an efficiency tool: L’Oréal generated description pages for thousands of products for retailers worldwide; Trivago ran a global campaign with one actor whose voice AI adapted into 10 languages, cutting production time in half (past campaigns needed up to 20 spokespeople and 35 productions).
  • Human + AI examples: La-Z-Boy’s “Decliner” texted AI-generated excuses to skip plans; Zipcar used AI headlines its agency tweaked; Under Armour combined sports-psychology research with AI to write a motivational speech.
  • Drawbacks: may not produce breakthrough “big ideas”; brand safety (KFC Germany’s semi-automated Kristallnacht promotion); ethics and transparency (LEGO apologized for AI images that broke its own policy); copyright and attribution; agencies fear clients will take AI in-house, and Google, Meta, ByteDance, Alibaba and Amazon are now the five biggest global ad sellers.

The traditional media compared

MediumStrengthsWeaknesses
TelevisionMost powerful medium; vividly demonstrates attributes and benefits; portrays user imagery and brand personality; captive audiences at live events (Super Bowl, Oscars)Fleeting; creative can overshadow the brand; clutter; streaming and ad-free services give viewers control

Aflac shows TV’s power: the squawking duck campaign raised sales 28% in its first year and name recognition from 13% to 91%; the duck joined the corporate logo in 2005 and also drives sales in Japan, Aflac’s biggest market.

More than half of U.S. consumers’ total media time is spent online, so marketers must go there, but customers set the rules of engagement.

Online ads

  • Advantages: effects are traceable (unique visitors, clicks, time, what they do, where they go next), so messages can be tested and optimized; contextual placement on related sites; keyword ads reach people already in the buying process; any format from text to interactive.
  • Disadvantages: consumers screen most messages out; bogus clicks from software inflate apparent results; brand safety (ads next to offensive content); loss of control (hacking, vandalism). The online share of advertising has still grown steadily.
  • Native advertising: paid messages that match the look and feel of the publication, designed to be non-disruptive. Three common formats: (1) content recommendations below an article, (2) in-feed ads in social news feeds, (3) promoted search listings above organic results. It also appears in print, TV and radio.

The company website

A site should express the company’s purpose, history, products and vision, and be attractive on first view and worth revisiting. Visitors judge it on ease of use (easy to understand and navigate) and aesthetic appeal (layout, fonts, color). J.D. Power found consumers “delighted” with an automaker’s site were more likely to take a test drive. Microsites supplement the main site, e.g. an insurer’s used-car-buying advice page on used-car sites.

Driving traffic: SEO vs. SEM

Keyword guidance: broad terms (“iPhone,” “burger”) build the brand; specific ones (“Apple iPhone 14 Pro Max”) generate and convert leads. Bid on each keyword according to its probable return.

Exam angleSEO = improve your rank in non-paid results (cheaper); SEM = pay to appear for keywords (pay per click). Name the three native-ad formats and the online-ad downsides (bogus clicks, brand safety).

Social media let consumers share text, images, audio and video with each other and with companies, and vice versa. They give a brand a public voice, cheaply reinforce other communications, keep companies current, and let them build communities that become long-term assets. In 2024 there were over 5.2 billion social media users, with 13 new accounts every second. But they are rarely the sole source of a brand’s communications, and only some consumers want to engage with some brands, some of the time.

Challenges: “always-on” consumers expect near-instant responses; companies take the good with the bad (Frito-Lay’s “Do Us a Flavor” app crashed on day one under heavy traffic, then recovered). A strong community will often defend the brand against unfair criticism.

Four social media platforms

PlatformKey pointExample
Online communities & forumsTwo-way flow gives hard-to-get insight; success needs activities that bond membersApple’s product discussion groups; GSK’s weight-loss community before launching Alli; LEGO’s crowdsourced designs (Minecraft sets)
BlogsRegularly updated journals; bring together people with common interestsThe FTC requires bloggers to disclose relationships with marketers
Social networksHuge audiences, but users are there to connect, not shop; ads can land beside offensive content; few posts “cascade” beyond the first recipientFacebook, X, YouTube, WhatsApp, Instagram, TikTok, WeChat
Customer reviewsOften the second most trusted source of brand information, after friends and family; later raters are swayed more by earlier positive ratings; yet negative reviews often carry more weight than positive onesOnline “influentials” who sway particular consumers are especially valuable

People share mostly for extrinsic reasons (social and self-image) more than intrinsic ones (fun, learning). Viral marketing follows the rules of entertainment more than selling (Walmart’s money-saving videos, Ocean Spray’s TikTok hits), and depends on consumers being willing to pass it on.

Influencer marketing

Using a popular online figure to promote an offering within their social circle: in effect a mix of publicity and paid endorsement, delivered through the influencer’s own networks. Especially important for beauty, clothing, food and tech, and for reaching Millennials and Gen Z.

  • What makes them effective: originality, follower size, and sponsor salience (how prominent the brand is in the post). They are less effective for new-product launches.
  • Costs (chapter figures): mega-influencers (over 1 million followers) about $15,000 per Instagram deal, though $5,000–$10,000 is more typical; fees average $100 per 10,000 followers; a sponsored YouTube video about $4,500, TikTok video about $3,500, Instagram post about $1,100; top influencers over $100,000.
  • TikTok: high engagement (#TikTokMadeMeBuyIt), but overtly commercial posts are poorly received: “Don’t make ads, make TikToks.” TV ads can’t simply be reused.
  • Fraud: over 60% of Instagram mega-influencers inflated their engagement and followers; 49.23% of influencers were involved in fraud in 2021; one company ran 3.5 million automated accounts. Advertisers now hire “social media detectives” to check for bots.
  • Examples: Neutrogena’s “Hydro House” TikTok series; L’Oréal’s expert creators for CeraVe; Tabasco with lifestyle creator Tinx (1.5 million followers) on a hot-sauce dressing; Starbucks with freestyle rapper Harry Mack.
Exam angleName the four platforms; know that reviews are the second most trusted source and negatives weigh more; list the three drivers of influencer effectiveness and their weak spot (new launches); explain influencer fraud.

U.S. consumers spend more time on mobile devices than on radio, magazines and newspapers combined.

  • Mobile ads can be more than static “mini-billboards”: apps add convenience, social value, incentives and entertainment.
  • Mobile boosts loyalty programs (track visits and purchases, earn rewards) and enables location-specific promotions to customers who opt in.
  • With traditional coupon redemption declining for years, mobile coupons can reach shoppers at or near the point of purchase, and cross-screen identities (with privacy safeguards) allow more relevant targeting.
  • The small screen and fleeting attention make informing and persuading harder, though consumers are more engaged on smartphones than when online; some m-commerce firms drop ads entirely to minimize clicks to purchase.
Exam angleList the four mobile characteristics and the uses they enable (loyalty, geolocation offers, mobile coupons); note the small-screen limitation.

Becoming part of a personally relevant moment can broaden and deepen a brand’s relationship with its target. Event marketing uses exhibits, displays and presentations, in person or livestreamed.

Risks: results can be unpredictable and outside the sponsor’s control, and some consumers resent commercialized events.

Experiential marketing

It communicates features and benefits and connects the offering to unique experiences, letting customers feel how it fits their lives rather than being sold to. Choosing a sponsorship: the event must meet the brand’s objectives and strategy, have enough awareness, the right image and the desired effects; its audience must match the target and view the sponsor favorably. The ideal event is unique, not crowded with sponsors, supports other marketing activities, and enhances the brand. Other experiences: company tours (Ben & Jerry’s, Boeing, Crayola, Hershey’s), corporate museums (Hallmark, Kohler, Beiersdorf/NIVEA), and off-site brand venues (World of Coca-Cola, M&M’S Stores).

Exam angleMatch a sponsorship to one of the seven objectives; list the criteria for choosing an event (fit, awareness, image, audience match, uniqueness, few sponsors).

Consumers talk about dozens of brands a day. Hush Puppies, Krispy Kreme, Crocs, Red Bull, Starbucks and Amazon were built through strong word of mouth (WOM). Viral marketing is WOM that encourages consumers to pass along company-developed audio, video or written content online.

Dollar Shave Club (chapter example)

A quirky 90-second launch video, with CEO Michael Dubin riding a forklift and dancing with a fuzzy bear while touting quality, convenience and price, drew millions of YouTube views. While signing up several hundred thousand customers, the company raised more than $20 million in venture capital, and it was later sold to Unilever for $1 billion. Dubin: “a funny video… tapped the pain point for a lot of consumers.”

What research says about WOM

  • Products don’t have to be outrageous to get buzz. Interesting brands are talked about more online; novelty has little effect on face-to-face talk, where salient, visible brands that come easily to mind dominate.
  • People generate positive WOM about their own good experiences, but pass on negative WOM mostly about others’ bad experiences they heard about.
Exam angleDefine viral marketing and earned media; contrast online vs. offline WOM drivers (interesting vs. salient); and the own-positive / others-negative pattern.

Publicity promotes the company and its offerings through editorial space (news stories, articles, editorials) rather than paid space, aiming to attract attention. Public relations (PR) goes further: managing the overall reputation of the company and its offerings and building relationships with the community and other stakeholders.

Publicity: upsidePublicity: downside
Free media (you may pay an agency, but not for the time or space); more credible because a third party is the source. Some experts say consumers are five times more likely to be influenced by editorial copy than by advertising.Can’t be directly controlled: it may be irrelevant or even hurt the company. Unpredictability is the price of low cost and high credibility.

Publicity’s objectives: build awareness, build credibility, boost sales force and dealer enthusiasm, hold down promotion cost. Tasks it does well: launching new products (LeapFrog, Beanie Babies, Silly Bandz); repositioning mature products (“I Love New York”); building interest in a category (milk, beef, potatoes); defending products in trouble (Tylenol, Toyota, BP); building the corporate image (Steve Jobs’s Macworld keynotes).

PR departments: three functions, six media formats

Three functionsSix PR media formats
Press coverage (news in the best light); corporate communications (internal and external understanding); lobbying (legislators and officials)Publications (annual reports, brochures, newsletters); events (news conferences, trade shows, contests); news (press releases, conferences); speeches; public service activities; identity media (logos, stationery, signs, uniforms, buildings)

The best PR departments counsel management to drop questionable practices so negative publicity never arises, and PR is especially important for limiting and repairing damage in a crisis.

Fenty Beauty at the 2023 Super Bowl (chapter example)

In her halftime set, Rihanna paused to touch up her nose with a $44 Fenty Beauty powder. Searches for the brand rose 833% after the game, and one source estimated $11.3M in media impact value for Fenty Beauty and $4.2M for Savage x Fenty in the following week, amplified by her brands’ social channels and Super Bowl-themed products.

Exam anglePublicity = free editorial space, credible, uncontrollable; PR = managing reputation and stakeholder relationships. Know the three PR functions and six media formats.

Frameworks that apply

  • Marginal analysis (Unit 2): move the next dollar to the channel with the highest marginal return until returns are equal; watch for diminishing returns.
  • The six IMC criteria (this chapter): coverage and overlap, each channel’s stand-alone contribution, commonality vs. complementarity, conformability, cost.
  • Measurability of digital (this chapter): online effects are traceable, but bogus clicks and the difference between correlation and causation matter.
  • Paid, owned, earned: which spending creates earned media, and how would you credit it?
  • CLTV (Unit 4): allocate toward channels that bring customers worth the most over time, not just the cheapest clicks.

Discussion questions

  • Which channels does the case let you compare, and what is each one’s cost per result?
  • Is the data showing marginal or average returns? Why does the difference matter for reallocating budget?
  • Which channels complement each other (so cutting one hurts another)?
  • How would you test a reallocation before committing the whole budget?
  • What would you recommend, and how would you measure whether it worked?
