The Accounting Cycle, Adjustments & the Closing Process

Step 1 · Start here

Accounting is a record-keeping loop with two sides to everything

Every period a company runs the same four-step loop: record what happened, fix what the records missed, report the results, then reset the scoreboard. Every entry has a left side (debit) and a right side (credit) that must match.

The basketball-season analogy

During a game you track points on a scoreboard. When the buzzer sounds, you check the final stats (did someone's basket get missed?), announce the score, then reset the scoreboard to 0–0 for the next game — but the result is added to the team's season record, which never resets. In accounting, revenues and expenses are the scoreboard (temporary: they reset every period). Retained earnings is the season record (permanent: it carries forward). “Closing the books” is the buzzer-and-reset step. Adjusting entries are the stat-check before the score is official.

Six words to know before anything else

Debit / Credit
Just left and right. Debit = left side of an entry, credit = right side. Not “good” and “bad”.
Journal entry
The written record of one transaction, with debits and credits that are equal.
T-account
A simple “T” that tracks one account: debits on the left, credits on the right.
Accrual accounting
Count revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, not when cash moves.
Adjusting entry
An end-of-period entry that fixes the timing gap between cash and earning/incurring.
Closing
Moving the period's revenue, expense and dividend totals into retained earnings so they start the next period at $0.

Tiny example: one month at Brightside Consulting

The owner invests $10,000 cash. The firm does $4,000 of consulting for cash, pays $1,500 of wages, and at month-end owes another $500 of wages it hasn't paid yet (the adjusting entry).

Cash = 10,000 + 4,000 − 1,500 = 12,500
Wages owed = 500 (liability)
Wages exp. = 1,500 paid + 500 owed = 2,000
Net income = 4,000 − 2,000 = 2,000
Equity = 10,000 + 2,000 = 12,000
Check: Assets 12,500 = 500 + 12,000 ✓

Without the $500 adjustment, expenses look like $1,500, net income like $2,500, and liabilities like $0 — all wrong. Closing then moves the $4,000 revenue and $2,000 wages into retained earnings (+$2,000) and resets both to $0.

Big idea

The accounting cycle is the process of recording events that happened during an accounting period. It's four steps, always in this order:

  1. Analyze transactions, prepare entries, and post entries
  2. Prepare and post accounting adjustments
  3. Prepare financial statements
  4. Close the books to set up the next accounting period
Adjustments happen before statements are prepared, and closing happens after. A common trap is assuming statements are prepared last.

Three cycle tools (different lenses, same transactions)

  • Financial Statement Effects Template (FSET) — captures transactions across all four financial statements at once; facilitates analysis and interpretation.
  • T-Accounts — expand each account into increases and decreases; can be used to keep running totals.
  • Journal Entries — capture the effects of transactions using Debits (DR) and Credits (CR).

These aren't competing methods — a well-rounded understanding of the cycle means being able to represent a transaction all three ways.

Exam angleIf asked to put steps of the cycle in order, or to identify which step a given action belongs to, anchor on the four-step sequence above.

The FSET equation

Built directly on the accounting equation, expanded to show how the Income Statement flows into Equity:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Cash Asset + Noncash Asset = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Earned Capital
Revenues − Expenses = Net Income → Earned Capital (as Retained Earnings)

Key insight: this single equation is the connective tissue between the Income Statement and the Balance Sheet — Net Income flows directly into Earned Capital/Retained Earnings, exactly the linkage covered in Unit 2's "statement linkages" table. The FSET makes that linkage mechanically explicit, transaction by transaction.

T-Accounts & natural balances

T-Accounts capture increases and decreases to individual accounts. The natural balances follow directly from the accounting equation:

Assets (natural debit) = Liabilities (natural credit) + Equity (natural credit)

In a journal entry: Debit = left, Credit = right — a convention that never changes.

What increases and decreases each account type

Every account has a "natural" side — the side that increases it. The other side always decreases it. Revenue and expense accounts follow from the FSET equation above: since Revenues − Expenses = Net Income flows into Equity, revenue behaves like equity (credit-natural) and expense behaves like the opposite of equity (debit-natural).

Account typeIncreases withDecreases withNatural balance
AssetsDebitCreditDebit
LiabilitiesCreditDebitCredit
EquityCreditDebitCredit
RevenueCreditDebitCredit
ExpensesDebitCreditDebit

Why revenue and expenses land where they do: revenue increases Net Income, which increases Earned Capital (equity) — so revenue increases the same way equity does, with a credit. Expenses decrease Net Income, which decreases equity — so expenses increase with a debit, the opposite of equity's natural side, precisely because they pull equity down.

Quick way to remember it: assets and expenses both increase on the debit (left) side; liabilities, equity, and revenue all increase on the credit (right) side. Every adjusting entry and journal entry you build in this unit follows this table without exception.

Exam angle"Natural balance" questions are common — know that assets normally carry debit balances, while liabilities and equity normally carry credit balances, because that's the side of the equation they sit on. Expect a question that also tests revenue/expense placement specifically, since it's the part most often mixed up.

Why adjustments exist

Cash movement and economic activity don't always happen at the same time. Example: employees may earn wages during a period but not be paid until after period-end — failing to recognize the wages owed would understate liabilities and overstate net income. Adjustments fix that mismatch.

The four adjustment types (a 2×2 by timing)

Cash before recognitionCash after recognition
Expense-sidePrepaid (Deferred) ExpensesAccrued Expenses
Revenue-sideUnearned (Deferred) RevenuesAccrued Revenues
  • Prepaid (Deferred) Expense — an advance cash payment that will become an expense in the future.
  • Unearned (Deferred) Revenue — cash received from customers before goods/services are provided.
  • Accrued Expense — an expense incurred and recognized before cash is paid.
  • Accrued Revenue — revenue earned and recognized before cash is received.

Try it: classify the adjustment

A fresh scenario every time — decide which of the four types applies.

Exam angleClassify using two questions: has cash moved yet? Has the revenue/expense been recognized yet? Whichever happens first tells you "deferred" (prepaid/unearned) vs. "accrued."

The big pattern

An adjusting entry always touches one income-statement account and one balance-sheet account, and never touches Cash. (If cash moved, it was already recorded during the period.) Learn six recipes and you can do every adjustment on the exam.

On Oct 1 a company pays $18,000 for 12 months of insurance and records it as an asset (Prepaid Insurance). By Dec 31, 3 of 12 months are used.

$18,000 ÷ 12 = $1,500 per month

Recipe 2 — supplies (count, then plug)

The supplies account shows $24,800 before adjustment. A year-end count finds $7,100 left. The ending balance must equal what is really on the shelf, so the difference was used: $24,800 − $7,100 = $17,700. Debit Supplies Expense 17,700; credit Supplies 17,700.

Recipe 3 — depreciation

Equipment costs $150,000, lasts 10 years, no residual value. Straight-line: $150,000 ÷ 10 = $15,000 per year. Debit Depreciation Expense 15,000; credit Accumulated Depreciation 15,000. Accumulated depreciation is a contra-asset — the equipment's historical cost stays untouched. After 3 years: accumulated depreciation = 3 × 15,000 = $45,000, and book value = $150,000 − $45,000 = $105,000. Depreciation expense is on the income statement; accumulated depreciation is on the balance sheet. It uses up the asset's cost; it does not set cash aside.

Recipe 4 — unearned revenue becomes earned

On Sep 1 a company collects $42,000 for 12 months of service and records all of it as Unearned Revenue (a liability). By Dec 31, 4 months are delivered: $42,000 ÷ 12 × 4 = $14,000.

Dec 31DebitCredit
Unearned revenue14,000
   Service revenue14,000

The remaining $28,000 is still a liability: the company still owes 8 months of service.

Recipes 5 and 6 — accruals

  • Accrued wages: employees earned $11,800 not yet paid. Debit Wages Expense 11,800; credit Wages Payable 11,800. (Not “debit Wages Payable, credit Cash” — no cash moves yet.)
  • Accrued interest expense: $4,100 of interest on a note has built up but is unpaid. Debit Interest Expense 4,100; credit Interest Payable 4,100. Net income falls by $4,100.
  • Accrued interest revenue: $6,300 of interest has been earned but not received. Debit Interest Receivable 6,300; credit Interest Revenue 6,300. (Not “debit Cash”.)
Exam angleExpect to compute the amount (months used ÷ total months × price; beginning + purchases − ending count; cost ÷ life) and pick the entry. A quick sanity check: the credit side of a prepaid/supplies/depreciation entry is always an asset (or contra-asset) account, and Cash never appears. Also expect “what happens if the adjustment is skipped?” — use the last column of the table above.

One formula solves half the exam

Almost every balance sheet account with activity follows the same “bucket” logic. Whatever you start with, plus what flows in, minus what flows out, equals what is left:

Beginning balance + Additions − Reductions = Ending balance

Supplies15,00038,000 bought? used (expense)9,000 → used = 44,000

The last row: the Jan 8 payroll of $24,700 settles the $6,500 accrued in December and covers $18,200 earned in January. Only $18,200 is January expense. The $6,500 was already expensed in December.

Direction check: assets (supplies, inventory) go down when used, and the expense (supplies expense, COGS) goes up. A liability (unearned revenue, wages payable) goes down when the work is delivered or the bill is paid, and revenue goes up or cash goes out.

Why net income and cash flow differ

Accrual net income counts revenue when earned and expenses when incurred. Cash only counts what moved. A company can show positive net income and negative operating cash flow (customers haven't paid yet) — this is normal, not an error.

Worked example

Service revenue $480,000, cash collected $410,000. Wages expense $195,000, wages paid in cash $175,000. Ignore everything else.

Net income = 480,000 − 195,000 = 285,000
Cash profit = 410,000 − 175,000 = 235,000
Gap = 50,000 = receivables up 70,000 − wages payable up 20,000

Unpaid wages make cash look better than income; uncollected revenue makes it look worse. Likewise, wages expense = cash paid + unpaid at year-end (e.g. $78,000 paid + $19,000 owed = $97,000).

Exam angleWrite the four boxes and put the unknown in one. Typical prompts: ending inventory, purchases (= COGS + ending − beginning), revenue recognized from unearned revenue, wages expense for the year, net income vs. cash collected. Remember: a payment that includes a previously accrued amount only expenses the new portion.

The required order (memorize — each step depends on the last)

  1. Income Statement
  2. Reconcile Retained Earnings
  3. Statement of Stockholders' Equity
  4. Balance Sheet
  5. Statement of Cash Flows

You need net income before you can reconcile retained earnings; you need the reconciled retained earnings before you can complete the Statement of Stockholders' Equity; you need the SSE's ending balances before the Balance Sheet's equity section is complete; and so on.

Preparing the Balance Sheet from the FSET

Start with the beginning balances from the prior year's balance sheet, then use the FSET entries in the balance sheet columns to determine activity in each account during the year. The bottom row of the FSET provides the balance sheet equation.

Exam angleExplain why financial statements must be prepared in this specific order rather than any order — the dependency chain itself is the tested concept, not just memorizing the sequence.

What closing does

The closing process zeroes out only the temporary accounts — Revenues, Expenses, and Dividends — by transferring their ending balances to Retained Earnings. Their balance at the start of each accounting period is $0; they only record the current period's activity. Every other account (assets, liabilities, contributed capital, retained earnings) is permanent and simply carries forward.

Why a formal process is needed

Net income isn't automatically processed to the Balance Sheet as retained earnings — companies use journal entries to capture transactions and adjustments, so a formal closing process is required at each period-end to transfer temporary account balances into retained earnings.

Close order

  1. Close Revenue and Gain accounts
  2. Close Expense and Loss accounts (COGS, Operating Expenses, Nonoperating Expenses, Tax Expense)
  3. Close Dividend account
Exam angleA classic question tests whether you can identify temporary vs. permanent accounts. Another common angle: explain why dividends are closed directly to retained earnings even though they never touch the Income Statement — dividends reduce retained earnings directly (per Unit 2's roll-forward), so they still need to be zeroed out each period.

The idea

Real statements show you only balances. To work out what happened during the year, you run the roll-forward in reverse: you know the beginning balance, the ending balance, and one flow, and you solve for the other flow — then you write the journal entry it implies.

Example: cash paid for prepaid expenses

$ millionsPrior yearCurrent year
Prepaid expenses (balance sheet)4052
Prepaid-related expense (income statement)130
Beginning 40 + Cash paid X − Expense 130 = Ending 52
X = 52 + 130 − 40 = 142

Journal entry for the year: debit Prepaid 142, credit Cash 142 (the payments); debit Expense 130, credit Prepaid 130 (the using-up). Check: 40 + 142 − 130 = 52.

Example: cash collected from customers

Revenue 900, receivables rose from 120 to 150, and unearned revenue rose from 60 to 75. Cash collected = revenue − increase in receivables + increase in unearned revenue = 900 − 30 + 15 = 885.

Trap: the direction of each adjustment. A growing receivable means customers owe you more, so cash is lower than revenue. Growing unearned revenue means you were paid for work not yet done, so cash is higher.

The setup

A company works a five-day week and pays employees every second Friday. Employees earn $2,000 per workday in total. Its year-end is Wednesday, Dec 31. The last payday was Friday, Dec 26, covering work through that day. The next payday is Friday, Jan 9.

Period 1 — Dec 31 adjustment

Work since Dec 26: Mon Dec 29, Tue Dec 30, Wed Dec 31 = 3 days × $2,000 = $6,000 earned and unpaid. Debit Wages Expense 6,000; credit Wages Payable 6,000.

Period 2 — Jan 9 payroll

The Jan 9 check covers the two weeks since Dec 26: 10 workdays × $2,000 = $20,000 (assume no holidays). 3 of those days were expensed last year.

Jan 9DebitCredit
Wages payable6,000
Wages expense (7 days × 2,000)14,000
   Cash20,000

Across both years, each day of work is expensed exactly once: 3 days in year 1, 7 days in year 2. Without the Dec 31 adjustment, year 1 would be understated by $6,000 of expense and year 2 overstated by the same amount — the total across years is the same, but each year's net income is wrong.

The financial-statement effect on Jan 9: assets (cash) down 20,000, liabilities down 6,000, equity (via expense) down 14,000.

Full closing entries

At Dec 31, before closing, a firm has: Service fees earned $203,000 (credit), Rent expense $52,000 (debit), Salaries expense $118,000 (debit), Dividends $10,000 (debit), Retained earnings $64,000 (credit). Close in order: revenue, then expenses, then dividends.

Closing entryDebitCredit
1. Service fees earned203,000
   Retained earnings203,000
2. Retained earnings170,000
   Rent expense52,000
   Salaries expense118,000
3. Retained earnings10,000
   Dividends10,000
Net income = 203,000 − 52,000 − 118,000 = 33,000
Ending RE = 64,000 + 33,000 − 10,000 = 87,000

Check: 64,000 + 203,000 − 170,000 − 10,000 = 87,000. Revenue, rent, salaries and dividends all end at zero. Many textbooks route steps 1 and 2 through a temporary “Income Summary” account; the end result for retained earnings is identical, so follow whatever your instructor's problem asks.

Trap: the dividend closing entry is a separate third step — dividends are never part of net income, so they never go through the expense-closing entry.

The cycle

1 Record → 2 Adjust → 3 Statements → 4 Close

Adjust before statements. Close after.

Debits and credits

Debit = LEFT, Credit = RIGHT
Dr grows: Assets, Expenses
Cr grows: Liabilities, Equity, Revenue

Total debits = total credits, always.

The four adjustments

Prepaid expense: cash first, expense later
Unearned revenue: cash first, revenue later
Accrued expense: expense first, cash later
Accrued revenue: revenue first, cash later

Ask: which came first, cash or recognition?

Dr Expense / Cr Prepaid (or Supplies)
Prepaid used = price ÷ months × months passed
Supplies used = unadjusted − count

Never touches Cash.

Depreciation

Annual = (cost − residual) ÷ life
Book value = cost − accumulated dep.

Accum. dep. is a contra-asset. Cost never changes.

Unearned revenue

Receive: Dr Cash / Cr Unearned revenue
Earn: Dr Unearned revenue / Cr Revenue

Cash in advance is a liability, not revenue.

Accruals

Expense: Dr Wages exp / Cr Wages payable
Revenue: Dr Receivable / Cr Revenue

If you skip an accrued expense: expenses and liabilities too low, income too high.

Roll-forward

Beginning + In − Out = Ending
Purchases = COGS + End − Beg
Cash paid on accrued item = beg. payable + new expense

Statement order

IS → Retained earnings → SSE → BS → Cash flows

Each needs the one before.

Closing

Close: Revenue, Expenses, Dividends → Retained earnings
Temporary = Rev, Exp, Dividends
Permanent = Assets, Liabs, Contrib. capital, RE

After closing, temporary accounts are $0.

Traps the exam sets

  • Recording an adjustment as a debit or credit to Cash. Adjusting entries never involve cash.
  • Debiting wages payable (instead of crediting it) when the wages are first accrued.
  • Treating cash received in advance as revenue. It is a liability until earned.
  • Reducing the equipment account for depreciation instead of using accumulated depreciation.
  • Counting a paid payroll in full as new-period expense when part was already accrued last year.
  • Believing positive net income means positive cash flow, or the reverse. They can differ.
  • Thinking Retained Earnings or Common Stock is closed. Only Revenue, Expenses and Dividends are.
  • Preparing the statements last, or in the wrong order.

Before the exam

Likely exam & discussion angles

  • Put the four steps of the accounting cycle in order, or identify which step a given action belongs to.
  • Represent the same transaction using the FSET, a T-account, and a journal entry, and confirm all three produce a consistent picture.
  • Given a transaction scenario, classify it into one of the four adjustment types using the "has cash moved yet / has the revenue or expense been recognized yet" test.
  • Explain why financial statements must be prepared in a specific order rather than any order.
  • Identify which accounts are temporary (zeroed out each period) vs. permanent (carried forward).
  • Explain why dividends are closed to retained earnings even though they never appear on the Income Statement.
  • Connect this unit's adjustment types back to Unit 2's cash-vs-accrual distinction — these four categories are the mechanical implementation of accrual accounting.
  • Compute an adjustment amount and choose the entry: prepaid insurance or rent used, supplies used from a count, straight-line depreciation and accumulated depreciation, unearned revenue earned, accrued wages and interest.
  • State the effect of skipping an adjustment on assets, liabilities, expenses and net income.
  • Solve a roll-forward for the missing piece (ending inventory, purchases, supplies used, revenue from unearned balances, wages expense) and explain why accrual net income differs from cash flow.
