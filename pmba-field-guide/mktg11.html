MKTG 6090 · Chapter 11

Managing pricing and sales promotions

Price is the only marketing-mix element that earns revenue. This chapter shows how buyers perceive prices, how to set a price in six steps (objective, demand, costs, competitors, method, final price), how to run the break-even and markup math, how to adjust and change prices, and how to use sales promotions without training customers to wait for a deal.

Chapter 11, section by section

Price is the only revenue-producing element

Of the marketing mix, price is the one element that produces revenue; the others produce costs. Price also communicates the intended value positioning of the brand. Price comes in many forms (rent, tuition, fares, fees, tolls, retainers, wages, commissions) and many components (a car's sticker price is adjusted by rebates and dealer incentives).

How pricing has changed

  • History: prices were negotiated; the single-price policy arose with large-scale retailing in the late 19th century (Woolworth, Tiffany, Wanamaker).
  • The internet empowers buyers (instant comparison, in-store phone checks) and sellers (monitor demand and adjust prices, e.g., Uber surge pricing; custom promotions by demographic, behavior, or geolocation).
  • Who sets price: owners in small firms; division and product-line managers in large ones; top management sets objectives and policies. A pricing department often reports to marketing, finance, or top management. In B2B, results improve when pricing authority is spread across sales, marketing, and finance.

Common pricing mistakes

  • Cost-plus by habit: “calculate our costs and add the industry's traditional margin.”
  • Not revising prices often enough to capture market changes.
  • Setting price independently of the rest of the marketing program instead of as part of positioning.
  • Not varying price enough across items, segments, channels, and purchase occasions.

Opening and closing cases

Dynamic pricing means prices move up and down in real time with supply and demand. Wendy's (2024) announced a test and consumers heard “surge pricing” (prices only go up) and reacted angrily; Coca-Cola faced a similar backlash in 1999 over vending machines that would raise prices in hot weather. Lesson: violating pricing norms makes buyers feel treated unfairly (“price gouging”). Netflix is a pricing-as-value example: it added tiers and raised prices as it invested in original content (“Price is all relative to value”). The Marketing Spotlights cover Priceline (“Name Your Own Price,” later shifted to hotels where it earns a 15–20% commission versus about 3% on air bookings) and Uber (dynamic fares; surge pricing can multiply a fare by as much as seven times and is kept as a driver incentive despite fairness complaints).

Data note: dollar signs in the source text are OCR-garbled ("S7.99"), so specific Netflix, Priceline, and Uber dollar figures are omitted here.

Exam angleKnow that price is the only revenue element, the difference between dynamic and surge pricing, and the four common pricing mistakes. Expect a short scenario asking which mistake a firm is making.

Consumers process prices, not just read them

Buyers act on perceived price, not the stated price. They may hold a lower price threshold (below it, price signals poor quality) and an upper price threshold (above it, the product is not worth it). Example: a plain black T-shirt is $300 at Armani, $15 at Gap, $8 at H&M — fabric, cut, label, and brand image explain the gap.

When a price must be set, and the six steps

A firm sets price for the first time when it develops a new product, enters a new channel or area, or bids on contract work. Most markets have three to five price points (Marriott's brands run from Ritz-Carlton down to Fairfield Inn).

Step 1: Four pricing objectives

ObjectiveIdeaConditions favoring it
Short-term profitPick the price that maximizes current profit, cash flow, or ROI.Needs known demand and cost; risks sacrificing long-run performance.
Market penetrationVery low price to maximize share, expecting volume to cut unit costs (Texas Instruments).(1) Price-sensitive market, low price grows it; (2) costs fall with accumulated experience; (3) low price discourages competitors.
Market skimmingStart high to “skim the cream” from buyers with the highest willingness to pay, then drop slowly (Sony).(1) Enough buyers with high current demand; (2) high price does not attract competitors; (3) high price signals superior image.
Quality leadershipCharge a relatively high price to fund R&D, production, and service (St. Regis, Miele, Sub-Zero).Premium positioning with loyal customers.

Nonprofits differ: a university seeks partial cost recovery, a nonprofit hospital full cost recovery, a theater may price to fill seats, and a social agency may tie price to client income.

Step 2: Determining demand

The demand curve normally slopes down (higher price, lower demand); for prestige goods it can slope up for a while. Price elasticity of demand is how much quantity responds to a price change. Figure 11.1 contrasts two cases for a rise from $10 to $15:

Worked check (simple percent changes from the chapter's quantities): price rises 50%. In (a) quantity falls from 105 to 100, about −4.8%; revenue goes from $10 × 105 = $1,050 up to $15 × 100 = $1,500. In (b) quantity falls from 150 to 50, −66.7%; revenue goes from $10 × 150 = $1,500 down to $15 × 50 = $750. If demand is inelastic, a price rise can lift revenue; if elastic, a cut can.

  • Elasticity is low when: the product is distinctive with few substitutes; buyers do not notice the higher price; they are slow to change habits; they see the price as justified (costs, scarcity, taxes); the expense is a small part of income or end-product cost; another party bears part of the cost.
  • Elasticity can differ for cuts versus increases, can have a price indifference band, and differs short run versus long run.
  • A review of 40 years of research found an average elasticity of −2.62 (a 1% price cut raised sales 2.62%); higher for durable goods and for introduction/growth-stage products; promotional elasticities exceed actual price elasticities in the short run.

Step 3: Estimating costs

Demand sets a ceiling; costs set the floor. Fixed costs do not vary with production (rent, interest, salaries). Variable costs vary directly with output (the total varies; the per-unit amount tends to be constant). Total cost = fixed + variable; average cost = total cost / units. Average cost falls as fixed costs spread over more units until a plant's capacity, then rises; larger plants are efficient until diseconomies of scale set in (Samsung: 3,000 per day optimal in the chapter's example, 4,000 worse). Use activity-based cost accounting to judge true profitability by customer type.

The experience curve: average cost falls with accumulated production. Chapter figures: $100 per tablet at 100,000 produced, $90 at 200,000, $80 at 400,000 (each doubling cuts $10). If Samsung ($80), Firm A ($90), and Firm B ($100) all sell at $100, profits per unit are $20, $10, and $0. Samsung can cut to $90, driving out B and pressuring A. Risks: a cheap image, assuming weak competitors, and getting stuck with old technology if a rival innovates a lower-cost method.

Step 4: Competitors' prices

Within the range set by demand and cost, adjust for competitors: add the value of features you have that they lack; subtract the value of features they have that you lack. Value players (Aldi, Lidl, JetBlue, Southwest) combine low price with high quality. Launch your own low-cost operation only if it strengthens your existing business and gains advantages it would not have independently (HSBC's First Direct worked; United's Ted failed partly for lack of synergy).

Exam angleMatch each objective to its conditions (penetration = price-sensitive market and falling costs; skimming = high-willingness buyers and no new entrants). Be able to say whether a scenario is elastic or inelastic and whether raising price lifts revenue.

Three considerations, five methods

Costs set the floor, competitors' prices and substitutes provide an orienting point, customers' valuation of unique features sets the ceiling. Five methods: markup, target-rate-of-return, economic-value-to-customer, competitive (going-rate), and auction pricing.

Markup pricing and target-return pricing: the toaster example

Variable cost $10 per unit, fixed cost $300,000, expected sales 50,000 units.

  • Unit cost = variable cost + fixed cost / unit sales = $10 + $300,000 / 50,000 = $16.
  • Markup price = unit cost / (1 − desired return on sales) = $16 / (1 − 0.2) = $20; profit $4 per unit. If a dealer wants 50% on its selling price, it marks up 100% on cost to $20 / (1 − 0.5) = $40.
  • Target-return price = unit cost + (desired return × invested capital) / unit sales. With $1,000,000 invested and a 20% ROI ($200,000): $16 + $200,000 / 50,000 = $20.
  • Break-even volume = fixed cost / (price − variable cost) = $300,000 / ($20 − $10) = 30,000 units. At 50,000 units: revenue $1,000,000 minus total cost $800,000 = $200,000 profit.

Why markup is popular though illogical: costs are easier to estimate than demand; when all firms use it, prices are similar and price competition is minimized; it feels fair. Markups run higher on seasonal, specialty, slow-moving, high-handling, and demand-inelastic items (prescription drugs). Any method that ignores demand, perceived value, and competition is unlikely to be optimal. Target-return pricing, common in regulated industries such as utilities, also ignores demand and competition.

Data note: the OCR of the target-return formula line is garbled ("$16 + $20 / 50,000"); the figures above are recomputed from the stated $1 million, 20% ROI, and 50,000 units, and the resulting $20 matches the chapter's text.

Economic-value-to-customer pricing

Price is based on the buyer's perceived total value (performance, channel, warranty, support, reputation, trust). A seller can charge more if it convinces customers it offers the lowest total cost of ownership. A common error is giving away services as sales incentives rather than charging for them. Caterpillar: a competitor's equivalent tractor is $90,000; premiums for durability ($7,000), reliability ($6,000), service ($5,000), and longer parts warranty ($2,000) add up to $110,000 of value. Caterpillar discounts $10,000 to a $100,000 final price, so the buyer still receives $20,000 of extra value. Others: PACCAR commands about a 10% premium; Stag umbrellas raised quality and sales rose; Goodyear priced tires by expected miles of wear so buyers could compare.

Data note: the Caterpillar table is garbled in the source (“Silo,000” for $110,000); the sum above is rebuilt from the other listed amounts and the stated $10,000 discount and $100,000 final price.

Competitive pricing and auction pricing

Competitive (going-rate) pricing: price is based mostly on rivals'; common in commodities (steel, paper, fertilizer); small firms follow the leader and keep a fixed small premium or discount. Useful when costs vary or are hard to measure and competitor response is uncertain.

Auction typeDirectionWho / example
EnglishAscending bidsOne seller, many buyers; highest bidder wins (eBay; antiques, cattle, used equipment).
DutchDescending bidsSeller lowers the price until a bidder accepts; or one buyer, many sellers compete downward (SAP Ariba; reverse auctions like Pfizer's).
Sealed-bidOne bid, unseenSuppliers submit a single bid without knowing others' (governments). Bid expected profit balances not bidding below cost against not bidding too high.
Exam angleRe-derive the toaster numbers: unit cost $16, markup price $20, break-even 30,000, target-return price $20. Know which method ignores demand (markup, target return) and the three auction types.

From method to final price

Methods narrow the range; companies then build a pricing structure reflecting geography, segments, timing, order size, guarantees, and service. Because of discounts, allowances, and promotions, a firm rarely earns the same profit on every unit. Technology enables dynamic pricing by inventory, item velocity, competitor price, and advertising (Uber and Lyft, sports tickets, Amazon sellers repricing by the hour).

Price discrimination

Selling at two or more prices that do not reflect proportional cost differences.

DegreeMeaning
First-degreeEach customer pays a separate price based on the intensity of their demand.
Second-degreeLower price for larger volumes (though some tiered services, like cell plans, charge more at higher usage).
Third-degreeDifferent prices for different segments: customer-segment (student/senior admission), product-form (Evian 1 liter $3 vs 2 oz in a spray $9), channel (Coca-Cola by outlet), location (AMC seat prices), time (early bird, Valentine's roses).

Yield pricing (airlines, hotels): discounted limited early purchase, higher late prices, and low rates for unsold inventory just before expiry. To manage customer relationships, bundle offers to hinder comparison, or use variable pricing as a reward (APL gives cheaper rates for booking early). Legal: discrimination is legal if costs differ for different volumes or qualities; predatory pricing (below cost to destroy competition) is unlawful.

Conditions for price discrimination to work: (1) the market is segmentable with different demand intensities; (2) low-price buyers cannot resell to high-price buyers; (3) competitors cannot undersell in the high-price segment; (4) segmenting and policing cost less than the extra revenue; (5) it does not breed resentment. The chapter's ethical insight (prescription drugs: Daraprim, EpiPen) stresses that drug pricing affects social welfare; the AMA's TruthinRx campaign pushes for price transparency.

Inflationary environments

When prices rise sharply, leading brands feel pressure from private labels. Kraft Heinz raised prices about 11% in 2023 and saw sales fall in some brands; others cited brand strength (“Brand power today is pricing power tomorrow”).

Initiating price cuts

Reasons: excess capacity, or a drive to dominate through lower costs. Drawbacks: low-quality image; price buys share but not loyalty; price wars; higher-priced rivals with lower cost structures can match and outlast you; customers may demand concessions. Customers also guess motives: model being replaced, faulty item, firm in trouble, price will drop more, quality reduced.

Initiating price increases

A successful increase can raise profits a lot. Worked check: price $10, 100 units, costs $970: revenue $1,000, profit $30 (3% of sales). Raise price 1% (10 cents) to $10.10 with the same volume: revenue $1,010, profit $40, an increase of 33%.

Causes: cost inflation (and anticipatory pricing, raising by more than the cost increase), or demand exceeding capacity (raise prices, ration supply, or both). Coach avoided discounts, positioned as “expressive luxury,” and raised handbag prices about 30% over three pandemic years. Avoiding backlash: advance notice, explain in understandable terms, make low-visibility moves first (drop discounts, raise minimum orders, cut low-margin items), and use escalator clauses in long-term contracts. The more similar a firm's offerings, the more price differences look like gouging; differentiate and clarify.

Responding to competitors' price changes

Competitors react most when there are few firms, the product is homogeneous, and buyers are well informed. A competitor with a market-share objective will probably match; one maximizing profit may raise advertising or quality instead. Questions to ask: (1) why did they change price? (2) temporary or permanent? (3) what happens to our share and profit if we do not respond, and will others respond? (4) what will rivals do after each of our options?

Market leaders attacked by cheaper rivals can (1) further differentiate, (2) introduce a low-cost venture, or (3) reinvent themselves as a low-cost player. In fast-moving industries (meatpacking, lumber, oil) prepare contingent responses in advance.

Exam angleRe-derive the 1% price increase to a 33% profit increase and explain why it holds only if volume is unchanged. Know the three leader responses to a low-cost attacker and the four questions before answering a rival's cut.

What incentives do

Incentives (sales promotion tools) are mostly short-term tools to stimulate quicker or greater purchase by consumers or the trade. Digital coupons are the fastest-growing area (no printing, easy to update, higher redemption). Promotions give a short-run sales jump but little permanent gain; they trigger stockpiling (purchase acceleration), then a post-promotion dip, and constant discounting can devalue the offering. Consumer franchise-building tools carry a selling message (frequency awards, coupons advertising features, related premiums); price-off packs, unrelated premiums, contests, refunds, and trade allowances typically do not build the brand.

Net price analysis

List $3,000, average discount $300, promotional spending $450 (15% of list), co-op advertising $150: net price = $3,000 − $300 − $450 − $150 = $2,100.

Five-stage process and decisions

Establish objectives, select tools, develop the program, implement and control, evaluate results. Consumer objectives: more frequent or larger purchases, trial by nonusers, attract switchers. Retailer objectives: carry the brand, carry more, feature/display/reduce price, push the product. Program design: size, conditions for participation, duration, distribution vehicle, timing, and total budget (cost = administrative + incentive cost, times expected units; a coupon deal assumes only a fraction redeem).

StrategyIdeaFits when
PushUse sales force and trade promotion money to get intermediaries to carry, promote, and sell.Low brand loyalty, in-store brand choice, impulse items, benefits well understood.
PullUse advertising and promotion so consumers demand the product from intermediaries.High brand loyalty and involvement, perceived brand differences, brand chosen before the store.

Top firms (Apple, Coca-Cola, Nike) use both; push works better with a pull strategy behind it.

Tools

AudienceTools
ConsumerPrice reductions; coupons and promo codes; cash refunds/rebates; price packs (reduced-price pack, banded pack); premiums; frequency programs; prizes (contest, sweepstakes, game); tie-in promotions; seasonal discounts; financing.
