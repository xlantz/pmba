MGT 6050 · Unit 3

Conflict, voice, and culture

Units 1 and 2 covered how real teams form and how to build psychological safety into them. Unit 3 asks the harder question: what happens when people don't speak up — and how do you build a culture where they will? Two case studies (the Columbia shuttle disaster, Volkswagen) show the cost of silence. The hidden profile problem and the Bay of Pigs vs. Cuban Missile Crisis comparison show exactly what process changes turn silence into productive debate. One framework (Hansen's six tactics) shows how to run a meeting that surfaces real disagreement. And one deep case study (Amazon) gives you a real, ambivalent culture to test all of it against.

Unit 3, source by source

Wald, M. L., & Schwartz, J. (2003, August 4). "Shuttle Inquiry Uncovers Flaws in Communication." The New York Times.

Big idea

The Columbia shuttle disaster wasn't a failure of information — it was a failure of information flow. Engineers actually wanted spy-satellite images of possible launch damage. Those requests existed. They just never reached the person who could act on them, and a reassuring-looking risk analysis went unchallenged on the way up the chain.

What actually broke down

  • Several engineers made informal requests for satellite images of the shuttle's damaged wing. Mission management team leader Linda Ham said she never learned of them — despite actively asking around about it.
  • The chief engineer who reportedly wanted the images never spoke up about it at the mission management team meeting Ms. Ham chaired. As he put it afterward: "It never, never came up."
  • Ms. Ham relied entirely on a Boeing engineering analysis that concluded the foam strike posed no serious threat — without independent scrutiny.

"NASA Chicken"

Organizational consultant Joseph Grenny (co-author of Crucial Conversations) named the pattern: no one wants to be first to raise a flag that could slow a project or carry political and economic risk. Everyone waits, hoping someone else speaks up first.

The humiliation factor

One NASA engineer explained: "The NASA culture does not accept being wrong." Instead of an environment where "there's no such thing as a stupid question," the humiliation factor ran high — the opposite of what Unit 2's Edmondson material calls psychological safety.

Tufte's critique: the slide that hid the risk

Yale professor Edward Tufte later dissected the actual PowerPoint slide used to brief managers on the foam-strike risk, calling it a "PowerPoint festival of bureaucratic hyper-rationalism." The bullet-point summary was far more reassuring than the underlying data supported — the piece of foam that struck the orbiter was 640 times larger than anything NASA had tested, a fact buried in the fine print. Tufte's conclusion: "the real fault of upper management is they didn't look beneath the optimistic surface of the reports of their subordinates."

Exam angleDistinguish "we lacked information" from "we had information, but it never reached the right person, or arrived pre-filtered into false confidence." This case is squarely the second failure mode — and it's the one that recurs across NASA, Volkswagen, and Amazon in this unit.

Bregman, P. (2015, October 21). "The High Cost of Conformity, and How to Avoid It." Harvard Business Review.

Big idea

Adults conform to the people around them constantly, in small and large ways — and that same pull toward conformity, at organizational scale, can enable catastrophic ethical failure. The fix isn't a personality trait; it's a trainable three-step discipline.

Two examples, two scales

  • Small scale: Bregman kept mountain biking past his own skill level, injured, simply because his friends kept riding. "I continued because they did."
  • Large scale: Volkswagen installed emissions-cheating software in diesel cars for seven years across 11 million vehicles. When the head of VW America testified to Congress, he claimed only "a couple of software engineers" knew — implausible at a 583,000-person company. Someone, probably many people, chose silence.

The three-step framework

  1. Clear, strong, committed values. What do you actually believe, and are you willing to be embarrassed, disliked, or fired for it?
  2. Truly see what's going on around you. Not denial — actually registering what you're observing for what it is.
  3. The courage to act. Say something. Stand up to power if that's what it takes — skillfully and respectfully, to preserve relationships where possible.

Bregman's definition of leadership

"Leadership is the willingness to move in a different direction than others." Not a title. Not authority. The willingness to not conform.

Why courage is the hardest step

It requires going against the norm of what's happening around you — which doesn't come naturally to adults and has to be practiced. Bregman suggests starting small: keep a shared space clean when others leave it messy, work when others take sick days, make different choices than the crowd — then notice that you can tolerate the discomfort. That tolerance is what frees you to act on your values when the stakes are actually high.

Exam angleApply the three-step framework to a scenario and identify which step likely failed. At Volkswagen, was it unclear values, failure to see, or lack of courage? Bregman's honest answer: probably some of each, and all three are genuinely hard, especially when it means risking friendships and jobs.

MGT 6050 Class 3 slides, "Information Sharing and Conflict in Teams" (content only — the Murder Mystery exercise itself and scorecard-assignment logistics skipped per your instruction).

The hidden profile problem

A hidden profile exists when a superior decision alternative stays hidden because each team member only holds a portion of the information that would support it. Hidden profiles only emerge when all information is fully shared — but teams tend to focus on shared information that everyone already has in common (Stasser, 1992, calls this the "common information bias"), rather than drawing out each member's unique information.

The evidence: shared vs. unshared information

Larson, Christensen, Franz & Abbott (1998) had three-person teams of physicians diagnose a medical case (most likely Lyme disease or infectious mononucleosis) where two-thirds of the information given to the team was the same across all three members. Findings: shared information got discussed earlier and more; the more unique information a team actually discussed, the more accurate its diagnosis became; and team leaders played an active role in whether that unique information ever got surfaced at all.

"If only HP knew what HP knew"

Two executive quotes capture the same underlying problem: a Boeing executive described knowledge sitting right next to someone going completely unnoticed, causing duplicated effort with no reliable way to get the right knowledge to the right person at the right time; a Hewlett-Packard executive summed it up as "If only HP knew what HP knew." In general, teams that share more information outperform teams that share less — the problem is a process problem, not an availability problem.

Why members don't share: pressures toward conformity

Two distinct psychological pressures keep people from voicing unique information:

  • Normative influence — fear of exclusion from the group, and uncertainty about others' norms and expectations.
  • Informational influence — becoming uncertain about your own perceptions and judgments once you see others disagree with what you'd say.

Organization or team culture can compound both by actively inhibiting the expression of conflict among members (the lecture cites NASA as an example, echoing the Shuttle Inquiry source above).

The Asch conformity experiments

Asch (1956) showed participants a "standard" line alongside three comparison lines (A, B, C) and asked which matched in length — a task with an objectively obvious correct answer. When surrounded by confederates who confidently gave the wrong answer out loud, a substantial share of real participants conformed to the incorrect group answer rather than trust their own eyes. It's a stark demonstration of how much normative and informational pressure can override even unambiguous, directly observable evidence.

Conformity and uniformity: the bigger risk

The most common fear about groups is too much conflict. The lecture's sharper point: a bigger risk is too much agreement. Groups must be confronted with what they privately believe, not just what they publicly agree to — and by conforming or staying passive, a member can end up complicit in unethical behavior they never actually agreed with.

Getting unique information out: what doesn't work, and what does

Intuitive-seeming fixes are actually ineffective, because they push members to focus even more on common information rather than unique information: increasing the amount of discussion, increasing group size, increasing the amount of information provided, making the team accountable for the decision, providing incentives, or simply giving the team more time.

Effective strategies instead set up the right process and structure: redirect discussion explicitly toward unshared information; determine what expertise each member actually holds; suspend initial judgment; create psychological safety; encourage full discussion of minority opinions; cultivate norms that require disagreement (a designated devil's advocate); and establish a standing pattern of identifying and questioning underlying assumptions (the dialectical decision method).

Types of diversity

Three distinct kinds of diversity show up in team research, and they matter differently:

  • Informational diversity — differences in members' knowledge bases.
  • Social category diversity — demographic differences (age, race, gender, etc.).
  • Value diversity — differences in beliefs about the group's philosophies and values.
Exam angleA classic trap: a question describes a team trying to surface a hidden profile by talking longer, adding more members, or offering a bonus for the right answer — all four of the "ineffective" strategies sound reasonable but are specifically called out as not working, because they all amplify focus on shared information rather than unique information.

Types of conflict

Jehn (1997) and DeWit, Greer & Jehn (2012) distinguish two fundamentally different kinds of team conflict:

  • Task conflict — disagreement about the task itself (ideas, plans, what work should be done) or the process for doing it. This produces positive outcomes, but only when relationship conflict is low and psychological safety is high.
  • Relationship conflict — disagreement rooted in personal and social issues unrelated to the task (tension, annoyance, frustration, irritation). This produces negative outcomes essentially always.

The practical goal follows directly: promote functional (task) conflict, and minimize dysfunctional (relationship) conflict.

How much conflict is too much?

Margaret Heffernan's TED talk argues good disagreement is central to progress; Cozier & Schwenk go further, arguing conflict and dissent are what organizations actually need to succeed. But the lecture immediately complicates this with a real counter-example: Amazon (the same case study covered later in this unit), where an aggressively confrontational culture of disagreement produced its own serious costs. More disagreement isn't automatically better — it has to be the right kind, under the right conditions.

Contrasting cases: Bay of Pigs vs. the Cuban Missile Crisis

The clearest illustration of "process matters more than the people" comes from two decisions made by the exact same president and largely the same inner circle, just 18 months apart:

Process characteristicBay of Pigs (April 1961)Cuban Missile Crisis (Oct. 1962)
Leader roleKennedy present at all critical meetingsKennedy deliberately absent from preliminary meetings, to create a "second chance meeting"
Devil's advocatesNo one designated to play that roleTwo confidantes explicitly played "intellectual watchdog," probing every argument's flaws
Group normsAdherence to protocol; deference to statusMinimized status/rank differences; freedom from protocol
Cohesion & perspectivesExtreme secrecy; small in-group; outsiders excludedDirect communication with lower-level experts; outside voices brought in periodically
Constructive conflictOne small subgroup both planned and judged the planTwo equal-power subgroups exchanged position papers and vigorously critiqued each other; evaluation kept separate from creation
Consideration of alternativesRapid convergence on one plan; no competing options presentedBalanced consideration of multiple alternatives, each argued to the president

Same president, similar stakes, wildly different processes — and wildly different outcomes. This is the strongest evidence in the whole course that process, not talent or intentions, is what separates a functional decision-making team from a disastrous one.

Dealing with dysfunctional conflict

Recognize dysfunctional conflict can happen, and build a structure that both prevents it and still allows functional conflict: psychological safety, a common goal, team identity, trust, an appropriate incentive structure, a team contract, and room for controlled "venting" discussion of conflict rather than suppressing it entirely.

Managing interpersonal conflict directly

Practical steps for a one-on-one conflict conversation: talk with other members first to confirm your own perception isn't off; assess whether the issue is actually important enough to raise; list the specific behaviors you've observed, not vague impressions; have tentative suggestions ready, not just complaints; listen carefully to the other person's view; use supportive communication (descriptive, problem-solving oriented, empathetic); pick the right communication channel; stay in the present tense and negotiate future changes rather than relitigating the past; and personalize the confrontation ("I get frustrated when I see X" rather than "you always do X").

Exam angleBe ready to name specifically which process differences moved the Kennedy administration from the Bay of Pigs failure to the Cuban Missile Crisis success — this table is a favorite source for "identify what changed" questions, and the answer is almost always about process (devil's advocates, separating plan-creation from plan-evaluation, breaking status hierarchies), not about the people involved.

Hansen, M. T. (2018, January 10). "How to Have a Good Debate in a Meeting." Harvard Business Review.

Big idea

The whole point of a meeting is to have a discussion you can't have any other way — yet most meetings avoid real debate entirely. Based on a study of 5,000 managers and employees, Hansen found the best performers are specifically skilled at generating rigorous, respectful conflict in meetings, not at avoiding it.

Six tactics for a good fight

  1. Start with a question, not your opinion. A well-framed, non-leading question invites real debate; announcing your view first (as one CEO did, going around the table for a rubber-stamp) kills it.
  2. Help quiet people speak up; don't let talkers dominate. "Warm call" people before the meeting — tell them their view matters and you want it heard.
  3. Make it safe to take risks. Create Edmondson-style psychological safety by leading with a risky idea yourself, supporting those who try, and sanctioning ridicule. Only 19% of managers in Hansen's study were good at this — but they performed dramatically better (a 0.63 correlation, on a 0–1.0 scale).
  4. Take the contrarian view. American Express CEO Harvey Golub would deliberately argue the opposite of the proposal on the table — asking whether to lower a price when the meeting was about raising it — to force people to defend their actual position with solid reasoning. A devil's advocate role works the same way.
  5. Dissect the most fundamental assumptions. The Bay of Pigs Invasion failed partly because JFK's team never seriously challenged the assumption that a small invasion force would spark a popular uprising against Castro. Go after assumptions "like a prosecutor goes after a criminal."
  6. Cultivate transparent advocates; get rid of the hard sell. A transparent advocate shows a plan's flaws along with its strengths; a hard sell (like a used-car salesperson) hides the negatives. Watch for confirmation bias (only noticing supportive data) and escalation of commitment (continuing to defend a plan despite mounting bad news). A pre-mortem — "assuming this fails, why?" — is a concrete countermeasure.
Exam angleThis source directly cites Edmondson and the Google study from earlier units — expect a question asking you to connect Hansen's tactic #3 back to Unit 2's psychological safety material specifically, not just as a generic "be nice" gesture.

Kantor, J., & Streitfeld, D. (2015, August 15). "Inside Amazon: Wrestling Big Ideas in a Bruising Workplace." The New York Times.

Big idea

A deep, deliberately ambivalent investigation into Amazon's culture, built on Jeff Bezos's 14 Leadership Principles — a culture that produces genuine innovation and enormous individual opportunity, alongside brutal internal competition, anonymous peer sabotage, forced ranking, and harsh treatment of employees during health and family crises. This is the case study the rest of the unit is built to test.

The system, piece by piece

  • Leadership Principles — 14 rules including Customer Obsession (#1), Ownership (#2), Hire and Develop the Best (#5), Think Big (#7), Bias for Action (#8), Frugality (#9), Earn Trust (#10), Dive Deep (#12), and Disagree and Commit (#13).
  • Disagree and commit — rip into a colleague's idea with blunt, even painful feedback during debate, then fully align behind whatever decision is made.
  • The Anytime Feedback Tool — lets any employee send praise or criticism about a colleague straight to that colleague's manager. Bosses see who sent it; the subject usually doesn't. Many employees described it as a channel for sabotage — making quiet pacts to bury the same person at once, or trade lavish praise.
  • Organization Level Review — an annual, semi-open forced-ranking session (what other companies call "stack ranking" or "rank and yank," a practice Microsoft, GE, and Accenture have since dropped). Former HR executive Robin Andrulevich called the resulting annual culling "Purposeful Darwinism."
  • The human cost — multiple former employees described being placed on performance improvement plans or edged out after cancer treatment, miscarriage, or other personal crises, rather than given time to recover.

The central tension

Amazon's own language sounds like Hansen's healthy-debate playbook: rigorous critique, "never settle," disagreement encouraged. But look at the mechanism. Hansen's and Edmondson's frameworks both require that people can speak up without fear of retribution. Amazon's feedback tool and stack-ranking system tie disagreement directly to anonymous reporting and elimination — arguably the opposite of psychological safety, even while using its vocabulary.

Exam angleThe most likely essay/discussion prompt in this unit: is Amazon's culture an example of healthy, Hansen-style debate, or does it violate Edmondson-style psychological safety? Good answers will name the specific mechanism (anonymous feedback tied to ranking and firing) rather than just asserting an opinion either way.

Likely exam & discussion angles

  • Given a scenario where a subordinate has information a leader needs but never says anything, explain what specifically broke down (culture, channel, or both) and connect it to "NASA Chicken" and the humiliation factor.
  • Given a corporate wrongdoing scenario, apply Bregman's three-step framework to identify where the failure most likely occurred.
  • Design a meeting agenda item using at least three of Hansen's six tactics for a described decision the class is debating.
  • Explain, specifically, why Hansen's tactic #3 (psychological safety) is not just a restatement of "be nice" — tie it back to Edmondson's actual definition from Unit 2.
  • The signature prompt for this unit: is Amazon's culture an example of Hansen-style healthy debate, or does it violate Edmondson-style psychological safety? Support your answer by naming the specific mechanism (e.g., the Anytime Feedback Tool's anonymity, or its link to stack ranking) rather than just asserting an opinion.
  • Cross-unit synthesis: compare NASA's 2003 culture of silence to Amazon's 2015 culture of aggressive conflict — two very different failure modes (too little disagreement vs. disagreement that isn't actually safe) that both violate the same underlying principle.
