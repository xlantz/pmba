Defining marketing for the new realities

Marketing is about identifying and meeting human and social needs in a way that fits the organization's goals — value creation is both the starting point and the end goal, and selling is only the tip of the iceberg. Four forces have reshaped markets and pushed companies toward holistic marketing, where the whole company, not just the marketing department, creates value.

Chapter 1, section by section

Two definitions, one shared idea

The social definition of marketing is a societal process by which individuals fulfill their needs by creating and freely exchanging products and services of value with others. The managerial definition is the art and science of identifying, attracting, and retaining customers through creating, delivering, and communicating superior customer value. They sound different, but both hinge on the same idea: value. Drucker put it sharply: "the aim of marketing is to make selling superfluous." If you understand the customer well enough, the product sells itself — the Nintendo Wii, Apple iPad, and Toyota Prius were all swamped with orders because of marketing homework, not great retail selling.

Ten things that get marketed

Marketing isn't just physical goods. The textbook lists ten domains: goods (cars, food), services (airlines, consultants — a growing share of advanced economies, roughly two-thirds of U.S. output), events (the Olympics, trade shows), experiences (Disney's Magic Kingdom), persons (Oprah, Michael Jordan as brands), places (Las Vegas's "What Happens Here, Stays Here"), properties (real estate, stocks — intangible ownership rights), organizations (museums, nonprofits), information (Nielsen, Gartner), and ideas (social causes like Coca-Cola's "World Without Waste").

Companies that failed to update their business models — Yahoo!, Blockbuster, Barnes & Noble — were overtaken by Google, Netflix, and Amazon. Fresh ideas tend to come from three underrepresented groups: employees with contemporary or diverse perspectives, employees far from headquarters, and employees new to the industry.

Four forces reshaping every market

Four forces are pushing companies toward holistic marketing:

ForceWhat's changing
TechnologyE-commerce, mobile, cloud, and AI enable new business models (Netflix, Airbnb, Uber) and one-to-one interaction at scale; analytics and AI have been democratized so smaller companies can use them too.
GlobalizationFriedman's "the world is flat" — countries are more multicultural, and most of the next billion middle-class consumers will likely be Asian. Outsourcing cuts costs but risks jobs, IP, and supply-chain disruption.
Physical environmentClimate change (extreme weather, rising seas) and health conditions (pandemics) are reshaping where and how companies operate.
Social responsibilityMarketers must weigh the ethical, environmental, legal, and social context of their work — satisfying target markets while preserving or enhancing consumers' and society's long-term well-being.

Those four forces shape three new market outcomes: new consumer capabilities (buy anywhere after researching in-store, or "showrooming"; share opinions on social media; reject unwanted marketing), new company capabilities (mass personalization, richer customer data, more efficient reach), and a new competitive environment (deregulation, retail transformation, and a tug-of-war between disintermediation and reintermediation).

Exam angleDisintermediation and reintermediation are opposites, and it's an easy pair to flip under time pressure. Disintermediation is a direct-to-consumer startup (Warby Parker, Casper, Away) cutting out the middleman. Reintermediation is a traditional firm fighting back by blending online and physical — "brick-and-click."

Holistic marketing's four components

ComponentWhat it is
Relationship marketingBuilding mutually satisfying long-term relationships with four constituents — customers, employees, marketing partners, and the financial community — to earn and retain their business. The end result is a marketing network.
Integrated marketingCoordinating every activity so it delivers consistent value and message; "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts." A GE MRI sale comes bundled with installation, maintenance, and training.
Internal marketingHiring, training, and motivating employees who want to serve customers well — needs both vertical alignment (with senior management) and horizontal alignment (with other departments).
Performance marketingUnderstanding the financial and nonfinancial returns to business and society — brand equity, customer lifetime value, and ROMI. Ben & Jerry's went from a "double" to a "triple bottom line" (people, planet, profits).

The market-value concept favors relationships over transactions, integrated marketing over piecemeal manual actions, a strong culture over disengaged employees, and science over intuition.

Five philosophies, from inside-out to outside-in

Companies tend to operate from one of five philosophies, moving from a company-centered ("inside-out") view toward a customer-centered ("outside-in") one:

PhilosophyCore beliefWhen it fits / the risk
ProductionConsumers want products that are widely available and inexpensive.Fits developing markets or an expanding market; focuses on efficiency and mass distribution (Lenovo, Haier in China).
ProductConsumers want the highest quality, performance, or innovative features.Risk: the "better-mousetrap fallacy" — believing a better product sells itself without pricing, distribution, and promotion.
SellingLeft alone, buyers won't buy enough, so push hard.Used for unsought goods (insurance, cemetery plots) or overcapacity; risky as a general strategy.
MarketingCustomer-centered; sense and respond (mid-1950s)."Find the right products for your customers, not customers for your products" (Dell's build-your-own platform). Levitt: selling focuses on the seller's need to turn product into cash; marketing focuses on the buyer's needs.
Market-valueHolistic; every functional area creates value for customers, company, and collaborators.Define the business as a customer-satisfying process, not a product — products are transient, needs endure.

Concrete illustration: Pepsi. With a product view, its market is carbonated soft-drink drinkers and its rivals are other soda companies. With a market-value view, its market is anyone who is thirsty, so its rivals include water, juice, tea, and coffee. The same shift shows up across Table 1.1 in the book: Union Pacific went from "we run a railroad" to "we move people and goods"; Xerox from "we make copying equipment" to "we help improve office productivity"; Carrier from "we make air conditioners and furnaces" to "we provide climate control in the home."

Exam angleWatch for a scenario describing a company that just built a better product and assumed it would sell itself — that's the product-concept trap (the "better-mousetrap fallacy"), not the marketing concept. And don't confuse the marketing concept (sense and respond to the customer) with the broader market-value concept (every function in the company creates value, not just the marketing department).

Flipping the org chart

A traditional org chart puts top management at the top and customers at the bottom. A customer-oriented chart inverts it: customers on top, then frontline people who deal with them directly, then service (middle) managers who support the frontline, and top management at the bottom, whose job is to hire and support good managers. Bezos: customer focus drives proactivity — "we invent before we have to."

Low customer-centricityHigh customer-centricity
Product drivenMarket driven
Mass market focusedCustomer focused
Process orientedOutcome oriented
Reacting to competitorsMaking competitors irrelevant
Price drivenValue driven
Hierarchical organizationTeamwork

Being customer-oriented is necessary but not enough: the firm must also be creative, since rivals copy advantages fast. Even strong companies miss trends this way — Nestlé missed the coffeehouse trend (Starbucks), and Coca-Cola was slow on Snapple-type drinks and Gatorade.

Organizing the marketing department

StructureHow it works
FunctionalSpecialists report to a CMO. The most common form. Simple to administer, but groups compete for budget and status.
GeographicSales (sometimes marketing) organized by region, with area market specialists for key markets.
Product / brandAn extra layer on top of functional, hub-and-spoke with the brand manager at the center. Fast response and a focused advocate for small brands, but little authority, little functional expertise, and short tenures that push toward short-term, market-share-driven thinking.
Category managementA variation on brand management: manage by product category (P&G, Diageo). Reduces internal brand-vs-brand rivalry and matches how powerful retailers like Walmart and Target actually think.
MarketOrganized around distinct user groups (Canon: consumer, business, government). Market managers are staff, not line. Xerox, IBM, and HP all reorganized by industry.
MatrixBoth product and market managers. Costly and prone to conflict over authority.

The CMO sits in the C-suite, usually reports to the CEO, and leads product development, brand, communication, research, sales, promotion, distribution, pricing, and service. Their single most important role: bringing the customer perspective into every touch point. David Packard's line still holds: "Marketing is far too important to leave to the marketing department" — CMOs coordinate through persuasion, not authority, and partner with the CFO on ROI measures.

Exam angleP&G's shift from brand management to category management is a favorite scenario: know why firms make that switch (retailers manage shelf space by category, not by brand, and category management cuts internal brand-vs-brand competition for resources) even though brand management gives a single product a more focused advocate.
