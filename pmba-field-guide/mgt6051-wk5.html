Schein: Organizational Socialization and the Profession of Management (1968)

ConceptOne-line meaningExample (invented)
Organizational socializationThe process of 'learning the ropes': being taught what matters in an organization or unit. It recurs at every new school, job, department or rank.A newly promoted supervisor relearns what counts in her new team.
Price of membershipOnly the values, norms and behaviors the organization requires of any new member count as socialization, not all learning. They cover goals, preferred means, role responsibilities, required behavior and rules protecting the organization's identity.Never criticize the firm in public; always return client calls the same day.
UnfreezingIf a newcomer's values are out of line, the first phase loosens the old self so new values can be learned. Initiation rites and hazing are extreme forms.A hotshot MBA hire is told to shadow for three months before touching a client.
Upending experiencesPlanned or accidental events that disconfirm the newcomer's assumptions about self, company or job: trivial work, impossible work, practice-only work, unread reports, long watching.A manager hands a cocky engineer a circuit that breaks the textbook yet sells fine, and asks why it works.
Why people tolerate itSuccess depends on the entrant's initial motivation and on how costly leaving is (pension and stock plans, a year of learning invested, 'start over elsewhere').'You just spent a year learning our systems; why restart?'
Peer groupsNovices band together to defend against pressure and to work out norms. If group norms support the organization they speed socialization; otherwise they can breed rebellion. Many organizations encourage them anyway.A cohort that studies together and keeps the firm's standards.
Where learning comes fromOfficial literature, key models, instructions from boss or trainer, peers acting as 'big brothers' who pass on subtleties, and rewards and punishments from your own experiments.A senior colleague quietly explains what the boss really wants.
Commitment mechanisms(1) Invest effort and time in the newcomer so loyalty feels owed; (2) get small behavioral commitments the person later justifies by adopting the values; also promoting a rebel into responsibility, and appeals to general ideals such as 'the good of the company'.A critic gets her own team and starts defending the policy she once mocked.
Becoming a full memberTransition events (real responsibility, titles, status symbols, confidential information) signal acceptance and help values become part of identity.Being handed the key account and access to the budget.

Type 1 RebellionNo values or normsPerson is expelled or works to defeat the goals.
Type 2 Creative individualismOnly the pivotal norms; rejects the restThe response organizations should aim for.
Type 3 ConformityAll values and normsCreativity is curbed; drift toward sterile bureaucracy.
Norm typeMeaningConsequence of breaking it
PivotalMust be accepted as the price of membership (e.g., getting the job done).Strongest reward and punishment; you will not last.
RelevantDesirable but not required (dress, decorum, not criticizing the firm publicly). Some may be pivotal in some units.Milder pressure.
PeripheralUnimportant to the organization's functioning (the text also says 'irrelevant').Little or none. Schein warns bosses against over-teaching these.

Schein: professional versus organizational socialization

Schools socialize students toward a profession's values; the first job socializes them to a specific organization. Professional values stress general principles, expert knowledge, objectivity toward clients, status by achievement, service over self-interest, loyalty to colleagues and codes of ethics. Organizations ask for loyalty to one firm, local know-how and obedience to the boss. Schein says these often conflict, so graduates postpone their professional values or rebel: in his panel 73% of the class of 1962 had already left their first job, as had 67% of 1963 and 50% of 1964. Recommendations: schools add apprenticeship, a practicum on changing organizations, and career-planning help. Companies should understand their own socialization practices, focus on pivotal values only, and train the first boss, who creates the climate for rebellion, conformity or creative individualism.

Thomas & Griffin: The Power of Social Information in the Workplace (1989)

ConceptOne-line meaningExample (invented)
Social informationComments, observations and cues from people whose view of the job the employee treats as relevant; employees respond to the job as they perceive it, not only the 'objective' job.Two reps with identical territories describe the company as fair vs ripping them off (the Adams and Woods case).
Three processes(1) Salience: what gets talked about seems important. (2) Evaluation: attitudes form and cues suggest what to do. (3) Justification: people explain or adjust their views to fit what others say.A team that complains about the clock-in system makes it a top issue.
Four sourcesCo-workers (daily contact, credible, reinforce views); supervisors (management's voice and sometimes a friend; the source management controls most); friends and family (outside reference point, support, may seem more objective or naive); customers or clients (evaluation of the company and its products).A spouse asks 'isn't that boring?' and changes how you see your work.
Asch light experimentAlone, under 1% misjudged line length; when confederates gave wrong answers, over a third went along. If people bend on objective facts they bend more on vague job features.You agree 'the meetings are pointless' because everyone else does.
New vs experienced employeesNewcomers are more receptive. With experience the number of sources shrinks while reliance on a few trusted ones can grow.Week-one hires absorb every hallway opinion; veterans listen to two people.
Objective job vs social informationIf survey responses come from the objective job, redesigning the job should change them; if they come from social cues, changing the job may do little until the cues change. Griffin's field experiment: managers trained to give positive cues shifted perceptions without changing the job.Redesigning tasks fails because the break-room story is still 'this place is a dead end'.
Manager guidelinesBe aware of which sources employees use; you cannot control family and customers; supervisors are the key lever ('for many employees the supervisor is the organization'); placing a newcomer with a supportive group helps only briefly and can look manipulative.A boss who jokes about top management teaches the team to distrust it.

Main claim: wages, full-time benefits and bonuses are essential, but social aspects of hiring and onboarding, above all mutual respect and trust, matter more for keeping young workers. Based on interviews with workforce development professionals.

7A culture where people ask questionsRespectful treatment when they reveal ignorance.

Week 4: Onboarding & culture
Organizational socialization
Schein: the process of 'learning the ropes'; learning the values and norms required of any new member (the price of membership).
Week 4: Onboarding & culture
Upending experience
Schein: planned or accidental event (trivial work, impossible work, unread reports) that disconfirms a newcomer's assumptions.
Week 4: Onboarding & culture
Pivotal, relevant, peripheral norms
Schein: pivotal = required for membership; relevant = desirable; peripheral = largely unimportant.
Week 4: Onboarding & culture
Rebellion / creative individualism / conformity
Schein's three responses: reject all norms; accept only pivotal ones; accept all.
Week 4: Onboarding & culture
Social information
Thomas & Griffin: cues from co-workers, supervisors, friends/family and customers that shape how employees see their job.
Week 4: Onboarding & culture
Hygiene vs motivator
General background (Herzberg): hygiene prevents dissatisfaction; motivators create engagement.

Before the quiz

Likely quiz angles

  • Order Ingram & Choi's five steps and label values-as-magic vs smoke screen.
  • Match a leader to one of the four Toy Factory styles and predict performance versus satisfaction.
  • Name which of Jordan et al.'s stages or Stage-3 approaches a story shows.
  • Place a K&P commitment under the right practice.
  • Identify which of the four FAE causes a scenario illustrates.
  • Tell structured from unstructured and behavioral from situational interview questions.
  • Match an onboarding failure to one of the ten lessons.
  • Separate easily confused pairs: motivator vs hygiene, values vs beliefs, practice vs commitment, pivotal vs relevant norms, social information vs the objective job.
