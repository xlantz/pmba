Unit 1

What makes a group a team?

Three lenses on the same question, in the order you should hold them: Katzenbach & Smith give you the structural discipline of a real team; Hackman argues most attempts fail badly and tells you why; Google's Project Aristotle shows that once you control for structure, the norm that decides everything is psychological safety.

Orientation

Three lenses, held at once

Don't read these as three competing answers — they're three layers of the same problem. Structure without norms stalls; norms without structure has nothing to organize.

Katzenbach & Smith (optimist)Hackman (skeptic)Duhigg / Google (culture researcher)
Core claim Real teams are the most productive unit management has — if the six elements are in place. Teams are oversold. Coordination and motivation losses mean a team can be worse than no team at all. Team composition barely predicts success. Team norms — especially psychological safety — predict almost everything.
Evidence style Case narratives (Burlington Northern, Kodak, Garden State Brickface…) Research studies and statistics (executive surveys, orchestras, aviation, NASA) Internal data science (180 Google teams) + one academic study (Woolley et al., Science 2010)

Where they agree: all three deny that talent, personality fit, or good vibes alone explain success — real teams need clear structure and healthy interaction norms.

Katzenbach, J. R., & Smith, D. K. (1993). The Wisdom of Teams: Creating the High-Performance Organization, Ch. 3, "Team Basics."

Big idea

A "team" is not just any group that works together or shares good values — it's a specific, disciplined unit defined by six elements. The single most important driver of whether a group becomes a real team is a relentless focus on performance, not chemistry or togetherness.

"A team is a small number of people with complementary skills who are committed to a common purpose, performance goals, and approach for which they hold themselves mutually accountable."

The six-element framework

  • Small number — typically 2–25 people, most successful teams under 10. Groups of 50+ can't operate as one real team; they should form real subteams that create an "extended team" ripple effect on the larger group.
  • Complementary skills, in three categories: technical/functional expertise, problem-solving & decision-making, and interpersonal skills. Key nuance: teams rarely have every needed skill at the start — they develop them through the discipline of pursuing the goal.
  • Common, meaningful purpose — usually originates from a "solution space" management sets (clear enough boundaries, loose enough for ownership), then the team makes it their own through ongoing "purposing" — continuous revisiting that never really stops.
  • Specific performance goals — concrete, measurable targets. Purpose gives meaning and emotional energy; goals give trackability, focus, "small wins," and accountability.
  • Common working approach — an economic/administrative side (how work gets divided, scheduled, decided) and a social side (emergent roles: challenging, interpreting, supporting, integrating, remembering, summarizing).
  • Mutual accountability — the defining test. "The boss holds me accountable" (hierarchy, individual) vs. "We hold ourselves accountable" (team, mutual). Only the second makes it a team.

Traps to avoid

  • Teamwork values ≠ a team. Sharing, cooperating, and being nice does not by itself create a team without a specific shared purpose and goals.
  • Real team vs. extended team. A real team is small and meets all six elements. An extended team is a larger group whose performance is stimulated by a real team's influence, without the larger group being bound by mutual accountability.
  • Individual accountability ≠ mutual accountability. A group of highly accountable individuals can still fail to be a team if they never hold themselves jointly responsible for a shared result.
  • Purpose is rarely self-generated. Most teams shape their purpose in response to a management-defined solution space, not from a blank slate.

Why team size matters: the coordination-links problem

Every added member adds a link to every existing member — the count grows as n(n−1)/2, not linearly. This is why Hackman caps his own teams at 6 and warns against "double digits."

Every line is one relationship someone on the team has to manage. A 4-person team needs 6 relationships; a 10-person team needs 45.

Team Performance Curve — five stages

StageWhat it is
1. Working groupOutput = sum of "individual bests"; no collective work-product pursued
2. Pseudo-teamAbandoned the discipline, often stuck in conflict (like Tuckman's "storming") — the lowest point on the curve, even below the working group
3. Potential teamMoving the right direction, real but partial impact; purpose/goals/accountability still incomplete
4. Real teamAll six elements in place — the minimum bar for a successful project team
5. High-performing teamEverything a real team has, plus deep commitment to each other's personal growth
"The steepest performance gain comes between a potential team and a real team."
Exam angleGiven a scenario with heavy conflict and no shared accountability, place it precisely on the curve (likely "pseudo-team") instead of just saying "not a real team" — and be ready to explain why a pseudo-team can score worse than a plain working group. Also watch for questions on what exactly separates "potential" from "real" — the sources flag that gap as the biggest performance lever.

Coutu, D. (2009, May). "Why Teams Don't Work" (interview with J. Richard Hackman). Harvard Business Review.

Big idea

The skeptic's counterweight to Katzenbach & Smith. Hackman's research-backed argument: the near-religious belief that teams are always better than individuals is wrong. Teams frequently underperform because of coordination and motivation losses — "having a team is often worse than having no team at all." This raises the bar rather than contradicting the team-basics discipline: teams are hard to get right, and most attempts fail the basics badly.

Hackman's five conditions for team effectiveness

  • Real team — bounded membership. Across 120+ senior executive teams, fewer than 10% of members agreed on who was even on their team. Fix: "ruthless decisions about membership" (see the CFO example).
  • Compelling direction — emotionally demanding to set, because it requires exercising authority, which creates real risk (resistance, pushback, even career risk) for the leader.
  • Enabling structure — team design (size, task, membership) that sets the team up to succeed rather than fight itself.
  • Supportive context — reward, information, and training systems that actually reinforce teamwork instead of only optimizing individuals.
  • Expert coaching — team-focused (not individual-focused), delivered at the right moments.

Case: the "team destroyer" CFO

A large financial services firm's CFO was skilled but unwilling to seek collective solutions. The CEO kept him in his role but excluded him from the executive committee, communicating with him separately. Result: the CFO was happier without meetings, and the committee finally functioned as a real team. Illustrates: boundedness sometimes requires actively removing capable people from the team.

Myths vs. what the research shows

MythWhat the research shows
Harmonious ("happy") teams perform betterHackman's symphony study: grumpy orchestras played slightly better. Causality is backwards — good performance creates satisfaction, not the reverse.
Bigger teams are better (more resources)Links to manage between members grow near-exponentially with size. "No double digits" — Hackman caps his own course teams at 6.
Teams get stale over timeLittle evidence for general staleness (exception: R&D teams benefit from a small talent infusion every 3–4 years). The real problem is usually too much turnover, not too little.

Why team newness is a liability

  • NTSB data: 73% of aviation incidents occurred on a crew's very first day flying together; 44% of those on the crew's very first flight.
  • NASA study: fatigued crews with a history of working together made about half as many errors as rested crews who'd never flown together.
  • Airlines schedule pilots/planes as interchangeable units for financial efficiency (one estimate: 5.6 years before the same two people are paired again).
  • Counterexample — Strategic Air Command (SAC): crews trained and stayed together for years and outperformed every other flight crew studied. When zero-error, real-time performance is required, team stability is worth the efficiency cost.

The "deviant"

A team member who resists excess homogeneity and consensus, asking "why are we even doing this?" Teams with a deviant outperformed teams without one on originality — but deviants pay a personal cost (they raise the group's anxiety, and leaders often push them off the team).

Team coaching: timing matters

Coaching individuals did little to improve senior executive team performance — coaching has to target group process, delivered at three points: launch (orient members to the task), midpoint (review what's working/not, correct strategy), and end (reflect on lessons). The first few minutes of a team's existence disproportionately shape its whole life — conductor Christopher Hogwood: "All I have is the first few minutes."

Should we always default to teams?

No. The "build a house" pop-quiz example: the assumed-obvious answer (a team finishes faster) is often wrong. An overly strong "team player" culture can also suppress dissent — in one team Hackman studied, members self-censored real concerns to preserve harmony and the team pursued a plan headed for failure.

Exam angleUse this reading to argue the other side of a "should we use a team here?" question, or to critique a case where a team failed — check for unbounded membership, unclear direction, an unsupportive reward system, coaching aimed at individuals instead of the group, or a suppressed "deviant." This is also the natural source to cite for research evidence (numbers, studies) rather than narrative case examples.

Duhigg, C. (2016, February 25). The New York Times Magazine.

Big idea

A third lens. Where Katzenbach & Smith focus on structural discipline and Hackman on skepticism/conditions, this article — reporting on Google's internal "Project Aristotle" research — argues that who is on a team matters far less than how the team members interact, and identifies psychological safety as the single most important team norm.

Origin story: Julia Rozovsky at Yale

Her assigned MBA study group — teammates from similar high-achieving backgrounds — was stressful: leadership fights, one-upmanship, constant self-editing. Her voluntary case-competition team — wildly different professional backgrounds (Army officer, nonprofit director, researcher) — was fun, supportive, and won. Same person, similarly capable teammates, opposite experiences. The difference wasn't who was on the team, it was how the team treated each other.

What Google tested and found didn't matter (composition)

Project Aristotle analyzed 180 teams for composition patterns: personality mix, shared hobbies, similar educational backgrounds, introvert/extrovert balance, tenure together, gender balance. No consistent pattern emerged.

"The 'who' part of the equation didn't seem to matter." — Abeer Dubey, Google People Analytics

What did matter: group norms, especially psychological safety

Psychological safety (Amy Edmondson, 1999): "A shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking" — confidence the team won't embarrass, reject, or punish someone for speaking up.

Google's data pointed to psychological safety as the norm most critical to team success — ahead of (but alongside) having clear goals and a culture of dependability.

The academic backbone: Woolley et al., Science (2010) — "collective intelligence"

699 participants, small groups, varied collaborative tasks (brainstorm uses for a brick; plan a shopping trip with conflicting individual grocery lists requiring trade-offs). Finding: teams that did well on one task did well on all of them — evidence of a group-level "collective intelligence" distinct from individual smarts. Two behaviors distinguished the high-collective-intelligence teams:

  • Equality in conversational turn-taking — everyone spoke roughly the same amount over the course of the tasks.
  • High average social sensitivity — skill at reading others' emotions/thoughts from nonverbal cues, measured via the "Reading the Mind in the Eyes" test.

Thought experiment: Team A vs. Team B

Team ATeam B
All highly accomplished individuals; efficient, agenda-driven; one expert speaks at length per topic; no idle chat; ends on time.Mixed accomplishment levels; people interrupt, jump in, go on tangents; socializes before/after the "real" agenda.

Team B wins on collective intelligence — despite looking less impressive, equal turn-taking and social sensitivity make it more than the sum of its parts. Team A's efficiency masks unequal airtime and low emotional read — the opposite of psychological safety.

Case: Matt Sakaguchi

A Google manager (former SWAT team member) ran a norms survey on his new team; results showed members felt unclear about their role and the impact of their work, despite Sakaguchi believing the team was strong. At an off-site, he opened by disclosing his Stage 4 cancer diagnosis — an act of vulnerability that led other members to share personal struggles, after which the team found it far easier to discuss real workplace friction and adopt new norms. Illustrates: a leader modeling vulnerability can be a catalyst for psychological safety — though the article stresses this isn't the only path; "any group can become Team B."

Postscript: Rozovsky's "Ouch" email

Rozovsky made a mistake in a meeting; a teammate's terse "Ouch" reply stung. Rather than let it fester, she used the psychological-safety vocabulary directly: "Nothing like a good 'Ouch!' to destroy psych safety in the morning." Her teammate replied, "Just testing your resilience," and the tension defused in one exchange. Illustrates: naming a norm explicitly, with shared vocabulary/data behind it, makes it easier to address small friction before it compounds.

Exam angleUse this reading whenever a question asks why a well-credentialed team underperforms, or what one change would most improve a struggling team — the answer is almost always "build psychological safety" rather than "change who's on it." Also strong for contrasting composition-based explanations of success (skills, personality fit) against process/culture-based explanations (norms, safety) — cite the 180-team null result as direct evidence against pure composition-based thinking.

Before the exam

Likely exam & discussion angles

  • Given a case or a group you've worked in, argue whether it is a "real team" using the six elements as your rubric.
  • Explain why teamwork values alone aren't sufficient — use the Sandy Charlap / Dutchess Day School example.
  • Contrast "the boss holds me accountable" with "we hold ourselves accountable," and explain why this is the pivotal test.
  • Given a dysfunctional group (heavy conflict, no shared goals), place it on the performance curve — most likely a pseudo-team — and explain what would move it toward "potential," then "real."
  • Argue the skeptic's side: make the case that a team is the wrong tool for a given job (Hackman's "build a house" example or the coordination-cost argument).
  • Diagnose a failing team against Hackman's five conditions — which one is missing, and how do you know?
  • Explain why removing a talented person from a team (the CFO example) can be the correct move.
  • Explain why "the right skill mix" (K&S) isn't sufficient — cite Google's 180-team null result on composition.
  • Given a meeting transcript, judge its collective intelligence using turn-taking equality and social sensitivity.
  • Explain how a leader can build psychological safety without a dramatic personal disclosure like Sakaguchi's.
