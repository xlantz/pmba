ACCTG 6000 · Unit 9

Intercorporate investments

What happens when one company owns shares of another. The accounting depends on one question: how much say does the owner have? A small stake is just an investment (fair value). A big stake with real influence uses the equity method. A controlling stake means the two companies are reported as one (consolidation). Read top to bottom and you go from “why would a company own another company?” to every number on the exam. The professor's 30 practice problems are linked below.

Step 1 · Start here

Ownership is a dial, and the accounting follows the dial

The more of another company you own, and the more say you have, the more of it shows up in your own statements.

Think of a restaurant

Own a few shares of a restaurant chain and you are a customer with a stock certificate: you just track what the shares are worth. Own a quarter, with a seat at the owners' table, and its success is part of your success, so you count your share of its profits. Own most of it and you run it: you report its kitchen, its cash and its bills as if they were your own, and tell readers which slice belongs to the minority owners.

Five words to know first

Investee
The company whose shares are being owned.
Significant influence
A real voice in its decisions (about 20–50%).
Control
Power to direct it (over 50%).
Subsidiary
A company you control.
NCI
“Noncontrolling interest”: the part of a subsidiary the outsiders own.

Tiny example: one $1,000 profit, three ways

Investee earns $1,000 and pays out $400. You own…

10% (passive): income = dividend = $40
30% (influence): income = 30% × $1,000 = $300; dividend $120 reduces the investment
60% (control): report 100% of its revenues and expenses; NCI gets 40% × $1,000 = $400

These percentages are presumptions, not laws. Substantial board representation can show influence at 15%. A 25% owner who has no board seat, no say in policy and faces a 70% controlling holder may have no real influence. Ability to influence is what matters.

The usual progression as a relationship deepens: passive → equity method → consolidation.

Exam anglePick the method from the ownership %, then check the facts for influence. Know why each method fits (influence means the results are tied together; control means one economic entity).

A passive investor can't influence the investee, so it just tracks market value. Two sources of income:

  • Dividends received = income (Dr Cash / Cr Dividend Income), using only the investor's own share.
  • Change in fair value (even unrealized) goes through net income: Dr Investment / Cr Unrealized Gain.

Examples

Buy 20,000 sh at $18; year-end price $22
Unrealized gain = 20,000 × ($22 − $18) = $80,000

5% holder; investee pays $600,000 of dividends
Dividend income = 5% × $600,000 = $30,000

If a 10% investee earns $2,000,000 but pays no dividends and the price doesn't change, the investor's income is $0. Investee earnings don't matter to a passive holder.

Exam angleGain = shares × price change. Dividend income = ownership % × total dividends. No dividends and no price change means no income.

The investor's fortunes move with the investee, so the investor records its share of earnings as they are earned, not when dividends arrive.

EventEntry
Investee earns NIDr Investment / Cr Equity in Earnings (share of NI)
Investee pays dividendsDr Cash / Cr Investment (share of dividends)

Dividends are not income here: counting both the earnings and the dividend would double count the same profit.

When the price paid exceeds the share of book value

If the investor pays more than its share of the investee's book value, the excess is attributed to specific assets and amortized like them. Makeshift pays $1,500,000 for 25% of Canyon; its share of book value is $1,300,000; the $200,000 excess is equipment with 10 years left.

Share of NI: 25% × $600,000 = $150,000
Excess amortization: $200,000 ÷ 10 = $20,000
Equity-method income = $150,000 − $20,000 = $130,000
Exam angleEnding investment = cost + % × NI − % × dividends (and subtract the excess amortization from income). Investee earns but pays nothing: income and investment both rise. Dividend entry credits the Investment, not income.

With control, the parent and subsidiary are presented as one economic entity. That means:

  • Add 100% of the subsidiary's assets, liabilities, revenues and expenses line by line, even if the parent owns only 80%.
  • Eliminate the parent's Investment in Subsidiary (otherwise the subsidiary is counted twice).
  • Show the outside owners' slice as noncontrolling interest: in equity on the balance sheet and as an attribution of net income.
  • Eliminate intercompany transactions.

Quick checks: parent revenue $8M + subsidiary $3M (no intercompany) = $11M consolidated revenue. A 75% subsidiary earns $1.2M: NCI = 25% × $1.2M = $300,000. Parent standalone NI $500,000 plus 70% of a $300,000 subsidiary = $710,000 attributable to the parent (consolidated NI $800,000, NCI $90,000). An investor's equity-method income line disappears in consolidation and is replaced by the subsidiary's actual revenues and expenses.

Exam angleAlways 100% of the subsidiary's lines. Consolidated NI = parent + 100% of subsidiary; NCI = NCI % × subsidiary NI; attributable to parent = consolidated − NCI. Know why the investment is eliminated (double counting).
Goodwill = (Price paid + Fair value of NCI) − Fair value of identifiable net assets

Goodwill is the leftover value that can't be assigned to a specific asset. If the total is less than or equal to the net assets, there is none.

Three worked cases

Marley: 4,000,000 + NCI 1,000,000 − 5,000,000 = $0
Bing (90% for 5,000,000; net assets 4,400,000):
  implied whole company = 5,000,000 ÷ 0.90 = 5,555,556
  goodwill = 5,555,556 − 4,400,000 = $1,155,555
Blazer (65% for 11,050,000; net assets 11,050,000):
  implied whole = 11,050,000 ÷ 0.65 = 17,000,000
  goodwill = 17,000,000 − 11,050,000 = $5,950,000; NCI = 35% × 17,000,000 = $5,950,000

100% of the subsidiary's assets and liabilities come in at fair value, goodwill is added, the investment is removed, and NCI is credited. Debits and credits both total $20,300,000.

Exam angleCompute goodwill from the formula. If the NCI value isn't given, infer it from the price paid: price ÷ ownership % gives the whole company. Then build the entry: assets and liabilities at 100% of fair value, goodwill, credit investment and NCI.

Selling to yourself isn't a sale. When a parent sells to a subsidiary (or the reverse), the sale and the matching cost are eliminated from the consolidated statements, because only dealings with outsiders count.

If the buyer still holds the goods at year-end, the seller's markup is an unrealized profit: it has not been earned from the consolidated entity's point of view, so it is removed from income and from ending inventory until the goods are sold outside the group.

Exam angleConceptual only: why intercompany sales are eliminated, and why unsold inventory needs an extra adjustment.
Optional · Go deeper

Beyond the exam set

You can skip this and still ace the testThere is no Unit 9 homework yet, so this block just stretches the same ideas one step past the professor's problems.

The “purchase excess” in the equity method and the “fair value adjustments” in consolidation are the same idea. If the target's equipment is worth $200,000 more than its book value, that step-up is depreciated going forward: under the equity method it reduces equity income ($20,000 a year over 10 years); under consolidation it increases depreciation expense on the combined statements. Either way, consolidated net income attributable to the parent equals the parent's equity-method income, which is why the equity method is sometimes called “one-line consolidation.”

Under the full-goodwill approach the NCI is measured at its fair value, which can include a different per-share price than the parent paid (a control premium). When the NCI value isn't given, the exam shortcut is to assume the same value per share.

Traps the exam sets

  • Treating equity-method dividends as income (they reduce the investment).
  • Including only the parent's % of the subsidiary's revenue (consolidation is 100%).
  • Forgetting the excess amortization when the price exceeds the share of book value.
  • Giving a passive investor income from the investee's earnings.
  • Applying the equity method to a 25% holder who clearly has no influence.
  • Forgetting to eliminate the investment account in consolidation.

Before the exam

Likely exam angles

  • Choose the method from the ownership level and the facts about influence.
  • Passive: unrealized gain from a price change; dividend income as % × dividends; zero income when nothing is paid.
  • Equity method: ending investment balance, the share-of-income entry, the dividend entry, and income after amortizing a purchase excess.
  • Consolidation: 100% of revenues, NCI share of income, income attributable to the parent, and why equity income and the investment are eliminated.
  • Goodwill from price, NCI and net assets (inferring NCI when it isn't given), and the full acquisition entry.
  • Conceptual: significant influence, why dividends reduce the investment, why intercompany sales are eliminated, what goodwill represents.
