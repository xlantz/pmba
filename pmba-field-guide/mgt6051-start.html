Part 1, step by step

What the course is

MGT 6051 is about diagnosing and managing human behavior in organizations: understanding the root causes of attitudes and behavior so you can generate energy and commitment and channel them toward organizational goals ethically and responsibly. The course runs in two halves (3.0 units total: 1.5 in the Fall, 1.5 in the Spring). This page covers Part 1. After Part 1 you temporarily receive an “incomplete” until Part 2 is completed (the PMBA office, not the instructor or TA, can help if that affects reimbursement).

The three learning goals

#Goal
1Conceptual and analytical knowledge about behavior at the individual, interpersonal, group and intergroup levels, and how organizational, societal and cultural contexts affect it.
2Awareness of your own and others’ assumptions, motivations and values in managerial interaction.
3Sharper problem definition, a richer set of diagnostic models, and a refined process for generating and selecting action alternatives.

Stated course objectives (short version)

Understand theories of managing and leading people (motivation, commitment, decision making, power and influence), and practice diagnosing organizational problems “from hiring and onboarding, to navigating hierarchy.” Part 1 follows the first half of that arc: values, leadership, hiring, onboarding and culture.

Materials

Kouzes & Posner, The Leadership Challenge, 7th ed. (2023); the Harvard Coursepack (the “H” marks in the schedule, presumably); and weekly Canvas readings and podcasts.

Reading reflection (pass/fail)

Before most in-person sessions you complete an online reflection in a small discussion group that changes each time. The prompt is the same every week: write one short paragraph on each of two required readings/podcasts/videos (max one page in total). Each paragraph needs two parts:

PartWhat it must do
1. PrincipleA brief description and definition, in your own words, of the principle that was most meaningful, and why.
2. ApplicationHow you have seen that principle operate at work or school, using a different example from any already in the text.

It is explicitly not a summary. There are no make-up reflections if you miss one.

Peer discussion (pass/fail)

Continue the discussion by giving a short paragraph of feedback on two (or more) classmates’ reflections in your weekly group. Ask: could you tell they understood the principle and could apply it to a different situation? Respond in an emotionally intelligent way (self-awareness, empathy, perceptiveness); advice is welcome. Peer discussions are not anonymous.

Study-aid note: these pages explain concepts for the exam. The reflections and peer feedback are personal work in your own words, so write them yourself.

The notes page

Either one page, front and back, hand-written by you, or one side (front only), typed by you in 12-pt Times New Roman, printed and brought to class. You must turn the notes in.

Makeup rule

Anyone scoring below 70% can take a makeup the following week; the makeup is capped at 70%, and only the best score counts. In an emergency, make-ups can be arranged with the PMBA office.

Example: a student scores 64% on the midterm, retakes and earns 88% on the makeup. The recorded makeup score is capped at 70%, and since 70 is higher than 64, 70% is the score that counts. A student who scores 72% is not eligible for the makeup.

What the quiz may probeFormat and length differences between quiz and final, the exact notes rules, and the cap at 70% on the makeup.

These are general study strategies, not content. The aim is a page you built yourself, so that making it does most of the studying.

What belongs on a cheat sheet

Put on the pageWhy
Frameworks as short lists or mini-diagramsSteps, categories and styles are hard to reconstruct under time pressure and compress well.
Names, numbers and datesPure recall items are the cheapest to look up and the easiest to blank on.
Confusable pairs with the one-line differenceScenario questions often hinge on telling two similar concepts apart.
One-line trigger for a worked example per frameworkLets you recall a concrete case to test a scenario against.
Cross-week linksBig-picture questions reward seeing the thread across weeks (see the map below).

What to leave off

  • Full paragraphs and copied slide text; if you cannot scan it in seconds, it will not help.
  • Anything you already know cold; spend the space on what you keep forgetting.

How to organize it

  1. Pick zones before writing. One zone per week or topic, plus a corner for confusable pairs and one for numbers, names and dates.
  2. Draft from memory first, then check against the readings and slides and fill gaps. The gaps are your study list.
  3. Use anchors, not sentences. A keyword or name triggers the rest of what you know.
  4. Leave about a tenth of the page blank for last-minute additions.
  5. Format-check against the rules (handwritten both sides, or typed front only, 12-pt Times New Roman) and remember it is collected.
  6. Test it: do a practice quiz using only the page and note which questions needed something that is not on it.

Example of an anchor: instead of a full sentence on how makeups work, write “<70% → makeup, cap 70.”

Space estimate: a typed single side at 12-pt Times New Roman holds roughly 500 to 600 words; a handwritten front and back page generally holds more. (General background, not from the syllabus.)
