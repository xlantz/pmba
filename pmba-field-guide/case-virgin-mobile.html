MKTG 6090 · Case brief · Unit 4 · Week 4

Virgin Mobile USA: Pricing for the Very First Time

A start-up wireless carrier must price its first service for 14-to-24-year-olds in a mature, crowded market. This brief restates the case facts and exhibits, explains how carriers make money and how customer lifetime value is derived, and lays out what a strong written memo has to cover.

Walk the case

Virgin Mobile USA, section by section

The setup

It is 2001–2002. Dan Schulman, a former AT&T executive (18 years) and ex-CEO of Priceline.com, has been recruited by Richard Branson to run Virgin Mobile USA, a new joint venture with Sprint. The service launches in July 2002 into a crowded, mature market. The case is written in his voice and his team's: the team must settle how the service will be priced before launch.

ItemWhat the case says
ProtagonistDan Schulman, CEO of Virgin Mobile USA (CMO Howard Handler also quoted)
DecisionWhich pricing structure to launch with. The case lays out three options and says the team had three constraints: prices competitive, money must be made, and no triggering of competitor reactions.
Growth goals1 million subscribers by end of year one; 3 million by year four (numbers disguised).
Business modelMobile virtual network operator (MVNO): buys minutes on Sprint's PCS network as used, so no big network build.
TargetYoung consumers, 14 to 24 years old; under-served by the big carriers.
Profit testSchulman wants every customer acquired to have positive lifetime value. Exhibit 11 gives the LTV formula and inputs.
What is dueWritten group case (graded) for MKTG 6090 Unit 4 / Week 4. The syllabus lists it in the 10/20 session row; its due note sits under the 10/22 row, so confirm the exact deadline on Canvas.

One-paragraph summary

Virgin Mobile USA enters a market with six national carriers by focusing on a young segment the big players had ignored. It leans on a content deal with MTV, low-cost retail channels such as Target and Best Buy, quirky advertising on a small budget, and cheap handsets. The unresolved piece is pricing. Youth distrust the industry's buckets, peak/off-peak rules and add-on fees, so pricing could be a differentiator, but the choice also drives churn, acquisition cost and profitability.

What the professor will probeBe able to state the facts of the setup cold (who, when, MVNO, Sprint, three options, three constraints), and to separate what the case reports from your own judgments.

Why big carriers had not targeted youth (as the case explains it)

  • Poor credit: many young people have no credit card or fail the credit checks that contracts require (the case later says 30% of prospects failed the checks).
  • An assumption of low usage: if the phone is not for business, carriers expect light use.
  • Acquisition cost versus value: with about $370 to acquire and about $30 a month to serve, low-usage customers look unprofitable.

What the case says about this segment

  • Usage is irregular month to month (school, vacation); they are in flux (college, leaving home, first phone).
  • Open to text messaging, downloads, ring tones, faceplates; the phone is a fashion statement.
  • Under 18, they cannot sign a contract; a parent must.
  • Distrust of hidden charges. The Singapore venture, positioned as hip and trendy, failed, though partners blamed a saturated market.

Target-market evaluation: the generic framework

Textbook segment tests ask whether a segment is measurable, substantial, accessible, differentiable and actionable, and whether it is attractive (size, growth, competition) and fits the firm's strengths. A segment can be attractive and risky at once. Typical risk categories to check against evidence: revenue and usage risk, credit and payment risk, loyalty and churn risk, the reach of your channels and media, and whether larger rivals can follow you in.

What the professor will probeNot "do you like the segment" but evidence for and against: growth and low penetration versus credit, usage, churn and fickleness. Expect a push on why incumbents stayed away and whether that reasoning was right or wrong.

The subscription-with-subsidy economic model

A carrier pays a large up-front acquisition cost (advertising, sales commission, handset subsidy) and then earns a monthly margin (ARPU minus cost to serve) for as long as the customer stays. Profit depends on how many months the customer lasts, which is why churn matters so much and why contracts (a hedge against churn) are central.

Cross-checking the case's acquisition cost

Ranges the case gives for the industry: advertising $75 to $105 per customer acquired (footnote 7), commission about $100 (industry average), handset subsidy typically $100 to $200. Adding the low ends gives 275 and the high ends 405, so the quoted $370 sits inside that range. The case does not itemize exactly how it reached $370.

Is there a financial logic to the pricing structure?

The framework points to several levers the case describes: contracts of one to two years lock in the monthly stream and recoup the subsidy; buckets and overage rates convert uncertain usage into predictable fixed fees; shrinking off-peak windows and add-on fees raise average revenue; credit checks screen for payment risk. Schulman's own claim is that the system earns money from customer confusion and mismatched plans.

Generic worked example (made-up numbers, NOT the case)

Suppose a hypothetical carrier earns $40 a month, spends $20 a month to serve, and pays $240 to acquire. Monthly margin is $20; simple payback is 240 / 20 = 12 months. A customer who stays 8 months loses money; one who stays 30 months earns well over the acquisition cost. This is why a carrier will pay for contracts and credit screens even if it means turning away some customers.

What the professor will probeThe chain acquisition cost, margin, churn and lifetime. Why are contracts, credit checks and handset subsidies logical for a carrier, and why do they simultaneously create customer dissatisfaction?

Company background

  • Virgin (UK) is a brand-extension company with more than 200 entities; successes (Virgin Music, UK mobile with about 2.5M customers in three years) and failures (Virgin Cola, Singapore).
  • UK mobile was the country's first MVNO, leasing capacity from Deutsche Telekom.
  • U.S.: 50-50 joint venture with Sprint; Virgin Mobile buys minutes as used on Sprint's PCS network, which was being upgraded and had spare capacity.

The offer: VirginXtras

Exclusive multi-year content and marketing deal with MTV Networks (MTV, VH1, Nickelodeon content, voting on shows, promotional airtime). Other features: text messaging, online real-time billing (call detail not on a paper bill, for privacy from parents), Rescue Ring, Wake-Up Call, ring tones, Fun Clips, The Hit List, Music Messenger, Movies. The team expected these to attract, increase usage of, and retain young users. Setup: what the case gives you (Exhibit 11)

General formula in the case: LTV = Σ (a = 1 to N) of [ Ma · r(a−1) / (1 + i)a ] − AC

Constant-margin, infinite-life simplification (the one the syllabus says to use): LTV = M / (1 − r + i) − AC

Here M is the margin per period, r the retention rate (r(a−1) is the chance the customer is still there for period a), i the interest rate per period, AC the acquisition cost, paid now (time 0), and not discounted. The case defines monthly margin as ARPU minus CCPU.

Units warning. Exhibit 11 describes N and M as yearly in the general formula, but its inputs (churn, ARPU, CCPU) are monthly. M, r and i must all use the same period. Use r = 1 minus monthly churn. The case says only "interest rates were 5%". The Unit 4 slides treat 5% as annual and divide by 12 (0.05/12 = 0.004167 per month); the exact conversion is 1.051/12 − 1 = 0.4074% per month. Say which you use.

Derivation: the two Unit 4 slide variants

Both variants assume a constant margin m per month and discount at i per month. Let a = r / (1 + i). Because r < 1 + i, a < 1 and the geometric series converges: 1 + a + a² + … = 1 / (1 − a).

Variant 1: "most common" (slide)Variant 2: the case formula (slide)
Timing assumptionFirst margin arrives at the end of month 1 and customers can churn during month 1, so even the first margin is multiplied by rFirst margin arrives at end of month 1; customers cannot churn before the first margin
Seriesm·r/(1+i) + m·r²/(1+i)² + m·r³/(1+i)³ + …m/(1+i) + m·r/(1+i)² + m·r²/(1+i)³ + …
Factor outm · (a + a² + a³ + …) = m · a / (1 − a)(m / (1+i)) · (1 + a + a² + …) = (m / (1+i)) · 1 / (1 − a)
Simplify (1 − a = (1 + i − r)/(1 + i))m · r / (1 + i − r)m / (1 + i − r)
Subtract ACCLTV = m·r/(1 + i − r) − ACCLTV = m/(1 − r + i) − AC

Why two versions? They differ only by timing: Variant 1 equals r times Variant 2 (before subtracting AC). Variant 2 matches Exhibit 11, because its general sum has r(a−1) and so gives the first period a survival rate of 1. A third slide variant: contract first, churn later

The slides also ask what happens if a contract prevents any churn for T months and then customers churn at rate c (retention r′ = 1 − c) afterwards. Structure: CLTV = Σ (n = 1 to T) of m/(1+i)n + [ m / (1 + i − r′) ] / (1 + i)T − AC. The first piece discounts T certain payments; the second values the post-contract stream as of month T (using Variant 2) and discounts it back to today. Use this only if your pricing plan has a contract-then-no-contract shape. The slides use this to show that a late jump in churn still lowers value.

Jargon

  • MVNO: mobile virtual network operator; sells service on another firm's network.
  • Post-paid / prepaid: billed after use under a contract versus minutes bought in advance.
  • Bucket: a monthly block of minutes at a fixed fee.
  • Overage: the per-minute charge above the bucket.
  • Peak / off-peak: higher versus lower rate time windows.
  • Handset subsidy: carrier sells the phone for less than it costs.
  • Gross add: new subscriber acquired in a period.
  • Disguised numbers: the case says figures are altered for competitive reasons; treat as case facts, not real data.

Before class

Likely discussion angles

  • What evidence supports or undermines a 14-to-24 target, and why did incumbents stay away?
  • Which pricing variable causes which customer pain, and why do carriers benefit from the status quo?
  • How do acquisition cost, margin and churn combine into lifetime value?
  • What are the differences between the CLTV variants, and which assumptions does each make?
  • How should contracts, prepaid, subsidy size, fees and off-peak definitions interact?
  • What might competitors do in response, and does that change the analysis?
  • Which assumptions in your own CLTV are weakest, and how sensitive is the result?
