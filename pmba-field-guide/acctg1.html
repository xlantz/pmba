Step 1 · Start here

A company’s financial reports are its report card

Financial reporting exists so outsiders can decide whether to lend to, or invest in, a company. It answers two plain questions: what does the company have, and who has a claim on it? and is it making money?

The personal net-worth analogy

Imagine tallying up your own finances. On one side you list what you own: savings, a car, a laptop. On the other, who paid for it: a student loan and car loan (other people’s money) and whatever is left over, your own stake. Those two sides always match, because every dollar you own came from somewhere. That is a company’s balance sheet. Then you add up a year of paychecks minus everything you spent, to see if you came out ahead. That is its income statement. A lender thinking about lending you money would want both, and so does a company’s bank or investor.

Six words to know before anything else

Asset
Something the company owns or controls that has value: cash, equipment, money customers owe it.
Liability
Something the company owes to someone who is not an owner: a loan, a bill to a supplier.
Equity
The owners’ stake. Assets minus liabilities.
Revenue
What the company earns by selling its goods or services.
Expense
A cost of earning that revenue: wages, rent, materials.
Net income
Revenue minus expenses. The profit (or loss) for the period.

Tiny example: Sam’s landscaping company

Sam’s company starts with $50,000 from the owner (stock) and a $30,000 bank loan. It buys a truck for $60,000 cash. Day one:

Assets: cash 20,000 + truck 60,000 = 80,000
Liabilities: bank loan = 30,000
Equity: owner's stock = 50,000
80,000 = 30,000 + 50,000 OK

During the year it earns $12,000 of revenue in cash and pays $7,000 of expenses.

Net income = 12,000 - 7,000 = 5,000
Assets end = 85,000 (cash 25,000 + truck 60,000)
Equity end = 50,000 + 5,000 = 55,000
85,000 = 30,000 + 55,000 OK

Now the four ratios are easy: Net profit margin = 5,000 ÷ 12,000 = 41.7%. ROA = 5,000 ÷ average assets 82,500 = 6.1%. Asset turnover = 12,000 ÷ 82,500 = 0.145. Check: 41.7% × 0.145 = 6.1%, the same ROA. That is the DuPont idea in one box.

The one equation behind everything

Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity

A company’s resources (assets) are paid for in only two ways: money from people who are not owners (liabilities) and money from owners (equity). So the two sides always match.

PieceWhat it isWho supplied it
AssetsWhat the company owns or controls: cash, receivables, inventory, equipment—
LiabilitiesWhat it owes: accounts payable, wages payable, bank debtNonowners (lenders, suppliers, employees)
Stockholders’ equityThe owners’ residual claim = Assets − LiabilitiesOwners (stock sold + profits kept)

Equity moves in only a few ways

  • Up: stock issued for cash, and net income (profit kept as retained earnings).
  • Down: dividends paid, a net loss, and stock repurchased.

Retained earnings is the running total of all net income the company has earned, minus all dividends it has paid. It is a cumulative balance, not reset each year, and it is not a pile of cash.

Every transaction keeps it balanced

TransactionAssetsLiabilitiesEquity
Owners buy $100 of stock for cash+1000+100 (no income)
Borrow $100 from a bank+100+1000
Buy $60 of equipment with cash+60 −6000
Earn $40 of service revenue in cash+400+40 (net income)
Pay $15 of wages−150−15 (expense)
Pay a $10 cash dividend−100−10 (not an expense)
Collect $25 owed by a customer+25 −2500 (revenue was recorded earlier)

Solving for the missing piece

Exam angleThe change version, ΔAssets = ΔLiabilities + ΔEquity, is the workhorse. Stock issued for cash raises assets and equity but not net income; a dividend lowers retained earnings but is not an expense; accounts payable is nonowner financing, while common stock and retained earnings are owner financing.

Big idea

Every company's operations break into three activity types — and this isn't just a conceptual list. These three categories are literally the three sections of the Statement of Cash Flows you'll study later, so getting comfortable with them now pays off directly.

ActivityWhat it covers
OperatingHiring & training staff, manufacturing, delivering services, marketing & selling, after-sale support
InvestingAcquiring land, buildings & equipment; growing via new products/services; acquiring other companies
FinancingRaising cash to fund operating and investing activities — selling stock, borrowing cash

Sorting real events

EventActivity
Pay employee wages; collect from customersOperating
Buy a factory; buy another companyInvesting
Sell new shares; borrow from a bank; repay debt; pay dividendsFinancing

The activities add up to the change in cash

Ending cash = Beginning cash + Operating + Investing + Financing

Operating +$130M, investing −$95M, financing −$15M with $45M to start: 45 + 130 − 95 − 15 = $65M. Another: start $40M, operating +75, investing −100, financing +35 gives $50M. Negative investing is normal for a growing company: it is spending cash on future capacity.

The four financial statements

Balance Sheet, Income Statement, Statement of Stockholders' Equity, Statement of Cash Flows. This unit is the roadmap — each statement gets its own deep dive later in the course.

Exam angleIf given a transaction (e.g., "the company sold new shares to fund a factory expansion"), be ready to split it apart: the share sale is Financing, the factory is Investing. Case questions often bundle more than one activity into a single scenario.

Big idea

Four ratios, one relationship. Individually, each ratio tells you something different about a company; together, via the DuPont decomposition above, they explain why a company's returns look the way they do.

RatioEquationWhat it measures
Return on Assets (ROA)Net Income ÷ Average Total AssetsProfit generated per dollar of assets, regardless of financing mix
Net Profit MarginNet Income ÷ Net SalesProfit earned per dollar of sales — cost control & pricing power
Total Asset TurnoverNet Sales ÷ Average Total AssetsSales generated per dollar of assets — an efficiency measure, not profitability
Return on Equity (ROE)Net Income ÷ Average Stockholders' EquityReturn generated specifically for shareholders
ROA = Net Profit Margin × Total Asset Turnover
ROE = ROA × Equity Multiplier, where Equity Multiplier = Average Total Assets ÷ Average Stockholders' Equity

The equity multiplier is a leverage measure: the more a company finances itself with debt rather than equity, the more its ROE is amplified above its ROA.

The one diagram to memorize

Almost every ratio question is really a question about this picture. ROE breaks into ROA and leverage; ROA breaks into margin and turnover.

Use averages, not year-end balances

Net income is earned across the whole year, but a balance sheet is a single day. So the asset or equity in the denominator is the average of the beginning and ending balances: (beginning + ending) ÷ 2. Beginning assets $440M and ending $500M give average assets of $470M. (If a question hands you only one balance, use it — and some professors use ending balances, so follow the question's instructions.)

Big idea

Financial reporting exists for one reason: to provide users with information that supports investment and management decisions. Every rule you'll learn this semester about what gets reported and how ultimately serves that single goal.

Three user groups, three decisions

UserDecision they're making
Investors & equity analystsJudge profitability and financial strength to estimate the value of equity securities
Lenders & credit analystsAssess the company's ability to repay debt and manage credit risk
Company managersInform decisions inside the company

Don't just list the three groups on an exam — pair each with the specific decision it's making. That pairing is what's actually being tested.

The broader 6-category user list

The course overview slides give a fuller enumeration that isn't limited to the three decision-focused groups above: Owners/Potential Investors, Creditors, Management, Government, Analysts, Employees. This doesn't contradict the three-group framework — it's just a more complete list. Know both versions.

Methods of accounting

Three recognized ways to record transactions:

MethodHow it works
Cash basisRecord transactions only when cash actually moves in or out. Common in small businesses.
Accrual basisRecord transactions when the underlying economic event happens, not just when cash moves. Required by GAAP for many companies.
Hybrid basisBlends cash and accrual concepts. Also typical in a small-business setting.

Unit 2 goes deep on cash vs. accrual specifically (including the roll-forward math that depends on it) — this is the roadmap version. Hybrid basis is the one most people forget exists.

Three business forces

External pressures that shape a company's goals and strategy: market conditions, competitive pressures, and regulations.

Exam angleGiven a piece of disclosed information, identify which user group cares most about it and why — that's a very natural short-answer prompt. If asked to name every recognized accounting method (not just cash vs. accrual), remember hybrid basis.

Big idea

If disclosure were free and risk-free, companies would disclose everything. They don't, because disclosure is a genuine cost/benefit tradeoff — and that tension is a favorite discussion-question setup.

BenefitsCosts
Lower cost of capital (lower interest rates or a higher stock price)Preparation & dissemination cost — it isn't free to produce
Improved recruiting effortsCompetitive disadvantage — tips off rivals
Stronger supplier-customer relationshipsLitigation potential
Political costs — visibility invites scrutiny
Exam angle"Why wouldn't a company disclose more than the minimum required?" → answer with the cost side. "Why would a company disclose voluntarily?" → answer with the benefit side, especially cost of capital. Know both directions — instructors like to flip the question.

Big idea

Financial statements are useful, but they aren't a perfect mirror of a company's economic reality. Four limitations are worth knowing cold — they're the natural answer to any "critique the financial statements" prompt.

  1. Historical cost, not market value. Statements generally reflect what was paid for an asset, not its current market value. Example: Apple's reported equity on its 10-K isn't close to the actual market value of its equity.
  2. Estimates can mislead. Various accounts rely on estimates to better reflect financial status — but those estimates can end up misleading users.
  3. The reporting framework keeps evolving. Standards for public-company reporting change over time; analysts and investors often modify standard reporting to get what they believe is more thorough information.
  4. Errors and fraud can materially impact reports.
Exam angleHistorical cost is the single most commonly tested limitation — if a question asks "why might the Balance Sheet understate a company's true value," this is almost always the expected answer.

Big idea

Ratios tell you what the numbers are. Two qualitative frameworks help you explain why. They are used alongside the ratios, not as a separate topic.

  • Porter's Five Forces — threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitutes, rivalry among existing competitors.
  • SWOT Analysis — Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats.
Exam angleFor a full “analyze this company” prompt, structure the answer as: (1) who is the user and what decision are they making, (2) what do the ratios show (compute and decompose them), (3) what strategic context (Five Forces / SWOT) explains the numbers.

Big idea

The ratios covered above (ROA, Margin, Turnover, ROE) are only one of three broader analysis techniques the course uses. The full toolkit:

TechniqueWhat it does
Horizontal AnalysisComparing an account's changes across multiple periods (e.g., how did revenue change year-over-year?)
Vertical AnalysisComputing an account as a percentage of a core figure (e.g., depreciation expense as % of total operating expenses — the same underlying idea as a common-sized Balance Sheet)
Financial RatiosGrouped into four categories: Liquidity, Profitability, Debt/Equity, Productivity
Only Profitability ratios (ROA, Net Profit Margin, Total Asset Turnover, ROE) are built out in depth in this unit so far. Liquidity, Debt/Equity, and Productivity ratios haven't been covered yet — watch for them in a later unit and this page will get updated when they are.
Exam angleIf a question asks you to "analyze" a company's financial statements, the safe structure is: (1) run horizontal analysis (trend across periods), (2) run vertical analysis (common-size the statements), (3) compute ratios across all four categories — not just profitability, even though profitability is what this unit has covered in depth so far.

Homework-level material

You can skip this and still ace the testThe homework is much harder than the exam: it uses real company filings, multi-year data and “explain why” questions. If the basics above felt easy and you want to see the same ideas stretched, open this. When you’re ready for graded practice: Unit 1 homework practice.

Same ROA, very different ROE: leverage

Two firms each earn net income of $8M on $100M of assets (ROA 8%). Firm U is funded entirely by equity ($100M). Firm L is funded with $60M of debt and $40M of equity.

Firm U: equity multiplier = 100 / 100 = 1.0 → ROE = 8% × 1.0 = 8%
Firm L: equity multiplier = 100 / 40 = 2.5 → ROE = 8% × 2.5 = 20%

Same profit and same assets, but L’s owners put in less of their own money, so each owner dollar earns more. Leverage amplifies in both directions: in a loss year L’s equity shrinks faster, and the lenders still must be paid. Higher ROE from debt is not free.

A multi-year ratio table

Year-end total assets are $400M, $460M and $520M for years 1–3. Net income is $27.6M in year 2 and $31.2M in year 3.

Year 2: avg assets = (400+460)/2 = 430 → ROA = 27.6/430 = 6.4%
Year 3: avg assets = (460+520)/2 = 490 → ROA = 31.2/490 = 6.4%

Net income grew 13% (31.2 ÷ 27.6 = 1.13) yet ROA is flat, because the asset base grew just as fast. This is why analysts look at returns, not profit alone. Note the first year in any data set has no beginning balance, so it cannot use an average.

Explaining a change with DuPont

Year 1: margin 5%, turnover 2.0 → ROA 10%. Year 2: margin 4%, turnover 2.6 → ROA 10.4%. Margin fell but ROA rose, because the company squeezed more sales out of every asset dollar (maybe it cut prices to move volume). When a homework asks “why did ROA change?”, state which factor moved and in which direction.

Pulling the numbers from a real filing

  • Net income is the bottom line of the income statement. Net sales may be labeled “Net sales,” “Net revenues” or “Total revenues.” Use the top line.
  • A 10-K balance sheet shows two year-end columns, so you can average them for one year’s ratio. Check you are reading total assets and total stockholders’ equity, not a sub-total.
  • Real equity can include noncontrolling interests or treasury stock; use the total line the question names. Different units ($ millions vs $ thousands) cancel in a ratio but must match inside it.
  • Comparing two companies (for example two beverage makers): compute all four ratios for each, then use DuPont to say whether the winner got there by margin, turnover or leverage.

Every Unit 1 formula and definition on one page

Accounting equation

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Equity = Assets − Liabilities
ΔA = ΔL + ΔE

Liabilities = nonowner financing. Equity = owner financing.

What moves equity

Up: stock issued, net income
Down: dividends, net loss, buybacks

Stock for cash: assets ↑, equity ↑, no net income. Dividends are not an expense.

Three activities

Operating: run the business
Investing: buy long-term assets
Financing: stock, loans, dividends

Ending cash = Beg + Operating + Investing + Financing.

The four ratios

ROA = NI ÷ Avg assets
Margin = NI ÷ Net sales
Turnover = Sales ÷ Avg assets
ROE = NI ÷ Avg equity

Average = (beginning + ending) ÷ 2.

DuPont

ROA = Margin × Turnover
ROE = ROA × Equity multiplier
Equity multiplier = Avg assets ÷ Avg equity

Multiply both factors; never judge ROA by one alone. More debt means a bigger multiplier and ROE above ROA.

Users → decisions

Investors: value equity
Lenders: can it repay debt?
Managers: internal decisions

Six-user list: owners/investors, creditors, management, government, analysts, employees.

Accounting methods

Cash: when cash moves
Accrual: when earned/incurred
Hybrid: a blend

Three methods, not two. GAAP requires accrual for many companies.

Disclosure trade-off

Benefits: lower cost of capital, recruiting, supplier/customer ties
Costs: preparation, rivals, lawsuits, political

Answer the side the question asks, then tie it to the scenario.

Limits of statements

1. Historical cost
2. Estimates
3. Evolving standards
4. Errors and fraud

Historical cost is the usual answer to “why understated?”

Traps the exam sets

  • Using year-end balances when the ratio calls for average balances.
  • Deciding ROA from margin alone (or turnover alone). Compute the product.
  • Calling accounts payable owner financing. It is a liability, so nonowner.
  • Treating a dividend as an expense, or a stock issue as revenue. Neither touches net income.
  • Mixing up turnover (sales ÷ assets) with margin (income ÷ sales).
  • Listing only cash and accrual accounting: hybrid is the third method.
  • Lumping a scenario into one activity: selling shares is Financing, building the factory with that cash is Investing.
  • Naming only three user groups when asked for the full six, or listing users without the decision each makes.

Worked examples

Fixed exercises, not multiple choice. Try each one on paper first, then click to reveal the step-by-step solution.

01Basic ROA calculationReveal solution

Meridian Outfitters reports Net Income of $3,600,000 and Average Total Assets of $45,000,000 for the year. Calculate its ROA.

Solution
  1. Recall the formula: ROA = Net Income ÷ Average Total Assets.
  2. Plug in the numbers: $3,600,000 ÷ $45,000,000.
  3. ROA = 0.08, or 8%.
  4. Interpretation: Meridian generates 8 cents of profit for every dollar of assets it holds, regardless of how those assets are financed.
02Margin, turnover, and the ROA linkReveal solution

Using the same Meridian Outfitters, Net Sales for the year were $60,000,000. Calculate (a) Net Profit Margin, (b) Total Asset Turnover, and (c) verify that Margin × Turnover reproduces the ROA from Problem 1.

Solution
  1. (a) Net Profit Margin = Net Income ÷ Net Sales = $3,600,000 ÷ $60,000,000 = 6%.
  2. (b) Total Asset Turnover = Net Sales ÷ Average Total Assets = $60,000,000 ÷ $45,000,000 = 1.33x.
  3. (c) ROA = Margin × Turnover = 6% × 1.33 = 8.0% — matches Problem 1. ✅
  4. Interpretation: Meridian's 8% ROA comes from a moderate margin combined with turning its asset base over about 1.3 times a year — not an extreme luxury or discount profile.
03ROE and the equity multiplierReveal solution

Meridian's Average Stockholders' Equity for the year was $18,000,000 (recall: Average Total Assets = $45,000,000, Net Income = $3,600,000). Calculate (a) ROE directly, (b) the Equity Multiplier, and (c) verify ROE = ROA × Equity Multiplier.

Solution
  1. (a) ROE = Net Income ÷ Average Stockholders' Equity = $3,600,000 ÷ $18,000,000 = 20%.
  2. (b) Equity Multiplier = Average Total Assets ÷ Average Stockholders' Equity = $45,000,000 ÷ $18,000,000 = 2.5.
  3. (c) ROA × Equity Multiplier = 8% × 2.5 = 20% — matches (a). ✅
  4. Interpretation: Meridian's assets are financed 2.5x over by equity-and-debt combined relative to equity alone — meaning debt makes up a meaningful share of financing, which is why ROE (20%) is well above ROA (8%).
04Same ROA, different business modelsReveal solution

Boutique Co. has a Net Profit Margin of 25% and a Total Asset Turnover of 0.4x. Volume Co. has a Net Profit Margin of 2.5% and a Total Asset Turnover of 4x. (a) Calculate each company's ROA. (b) Which business model does each profile suggest?

Solution
  1. Boutique Co.: ROA = 25% × 0.4 = 10%.
  2. Volume Co.: ROA = 2.5% × 4 = 10%.
  3. Same ROA (10%), completely different paths.
  4. Boutique Co.'s high margin / low turnover suggests a luxury or specialty retailer — high price, low volume. Volume Co.'s low margin / high turnover suggests a discount or high-volume retailer — thin margins made up for by sales velocity.
05Working backward from ROEReveal solution

A company has ROE of 24% and an Equity Multiplier of 3. Its Total Asset Turnover is 2x. Find (a) its ROA and (b) its Net Profit Margin.

Solution
  1. (a) Since ROE = ROA × Equity Multiplier: ROA = ROE ÷ Equity Multiplier = 24% ÷ 3 = 8%.
  2. (b) Since ROA = Margin × Turnover: Margin = ROA ÷ Turnover = 8% ÷ 2 = 4%.
  3. Working backward through the DuPont tree like this is a common exam format — you're rarely given all four ratios directly.
06Disclosure cost/benefit reasoningReveal sample answer

A mid-size manufacturer is deciding whether to voluntarily disclose detailed unit-cost data by product line, beyond what regulators require. Write a short argument for and against disclosing.

Sample answer outline
  1. For disclosure: More transparency can lower the company's cost of capital — lenders and investors face less uncertainty and may demand a smaller risk premium, translating to lower interest rates or a higher stock price. Detailed data can also strengthen relationships with suppliers and customers who use it to plan.
  2. Against disclosure: Unit-cost data is exactly the kind of information a competitor could use to undercut pricing — a direct competitive disadvantage. There's also a real preparation/dissemination cost to producing granular data, and any errors or unflattering figures create litigation and political-cost exposure.
  3. Structure to use on an exam: name the specific benefit and specific cost that apply to the scenario given — don't just recite the generic lists. The best answers connect a named cost or benefit to a detail in the prompt (e.g., "since this is a highly competitive commodity market, the competitive-disadvantage cost is especially large here").
07Identify the user, the activity, and the ratioReveal solution

A regional bank is reviewing a company's most recent financial statements before deciding whether to renew its $10M line of credit. The company recently sold new equity shares to fund a new distribution warehouse. (a) Which user group is the bank, and what decision is it making? (b) Which business activities are described in the second sentence? (c) Which ratio would the bank most likely scrutinize first, and why?

Solution
  1. (a) The bank is a lender/credit analyst, assessing the company's ability to repay debt (credit risk) before renewing the credit line.
  2. (b) Selling new equity shares is a Financing activity; building a new distribution warehouse is an Investing activity.
  3. (c) A lender is most focused on the company's ability to generate cash and repay obligations, so it would likely scrutinize ROA first (overall efficiency at generating profit from assets, independent of how those assets are financed) — and also watch the Equity Multiplier closely, since rising leverage directly affects the company's capacity to take on more debt safely.
Before the exam

Likely exam & discussion angles

  • Given a piece of disclosed information, identify which user group cares most and what decision it informs.
  • Given a transaction description, split it into Operating / Investing / Financing components.
  • Explain why a company would, or wouldn't, voluntarily disclose more than required — be ready to argue both directions.
  • Compute ROA, Net Profit Margin, Total Asset Turnover, and ROE from given figures, and show the DuPont decomposition connecting them.
  • Given two companies with the same ROA but different margin/turnover, explain the business-model difference this implies.
  • Explain how financial leverage (the equity multiplier) affects the gap between ROA and ROE.
  • Use Porter's Five Forces or SWOT to explain why a company's ratios look the way they do — not as a standalone topic.
  • Critique the reliability of a company's financial statements using the four limitations (historical cost, estimates, evolving standards, fraud risk).
  • Given a scenario, decide whether Horizontal Analysis or Vertical Analysis is the better technique to answer the question being asked.
  • Name all three accounting methods (not just cash vs. accrual) if asked to list every recognized method.
  • Use Assets = Liabilities + Equity to solve for a missing piece (equity from assets and liabilities, or the percentage of assets financed by nonowners), and classify a financing source as owner or nonowner.
  • Pick the transaction that raises assets and equity without touching net income (stock issued for cash), or the one that cuts retained earnings without cutting net income (a dividend).
  • Roll cash forward: beginning cash + operating + investing + financing = ending cash.
  • Use average balances for ROA and ROE, and know that a higher margin alone does not guarantee a higher ROA.
