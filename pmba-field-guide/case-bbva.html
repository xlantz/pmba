BBVA Compass: Marketing Resource Allocation

A regional bank with a shrinking budget has to decide how to spread advertising money across TV, branches, sponsorships, paid search and display ads, when only a small share of new checking accounts come online and the online funnel leaks at every step. Discussed 11/10 with Chapter 13.

Case brief only: the analysis and recommendation are yours (course policy: no AI for case analyses).

BBVA Compass, section by section

Who, what, when

In December 2010, Frank Sottosanti (chief marketing officer, BBVA Compass) met with brand and advertising managers and with people from the bank's media agency, Media Contacts (the interactive arm of Havas), to review performance and plan how to split next year's marketing money across offline and online channels. The case is an HBS write-up by Sunil Gupta and Joseph Davies-Gavin (rev. April 2012); data are disguised.

The decision facing the protagonist

Sottosanti says next year's budget will likely be the same as 2010 or smaller, even with the new NBA and bowl-game sponsorships in place, and asks how to spread that limited budget most effectively. The case does not offer a menu of options; the student has to build the allocation logic from the exhibits. This brief lays out the facts and tools, not an answer.

One-paragraph summary

BBVA Compass is a mid-sized, Sunbelt-focused U.S. bank owned by Spain's BBVA, competing against far bigger national banks. Most new checking accounts (about 80%) still come through branches and only about 5% come online, yet the bank gave online advertising 21% of the 2010 advertising budget because online is measurable and seen as a growth channel. Inside online, money is split roughly evenly between paid search and display. Search produces most of the clicks and a lower cost per completed application; display produces huge impression counts and a messier, multi-exposure path to conversion, which raises the attribution problem. Online customers differ from branch customers (lower retention, lower balances, higher fees), and a multi-step funnel (click, start, complete, approval, funding) sits between an ad click and a funded account.

Logistics

Discussion in the in-person session on 11/10 (Week 7). Reading: this case plus Chapter 13, Designing an Integrated Marketing Campaign in the Digital Age. Quiz on Chapter 13 is Monday evening before the 11/12 remote session.

What the professor will probeWho is in the room and what is being decided; that the budget is flat or shrinking; that online share of spend (21%) and online share of new checking accounts (about 5%) are the case's central tension.

BBVA Compass

BBVA (Spain) had 48 million customers and about 104,000 employees in over 30 countries and earned 49% of its income in the Americas. It entered the U.S. in 2004 (Valley Bank, focused on Mexican immigrants) and grew through Compass Bank (2007) and the FDIC-assisted acquisition of Guaranty Bank (2009). In the U.S. it was the 15th-largest commercial bank by deposits, with over 700 branches (Exhibit 1 total: 767) and $49 billion in deposits across seven states. Bank of America, by comparison, had over 5,900 branches. The stated goal: become a top-10 U.S. bank inside the current Sunbelt footprint, positioned between giant banks and local credit unions.

Three business lines: retail (consumers and small/mid businesses under $5 million revenue), corporate and commercial (over $5 million), and wealth management (over $1 million investable assets).

Target customer

"Strivers": aggressive and anxious about money, wanting a partner who thinks about them as individuals. Ideal targets are age 25 to 54, household income above $75,000, with needs reaching beyond checking (loans, investments). Checking itself is a mass-market product.

Marketing goals (Sottosanti)

  • Build awareness and trust in the brand (especially after the financial crisis).
  • Support the lines of business (checking, savings, mortgage, commercial banking).
  • Bring in new customers and grow total accounts.
  • Improve satisfaction and retention of current customers and cross-sell to them.

Growth by acquisition complicates all of this: merging cultures, aligning customer databases, and supporting new regions.

How the budget gets set

"A combination of art and science": overall sales goals and corporate objectives, growth potential of channels and markets, and past performance. Example from the case: only 5% of sales come from the Internet, but the online channel gets more than 5% of the budget because it is viewed as a growth opportunity. Spending is heavier in big branch markets. Armstrong's trade-off: online is trackable so ROI can be estimated, while offline is hard to measure, so offline "can lose budget even though [it is] critical."

Formulas you will need (inputs above; you do the math)

  • Cost per application (CPA) = media spend / completed applications (as the case defines it).
  • Conversion through the funnel = (share who start) x (share who complete) x (share approved) x (share who fund). Multiply the rates, do not add them.
  • Cost per funded account = media cost per application / (approval rate x funding rate), before adding other costs. Whether the ~$100 promotion cost is already inside the media figure is a judgment you must make and state (the case describes it as a separate effective cost per new online customer).
  • Effective acquisition cost = all costs you attribute to getting one funded customer (media, offer, and any other cost you can justify).
  • Simple CLTV = sum over years of (annual margin x probability still a customer in that year) discounted at the chosen rate. With constant retention r and no discounting, expected life is 1 / (1 - r) years if uncapped, or a five-year capped sum as the case uses for $800.

1. Online vs offline media: roles

Offline (TV, radio, outdoor, print, branches, direct mail)Online (search, display, social, email)
Typical roleMass reach, awareness, trust, emotional brand building; supports local branch trafficTargeted reach, response, lead capture, direct application or purchase
MeasurementIndirect and slow (brand tracking, market tests); hard to link to a saleClick, view and conversion data per ad; quicker feedback and testing
Cost logicCostly to produce and buy; paid for reach (CPM-style)Paid for clicks, impressions or conversions; easy to scale up or down
Common riskHard to prove ROI, so it can lose budget to what is measurableMeasurable is not the same as most important; attribution and duplication problems; limited by who is searching

Generic idea to keep in mind: measurable channels can look better than they are (they get credit for demand that other media created) and unmeasurable channels can look worse than they are. Ask what each channel is actually for before comparing them on one metric.

2. The online acquisition funnel

A funnel multiplies conversion rates across steps. Generic example with invented rates:

Step (invented)Rate at stepPeople left (start at 10,000 visitors)
Visit-10,000
Start a form12%1,200
Finish the form40%480
Approved75%360
Activated70%252

Overall 252 / 10,000 = 2.5%. Improving a step with a big drop (the biggest leak) usually gives the most for the least effort, and a leak early in the funnel (start a form) can mean the wrong people arrive, while a leak late (activation) can mean friction or unmet conditions. Levers: simplify the form, save progress, reduce required information, follow up with those who abandon (retargeting, email), clarify offer terms, speed up approval, and nudge funding. Which levers fit this bank is for you to decide.

3. Search vs display

Search captures demand that already exists (a person typing "free checking account" is looking now); expect lower reach but higher intent. Display creates or nudges demand (a person is reading a news page and sees the bank); expect lots of impressions, few clicks, and effects that show up later, often as a search or a direct visit. Measurement for display therefore needs more than last-click: look at assisted conversions, view-through, and tests comparing exposed vs not-exposed groups. Terms: CTR = clicks / impressions; CPC = cost / clicks; CPM = cost per thousand impressions; CPA = cost per acquisition (here, per completed application); share of voice = share of available impressions your ad received; reach = unique people exposed; frequency = average exposures per person.

4. The attribution problem

Last-click gives 100% of the credit to the final click before conversion. It is simple but ignores earlier ads. Multi-touch attribution spreads credit across touches (equal split, time decay, position-based, or data-driven weights). A related test: exposed vs not exposed groups. Caution when reading such tests: people who see many display ads may differ from those who do not (they may browse more, or be more interested in banking), so a higher conversion rate among the exposed does not by itself prove the ads caused it. Overlapping networks add a second problem: the same person is counted under several networks. Also note cookies track browsers, not people.

5. Sponsorship objectives

Sponsorship means paying for association with an event, league, team or cause. Typical objectives in marketing communications textbooks: raise awareness and visibility; build or shift brand image by borrowing the values of the property; deepen relationships (hospitality, community goodwill, employee pride); drive sales or leads through activation (offers, in-venue promotion); and show commitment to a community. Evaluating a sponsorship means linking the objective to a measure: awareness and consideration through brand tracking, image associations through surveys, activation response through promo codes. Check your Ch 12/13 notes for the textbook list your professor uses.

6. Marketing resource allocation and marginal analysis (the Unit 2 MR = MC idea)

In Unit 2, the slide idea is the economics rule that you keep expanding an activity while the marginal revenue (MR) of one more unit exceeds its marginal cost (MC), and stop where they are equal. For a fixed budget the same logic becomes: move dollars from the channel with the lower marginal return per dollar to the one with the higher, until the marginal returns per dollar are about equal across channels. (Check your Unit 2 slide for the exact wording.) The key is marginal, not average: a channel with a great average cost per customer may have little room left to buy more at the same price, and a channel with a poor average may still have a cheap next customer. Real constraints: the number of searches is limited by how many people search, measurement is imperfect, some spending (brand) works over a longer time, and spending in one channel changes results in others.

Generic example (invented): channel P returns 3 new customers per extra $1,000 at current spend; channel Q returns 1.5. Under the equal-marginal-return rule you would move money from Q to P, and expect P's return per $1,000 to fall and Q's to rise until they meet. This only works if you can estimate marginal (not just average) returns.

7. CLTV and acquisition cost

CLTV (customer lifetime value) = the present value of the margin a customer is expected to bring over their relationship. It combines retention, margin per period, and discounting. Acquisition cost (CAC) = what it costs to win one customer. The decision rule is to compare them: CLTV / CAC, or CLTV - CAC, ideally by channel, since channels attract different customers.

Invented exampleChannel AlphaChannel Beta
Acquisition cost per customer$60$110
CLTV per customer$450$700
CLTV - cost$390$590

Notice that "cheapest to acquire" (Alpha) and "highest net value" (Beta) can differ, and a ratio can rank channels differently than a dollar difference. Which metric to use depends on whether the budget or the number of customers is the binding constraint. Generic retention example (invented): with annual retention 60%, the expected share still active is 100%, 60%, 36%, 21.6%, 13% in years 1 to 5. Lower retention shrinks later years fast.

8. Chapter 13 ideas: integrated digital campaigns

Ch 13 themes to bring into the discussion (verify the specifics against your reading): an integrated campaign coordinates many channels around one message and customer journey; set clear objectives first (awareness, consideration, conversion, retention) and match media to the stage; use a mix of paid, owned and earned media; consistency across touchpoints (the "banking built for you" theme) reinforces recall; online and offline media work together (TV or sponsorship can lift search volume; search catches the demand it creates); measure with analytics and test, but beware over-trusting what is easiest to count; and plan for the full customer journey, not just the first click.

Q2. Is the 21% online / rest offline split appropriate?

In 2010, the bank allocated $1.22 million or 21% of the total ad budget to online advertising for acquiring checking account customers (see Tables B and C), while allocating the rest of the budget to offline marketing efforts. However, only 5% of new checking account customers came from the online channel, while branches accounted for 80% of new checking accounts (page 3). Is the advertising budget allocation between offline and online media appropriate? Why or why not?

Where to look: Tables B and C, p. 3 (5%/80% quote and the "more than 5% because it is a growth opportunity" logic), p. 4 (Armstrong), the customer-value facts table above.

  • Separate share of budget from share of customers, and say what a mismatch does and does not imply (average vs marginal).
  • Note what the 21% does not include: Table B is the advertising budget, not the full marketing budget.
  • Consider what each dollar buys at the margin, not just on average (see marginal analysis).
  • Consider what is and is not in the case about offline acquisition cost and offline contributions to online results.
  • Consider customer quality (retention, fees) and the growth-channel argument.
  • State your assumptions and what additional data would change your mind.

Q3. Sponsorships

Why did BBVA Compass sign multiyear sponsorship deals with the NBA and ESPN? Do you agree with this decision?

Where to look: p. 5 (Sponsorships), Exhibit 3a (awareness fell with the new name; goals for 2010-2012), Exhibit 3b (online awareness vs competitors), p. 3 (marketing goals), p. 7 (next year's budget).

  • Link each deal to a stated objective (awareness of the new name, reach in non-NBA markets, passion/values association).
  • Who signed what: the NBA deal was by BBVA Group; the bowl deal by BBVA Compass.
  • Which markets are covered and which are not (see Exhibit 1).
  • What the case says about cost and measured payoff (nothing), and how you would judge success.
  • Whether the case's own goals (acquisition, awareness, trust) are served and what trade-offs a flat budget imposes.

Q4. Steps in the online acquisition process

What are the steps a customer goes through in the online acquisition process and how can the bank improve this process?

Where to look: pp. 5-6 (Sottosanti's step description), Exhibits 5 and 7 (started vs completed), Exhibit 4b (offers and ads).

  • List every step and its stated rate; do not skip approval and funding.
  • Identify where the biggest drop-offs are and why people may leave there (the case offers some reasons).
  • Distinguish ideas that fix the step from ideas that change who enters the funnel.
  • Tie each improvement to evidence or to a measure you would track.
  • Note differences by engine or network in started-to-completed rates (Exhibits 5 and 7).

Q5. Effective acquisition cost and lifetime value, online vs offline

What is the effective acquisition cost and lifetime value of customers acquired through the online channel compared to the offline channel? Recall that we want to base our decisions on CLTV. Which channel produces a better customer?

Where to look: p. 3 ($800 value, $200 / $100-150 / $100 thresholds, retention 55% vs 65%, net income, regulation), p. 5 (promotion cost of $100), p. 6 (approval and funding rates), Table C (CPA).

  • Start from the formulas in the numbers section; list every input you need and where it comes from.
  • Decide how to adjust cost per completed application for approval and funding, and whether the promotion cost is extra.
  • Decide how to handle retention differences and whether the $800 average applies to each channel; state assumptions.
  • Note what the case does not give (offline acquisition cost, fee-income effects) and say how you treat it.
  • Compare to the case's thresholds, and test how sensitive the answer is to the fee regulation and your assumptions.

Q6. Attribution, search and display, and budget between them

A hot topic in digital advertising is the "attribution problem." Within the category of online advertising, what are the roles of search and display in acquiring new customers? Is the budget allocation between search and display appropriate?

Where to look: pp. 6-7 (search, display, Bernstein's journey example), Table C, Exhibits 5 and 6, Exhibit 10, Exhibits 8 and 9 (reach and overlap).

  • Explain the problem: last-click credit and what it hides.
  • Roles: search captures intent; what display does according to Exhibit 10 (and its limits as evidence).
  • What Exhibit 10 compares, why the groups may not be equal, and why network rows overlap.
  • Compare cost per application with what is not captured by it.
  • Tie any allocation view to marginal returns and the measurement caveats.

Q7. Which search engines and display networks perform best?

Which search engines and display ad networks are performing best?

Where to look: Exhibit 5 (engines), Exhibit 7 (networks), Exhibit 10 (assist effect), Exhibits 8 and 9 (reach and overlap), Exhibit 6 (keyword types).

  • Define "best" before ranking: cost per application, volume, completion rate, assisted value, reach.
  • Watch sample size (small networks and SuperPages).
  • Compare Exhibit 7 rankings with Exhibit 10 rankings; they measure different things.
  • Note data caveats flagged in the numbers section.
