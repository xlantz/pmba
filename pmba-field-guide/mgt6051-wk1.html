Week 1: Values

Values are the ends people pursue. Christensen asks how to measure a life by them, Ingram and Choi give a five-step process to align an organization's values with its strategy and its people, Kouzes and Posner start leadership with clarifying values, and the slides explain why managers misjudge what motivates people.

The setup: teach how to think, then ask three questions

Christensen opens with a story. When Intel's Andy Grove wanted to hear only what disruption meant for Intel, Christensen insisted on first explaining the model using the steel minimill story (Nucor starting at the low end with rebar). Grove then reached the Celeron decision himself. The lesson Christensen draws: teach people how to think and let them answer their own question, instead of handing them an answer. On the last day of his class he turns the course's theories on the students' own lives with three questions.

Question 1: How can I be sure I'll be happy in my career?

Theory: Herzberg on motivation. The powerful motivator is not money; it is the chance to learn, take on more responsibility, contribute to others, and be recognized for achievement. Christensen pictures a manager driving home either feeling unappreciated and underused, or feeling she learned, was recognized and mattered; the difference spills into how she treats her spouse and children. His conclusion: management is the most noble of professions if practiced well, because no other job offers as many ways to help others grow, take responsibility, be recognized and contribute to a team. He criticizes students who think business is only buying, selling and investing: doing deals does not give the deep rewards of building up people.

IdeaWhere it comes from
Motivators: learning, growing in responsibility, contributing, being recognizedThe article (Herzberg's assertion).
Hygiene factors (pay, conditions) prevent unhappiness but do not motivateGeneral background, not from the provided reading: Herzberg's two-factor theory. The article only says money is not the powerful motivator.

Example (invented): A hospital billing supervisor stops only tracking "claims closed per day." She starts explaining to each analyst how their work keeps patients out of collections, hands one analyst ownership of a denial-reduction project, and publicly credits it when denials drop. That is learning, responsibility, contribution and recognition: motivators in Herzberg's sense, with no raise attached.

Question 2: How do I make my family an enduring source of happiness?

Part A: Have a purpose, then allocate resources to it. A company's strategy is determined by what its resource-allocation process actually funds, which can differ sharply from what management intended, because decision systems steer money toward initiatives with the most tangible, immediate returns. Christensen applies this to life: your real strategy is how you spend your limited time, energy and talent across competing "businesses" (spouse, children, community, career, church). He has seen 1979 HBS classmates return to reunions unhappy, divorced and estranged from their children. None planned that outcome; they did not keep their life's purpose in front of them while deciding where to spend their time. High-achievement people unconsciously put spare effort where results are concrete (ship a product, close a sale, get promoted), while family investment pays off slowly ("kids misbehave every day"; maybe 20 years until you can say you raised a good child), so they underinvest in family and overinvest in career.

Purpose. Christensen spent an hour each night at Oxford thinking about the purpose of his life and judged it the most useful thing he learned: he uses econometrics a few times a year but uses his life purpose every day. Clarity of purpose "trumps" knowing frameworks such as activity-based costing, balanced scorecards, core competence, disruptive innovation, the four Ps and the five forces. His purpose came from his religious faith, but he notes faith is not the only source; a former student's purpose centered on bringing honesty and prosperity to his country and raising children committed to the same cause. A profession is one tool for pursuing purpose, not the purpose itself.

Deliberate vs emergent strategy (general background, not in this article). The article's version: if you do not allocate deliberately, the strategy that emerges from your daily choices can differ from the one you intended; but some unplanned opportunities that emerge can be good. The labels "deliberate" and "emergent" strategy are general strategy vocabulary.

Part B: Create a culture (Tools of Cooperation). Seeing the future is one thing; getting people to cooperate toward it is another. The model arrays cooperation tools on two dimensions: how much people agree on what they want, and how much they agree on what actions will produce it. With little agreement on both, you need power tools (coercion, threats, punishment). If the same way of working succeeds again and again, consensus forms; Edgar Schein describes this as how a culture is built: people follow priorities and procedures by instinct and assumption, not explicit decision, and culture sets what counts as an acceptable way to solve recurring problems and which problems get priority.

Applied to family: power tools (the simplest parenting tools) stop working in the teen years, so parents wish they had started early to build a family culture in which children instinctively act respectfully and choose right. Families have cultures just as companies do, built consciously or by accident. Self-esteem and confidence must be designed in early: children, like employees, build self-esteem by doing hard things and learning what works.

Example (invented): A startup's founders at first have to say exactly what to do and how (power-tool corner). Each time the "ship weekly, demo to customers every Friday" routine works, the team stops debating it. Two years later new hires copy it without being told: that routine has become culture.

Question 3: How can I be sure I'll stay out of jail? (the marginal cost trap)

Finance teaches us to ignore sunk and fixed costs and decide on marginal costs and revenues. That doctrine pushes companies to leverage what they already built rather than build the capabilities they will need, which is wrong when the future differs from the past (it almost always does). In personal life the same logic whispers, "just this once, in this extenuating circumstance, it's OK." The marginal cost of doing wrong once always looks low, and you never see the full cost of the path you are on. Christensen cites classmates: two of the 32 people in his Rhodes scholar class spent time in jail, and Jeff Skilling of Enron was an HBS classmate. These were good people who went off course.

His own case: he had committed at age 16 never to play ball on Sunday. At Oxford his undefeated team reached the final four and the championship game fell on a Sunday; teammates and coach urged "just this one time." He did not play. His lesson: life is an unending stream of extenuating circumstances, so crossing once would mean crossing repeatedly; it is easier to hold your principles 100% of the time than 98% of the time. Decide what you stand for and draw the line in a safe place.

Example (invented): A controller rounds an accrual up "just this quarter" to help a team hit a bonus threshold. Each later quarter has its own extenuating circumstance, and the line has already moved.

Humility and the yardstick

Humility. Students who described the most humble people they knew noticed high self-esteem: humility is not self-deprecation but the esteem you have for others. Good behavior flows from it (you would not steal from or lie to someone you respect), and a humble eagerness to learn from everybody makes learning opportunities unlimited. Arrogant or demeaning behavior is usually a symptom of low self-esteem.

The yardstick. After a cancer diagnosis (he was later spared), Christensen concluded the revenue his ideas generated mattered little to him; the measure of a life is the individual people whose lives you have touched and helped become better people, not prominence. His final recommendation: decide the metric by which your life will be judged and live each day to meet it.

What the quiz may probeMatch question to theory (career = Herzberg, family = resource allocation + culture, integrity = marginal cost). Identify a scenario as the "just this once" trap or as short-term-return bias in resource allocation. Know the Skilling and Rhodes-class detail, the 100% vs 98% line, humility defined as esteem for others, and "people helped" as the yardstick.

Source: Paul Ingram and Yoonjin Choi, "What Does Your Company Really Stand For?" (HBR Spotlight, Nov-Dec 2022). Paraphrased.

Why it matters

The authors call it values alignment when an organization's values fit both its strategy and its employees' own values. Benefits they report: higher job satisfaction, lower turnover, better teamwork, more effective communication, bigger contributions, more productive negotiations and, perhaps surprisingly, more diversity, equity and inclusion. Their favorite finding: in the divisions of one multiunit organization, COOs with low values alignment needed a 40% salary increase to be as likely to stay as COOs with high alignment. Examples of firms that struggled when employees felt their values trumped the organization's: Coinbase, Netflix, Twitter and Disney.

Example (from the article): two people both value creativity; one thinks it comes from quiet contemplation, the other from interaction with others. Same value, different belief about the path.

Two mistakes (traps)

TrapThe mistaken assumptionWhy it fails
Values as magicIf leaders keep reciting official values, employees will fall under their spell. (A global investment-bank leader told new hires that inside the walls "these are your values.")People resent values imposed unilaterally; they lose agency, become cynical and then behave less ethically.
Values as smoke screenTalk of "cultural fit" just hides biased hiring, so ban the idea.Culture is made of values, beliefs and norms, which matter for performance. The real problem is "fit" defined by irrelevant traits such as elite hobbies (sailing, squash). Meta banned the term, but Google still hunts for "Googliness," which is alignment around core values.

Values and diversity. The authors say valuing diversity of values means diversity of ends, which pushes people to work at cross-purposes. Kris Beevers (CEO of NS1) compares an organization to a vector field: vectors pointing in different directions cancel each other out. To get the benefits of diversity you need alignment.

The five-step values-alignment process (take the steps in order)

StepWhat you doDetails and case examples
1. Identify individual values of all membersWalk as many people as possible through building a values structure: their top values plus how they relate.Article: the structure holds the eight most significant values and the interdependencies a person sees (e.g., pursuing excellence serves achievement); takes 90 minutes or more. Shortcut: ask "Whom do you most value among your colleagues, and why?" (the why reveals personal values; used at an e-commerce company to predict performance). Benton Museum of Art at Pomona College (new building more than tripled its space, 2021): board, staff, leaders and interns found shared values: creativity, integrity, excellence, joy. Note: the class slide says circle 3 to 5 values; the article says eight.
2. Identify key priorities from strategyTop-down: ask "What is the most important thing the organization can do to achieve its strategy?"Do this second, because values-as-magic leaders do only this step. You cannot steal another firm's values: a Goldman Sachs veteran asked to list values for his new bank declined; Goldman's values worked because they fit its strategy and were held by its people (the "heart transplant without tissue compatibility" image).
3. Look for values "candidates"A task force (various ranks plus senior leaders) gets the step 2 priorities and step 1 results and drafts many candidate value statements that resonate and serve the strategy.Alignment does not need exact matches: someone who values achievement feels aligned with an organizational value like accomplishment. Guggenheim Museums: about 50 candidates, 25 shared organization-wide; a new chief culture officer added three.
4. Assess the candidatesGive everyone whose input matters a voice, not a vote.Benefits: reveals what resonates, and builds commitment. Leaders promise to consider all feedback but cannot promise any value, however popular, will be adopted. Tool used: All Our Ideas, which shows pairs and asks which is "more true, more useful"; alternatives are ranking all or picking a favorite five. Guggenheim included contractors, artists and board members.
5. Edit the top candidates into a final setA small team including leadership makes the substantive judgment calls.MOMA education department (2019): "Radicality" was popular but politically charged, so leaders kept it and reworded it so it tied to the mission and stayed acceptable to more conservative stakeholders. The slides add: then clarify the meaning, reiterate and use the values.

Results: New York-Presbyterian (NYP)

When Steven Corwin became CEO in 2011 (about 50,000 employees and affiliated physicians), he disguised himself as a custodian and cleaned hospital rooms, felt invisible, and concluded that respect mattered. NYP's four values: respect, quality, integrity, teamwork, with respect first. Years of values structures later, employees' top personal value was still respect (an ideal alignment). In spring 2020, NYP fared better than most hospitals during the Covid surge, in part, staff said, because the COO's daily briefings made them feel seen. The authors' closing point: values are powerful when aligned, but they are not magic; announcing them or carving them in marble does not make them real.

Example (invented): values alignment at a 120-person regional dental group

StepWhat happens at "Ridgeline Dental" (fictional)
1Every hygienist, assistant, dentist and front-desk person fills out a values structure. Frequent themes: respect, learning, reliability, family time.
2Leaders' strategy is same-day appointments for anxious first-time patients. Key priority: patients must trust us fast and flow must be reliable.
3A task force of two dentists, a hygienist, a billing lead and the practice manager drafts 30 candidates such as "Calm care," "Dependable," "Respect," "Always learning."
4All staff rank pairs ("Which is more true and useful?"). Leaders announce it is a voice, not a vote.
5The small team keeps "Respect," "Dependable," "Calm care." They write one clarifying sentence for each and use the values in hiring and weekly huddles.

Where Chapter 2 fits

In The Leadership Challenge (7th ed.), Chapter 2 "Clarify Values" opens the first of the five practices of exemplary leadership, Model the Way. Model the Way has two commitments: (1) clarify values by finding your voice and affirming shared values, and (2) set the example by aligning actions with shared values (Chapter 3). The chapter has two sections: Find Your Voice and Affirm Shared Values.

Find Your Voice (general explanation)

Before you can lead others, you need to know what you believe and care about, so that your words and actions come from your own convictions rather than from borrowed language. Credibility, which the book treats as the foundation of leadership, depends on people seeing your words and actions match. Check your copy for the chapter's specific exercises and examples.

Affirm Shared Values (general explanation)

A leader does not simply announce personal values. The leader's job is to build agreement around values a group can share and to give them voice, so people are committed to them rather than complying with them. Check your copy for the chapter's specifics.

Management as a discipline: normative vs behavioral

Normative theoryBehavioral analysis
How things should work: the "ideal student" does all reading in advance, does further reading, schedules lunches with classmates, uses a highlighting system.Asks why people really act as they do: the "real student" reads later, skims, splits work and shares summaries, waits until the last minute, is time-poor and trying to figure out what matters.

The slides' framework: organizational analysis is behavioral, PERSON x SITUATION. Understanding "why" people act as they do may reveal that normative models are unrealistic, shows the factors that constrain individual behavior, helps get closer to the normative model, and may lead to new normative models that work better in practice.

Example (invented): Normative rule: "Every manager holds a weekly one-on-one." Behavioral analysis finds managers are double-booked and skip them when a deadline hits. A fix that respects the situation: short standing check-ins built into existing team meetings.

What is management's value, and what managers actually do

Slide list of management's value: coordination and control, making things work better, order and consistency, planning, inventory management, onboarding and training, staffing and retention, lower costs and budgeting, profit orientation, new equipment ("Efficiency = $"). It cites Kotter (2001, "What Leaders Really Do") for what managers do ideally. Reality, from Henry Mintzberg's research (optional reading, "The Manager's Job," 1975): the job is reactive, time-poor, highly fragmented, hectic, with many demands, little planning time and few value-producing activities.

Do we overestimate extrinsic motivation? (Heath, 1999)

Citibank managers rated how motivated they were by intrinsic and extrinsic rewards and predicted the answers for 30 employees they knew well; the employees completed the same survey. Managers overestimated how much employees care about extrinsic factors. Implications for managers

The default assumption is that extrinsic matters more than intrinsic, but some data say otherwise. If you believe people want mainly extrinsic rewards you will: (1) be less likely to explain a task's importance and link it to broader goals; (2) be less likely to give responsibility, accountability and voice; (3) be more likely to neglect making jobs interesting; (4) be more likely to overemphasize extrinsic incentives.

Christensen on the slides, other bits

The slides summarize Christensen's three questions with short answers: career happiness = intrinsic motivation, relationships, building people up; family = allocate resources to them and build a culture; staying out of jail = avoid the slippery slope of "just this once." Also on the slides: a class liaison role (informal feedback channel to the professor), the TA Charlotte Ferlin's tips (do the reading early and take notes, bring a good attitude, have fun) and an icebreaker (name tag with a work strength, find a compatible partner and something non-obvious in common).

The thread

Week 1 asks: what do people (and organizations) truly value, and how do you align actions with it? Christensen is the personal version (purpose, allocation, integrity); Ingram & Choi is the organizational version (a process to align values with strategy and employees); K&P Chapter 2 is the leadership version (find your voice, affirm shared values); the slides supply the motivation and behavioral lens.

PairThey reinforce each other because...
Christensen & slidesHerzberg's motivators = the slides' intrinsic motivators (learning, contribution, purpose). Managers who assume pay is everything miss them.
Christensen & Ingram/ChoiChristensen's culture ("people act by instinct") and Ingram's values alignment both say shared values beat power tools and imposed rules. The "values as magic" trap resembles relying on power tools.
Ingram/Choi & K&PStep 1 is "find your voice" collectively; steps 3 to 5 are "affirm shared values." Both reject top-down announcements.
Christensen's "just this once" & K&P/IngramValues only work if lived 100% of the time; K&P Chapter 3 (Week 2) moves from clarifying to setting the example.

Where they differ or need care

  • Individual vs shared: Christensen's purpose is personal (his came from faith); Ingram & Choi say you cannot import someone else's values, and values must be held by employees.
  • Number of values: the article's structure has eight values; the slide exercise says circle 3 to 5.
  • Normative vs behavioral: the "ideal" values process works only if you account for real behavior (the person x situation lens): cynical employees, time pressure, "just this once."
  • Voice vs vote: inclusion builds commitment, but leaders keep final authority.

Example (invented): A new operations manager wants to improve on-time delivery. Intrinsic lens: explain why on-time delivery matters to customers. Values-alignment lens: ask the team what they value and which proposed norms fit. Christensen lens: when a client begs for one shortcut, hold the line, because the next exception will be easier. K&P lens: say it, then do it consistently.
