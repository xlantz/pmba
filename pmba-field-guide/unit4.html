MGT 6050 · Unit 4

Fairness, diversity, and the limits of teamwork

Units 1–3 built up what a real team is, how to launch one well, and how to keep people speaking up. Unit 4 asks what happens when that foundation cracks. Two research pieces establish the building blocks: a team-level "fairness climate" that can make or break performance, and the real cognitive benefits diversity brings to group decision-making. Then a two-part 1985 case study — the internal collapse of a 134-year-old Wall Street partnership — gives you a real, high-stakes story to test both frameworks against.

Unit 4, source by source

Henley, A. B., & Price, K. H. (2002). "Want a Better Team? Foster a Climate of Fairness." Academy of Management Executive, 16(3), 153–154. Summarizing: Colquitt, J. A., Noe, R. A., & Jackson, C. L. (2002). "Justice in Teams: Antecedents and Consequences of Procedural Justice Climate." Personnel Psychology, 55, 83–100.

Big idea

Fairness isn't just something individuals perceive on their own — teams develop a shared, collective sense of how fairly they're being treated, called a fairness (justice) climate. That climate is measurably linked to real outcomes: team performance and absenteeism.

The study

Colquitt, Noe, and Jackson surveyed 88 semi-autonomous teams (over 1,700 members) at six plants of an automotive parts manufacturer. Team members were surveyed individually about fairness, diversity, and collectivism; individual responses were averaged to create a team-level score. Supervisory ratings measured performance; company records tracked absences.

What predicts a team's fairness climate

  • Team size: the bigger the team, the less likely members were to feel fairly treated, and the less they agreed with each other about it. Larger teams make it harder for members to voice opinions about things like policies and procedures.
  • Team diversity: more diverse teams (gender, ethnicity, other demographics) showed less agreement among members about how fairly they were being treated — though diversity itself didn't directly lower the fairness level, just the consensus about it.
  • Collective orientation: when team members believed team interests outweigh individual ones, they were more likely to feel fairly treated (though this collective mindset didn't increase agreement about it — a different effect than diversity's).

What fairness climate predicts

The key finding: agreement amplifies whatever direction the climate points. Teams that agreed they were treated unfairly had the worst performance and highest absenteeism. Teams that agreed they were treated fairly had the best performance and lowest absenteeism. Agreement itself is what strengthens both the positive and negative effects — a team that's split on whether treatment is fair doesn't get the full benefit (or full cost) either way.

Practical implications

  • Staff teams, where possible, with people who already have a positive orientation toward teamwork.
  • Train (or select) team leaders to actively build a fairness climate — especially in larger or more diverse teams, where it's harder to achieve naturally.
  • Include all members in decisions that affect the team, and create real opportunities to voice disagreement.
  • Apply procedures consistently, and when there's a deviation, explain it fully and carefully to everyone — consistency and shared understanding are what drive agreement about fairness, not just the substance of the decision itself.
Exam angleThe subtle point examiners like to test: diversity and collectivism affect fairness climate through two different channels. Diversity mainly hurts agreement (consensus) without necessarily lowering the average fairness score. Collectivism mainly raises the average fairness score without necessarily improving agreement. Don't collapse these into one effect.

Phillips, K. W. (2014, October 1). "How Diversity Makes Us Smarter." Scientific American.

Big idea

Social diversity (race, gender, sexual orientation, and other social categories — not just expertise) makes groups more creative, more diligent, and better at problem-solving. The mechanism isn't magic: it comes down to informational diversity and an anticipation effect that changes how people prepare and behave.

Informational diversity: the core mechanism

People who differ from one another bring different information, opinions, and experiences to a task — the same logic that makes an interdisciplinary team (engineers + designers + quality-control experts) obviously useful also applies to social diversity. A male and a female engineer can have perspectives as different from each other as an engineer and a physicist.

Large-scale evidence (correlational)

  • Dezső & Ross (S&P 1500 firms, 1992–2006): female representation in top management was associated with a $42 million increase in firm value on average — and the effect was stronger for firms that prioritized innovation.
  • Richard (2003, 177 U.S. banks): racial diversity was linked to better financial performance specifically at innovation-focused banks.
  • Credit Suisse Research Institute (2012, 2,360 global companies): companies with one or more women on the board had higher average returns on equity, lower net debt to equity, and better growth.

Small-group evidence (causal)

  • Phillips, Neale & Northcraft (2006): three-person groups solving a murder-mystery exercise (where each member held unique clues) performed significantly better when racially diverse than when all-white.
  • Antonio et al. (2004): when a Black group member voiced a dissenting opinion to a group of white people, it was perceived as more novel and provoked broader thinking than the identical dissent delivered by a white group member.
  • Loyd, Wang, Lount & Phillips (2013): Democrats and Republicans prepared harder for a debate when told their disagreeing partner was from the opposing party than when told the partner shared their own party — even though the content of the disagreement was identical.
  • Freeman & Huang (2014, 1.5 million papers): papers written by ethnically diverse author teams received more citations and had higher impact than papers from more homogeneous teams.
  • Sommers (2006, mock juries): racially diverse six-person juries (four white, two Black) considered case facts more carefully and made fewer factual errors than all-white juries — not because the Black jurors introduced new information, but because the white jurors became more diligent and open-minded in the presence of diversity.

The anticipation effect

Homogeneous groups assume they'll agree and understand each other easily. The moment people notice they're socially different from one another, they anticipate disagreement — and start working harder, both cognitively and socially, to prepare for it. This happens even before any actual interaction takes place, which is why the mere presence of diversity (not just the information diverse members happen to contribute) changes group behavior for the better.

Exam angleBe ready to explain the Sommers jury finding precisely: the improvement came from the majority group members changing their own behavior, not from novel information the minority members added. This is the clearest evidence that diversity's benefit runs through anticipation and effort, not simply through "more facts on the table."

Auletta, K. (1985, February 17). "Power, Greed and Glory on Wall Street: The Fall of the Lehman Brothers." The New York Times Magazine.

Big idea

In 1983, Lehman Brothers Kuhn Loeb — Wall Street's oldest continuing investment-banking partnership, 134 years old and enjoying its most profitable year ever — was torn apart from within by a power struggle between its two co-CEOs. Within ten months, it would be sold. This first installment covers the personal and cultural roots of that struggle.

The two co-CEOs

  • Peter G. Peterson — polished, Establishment-connected, a former Bell & Howell president and Nixon's Secretary of Commerce. The firm's "Mr. Outside": public image, client relationships, name-dropping ("Henry," "Paul," "the Prime Minister").
  • Lewis L. Glucksman — a blunt, volatile trader who had spent 21 years at Lehman clawing his way up. The firm's "Mr. Inside": ran day-to-day operations, understood every department's numbers.

The banker vs. trader divide

Much of the conflict flowed from a cultural fault line running through Lehman (and all of Wall Street): bankers take a longer horizon, cultivate client relationships, and earn fees advising on deals; traders make fast, numbers-driven decisions buying and selling securities, often with the firm's own money. Bankers were traditionally seen as the elite; traders, including by some of their own partners, as lowly hired hands — despite traders generating two-thirds of the firm's profits.

Resentment as the hidden driver

Glucksman had accumulated resentments for decades: against "Our Crowd" Jews (established German-Jewish Wall Street families) who he felt looked down on him as a striving Eastern European Jew; against bankers who dismissed traders; against the sense that status, not performance, decided who was respected. He interpreted Peterson's polish and Establishment ties as more of the same condescension — even though Peterson himself was the son of Greek immigrants.

The lunch that broke the partnership

On July 12, 1983, at a lunch hosted by an outside executive, Peterson was seated at the head of the table; Glucksman was seated far down the side — "the equivalent, to him, of being seated in the bleachers." As Peterson delivered his familiar name-dropping "speech," Glucksman seethed, fidgeting and interrupting. He left the lunch determined to remove Peterson. Thirteen days later, after secret negotiations and a generous severance agreement, Peterson was gone.

A name to watch

As part of the reshuffling that followed, Glucksman promoted his 37-year-old protégé Richard S. Fuld Jr. to run all of Lehman's trading operations. Fuld would go on to become Lehman Brothers' CEO more than two decades later — and would lead the firm through its far more famous 2008 bankruptcy, the largest in U.S. history.

Exam angleThis case is often mistaken for the 2008 Lehman Brothers collapse — it's a different, earlier event (the 1983–84 sale to Shearson/American Express), though Fuld's appearance here as a young protégé is a real, documented connection between the two stories. Keep the timelines straight if asked to compare them.

Auletta, K. (1985, February 24). "The Fall of Lehman Brothers: The Men, The Money, The Merger." The New York Times Magazine.

Big idea

Having won control, Glucksman moved fast — too fast, by his own closest adviser's later admission — and a single bonus and stock-allocation decision in September 1983 detonated a slow-motion crisis of perceived unfairness that, within months, cost Lehman its independence.

The bonus decision that broke trust

Glucksman and four allies (mostly traders) took 25% of the entire year-end bonus pool. Several senior banking partners saw their bonuses cut or barely increased despite strong individual years, while trading-side allies saw large jumps. Stock allocation followed the same pattern — Glucksman's own share rose sharply. The decisions were made without full board discussion, violating a partnership's normal consensual process for dividing ownership.

The exodus

By early October, four partners had left or announced plans to leave; by November, six partners had departed, taking roughly $30 million (about 17% of Lehman's capital) with them. One departing partner, Eric Gleacher — the banker closest to Glucksman — said plainly: "What they did when they paid out the bonuses in September was the primary reason I left... Anybody with common sense would not be so greedy."

"A classic management error"

The turning point: Glucksman allowed a contentious board meeting to take place without him there. In that meeting, for the first time, nearly every board member voiced worry about the firm's capital or openly wanted to sell — something no one had said aloud while Glucksman was in the room. As board member Henry Breck put it: "What Glucksman had done is allow the board meeting to take place without his being there. It was a classic management error. An error we never allow our corporate clients to make." Power effectively shifted from Glucksman to the board in that single absence.

The forced sale

The board appointed a committee to study "capital alternatives" — a euphemism for exploring a sale. By early 1984, Lehman merged into Shearson/American Express, ending 134 years of independence. Peterson, appearing before the board to explain the severance terms he and Glucksman had privately negotiated for himself, was challenged by partner Peter Solomon: "Have you resigned, and are asking us to vote? Or is this an issue open to discussion?" Peterson replied, "I have resigned." No one else spoke up.

Two tragedies, two value systems

Older partners called the sale a "tragedy" of broken tradition and community — "My home was destroyed," said one, who'd joined the firm in 1928. A younger partner, Stephen Schwarzman, called it a tragedy too, but for a different first reason: "the business was not sold at the optimal time. Hence, an optimal price was not realized." Same word, two entirely different value systems about what the firm was actually for.

Exam angleThe signature synthesis prompt for this unit: use Henley & Price's fairness-climate research to name exactly what Glucksman got wrong in the September 1983 bonus decision (concentration of rewards among allies, no board consultation, no consistent explanation to those who lost out) — then explain why the partners' agreement that they'd been treated unfairly (not just the unfairness itself) is what makes the resulting exodus so consistent with the research's key finding about agreement amplifying outcomes.

Two sources, one story. The two Auletta pieces were published a week apart in 1985 as a single narrative — Part 1 covers the personal and cultural roots of the Glucksman–Peterson conflict; Part 2 covers what Glucksman did with the power he won, and how that decision cost him (and the firm) everything. Read them in order.

Before the exam

Likely exam & discussion angles

  • Given a team scenario, identify whether the fairness-climate issue is about the average level of fairness, the level of agreement about it, or both — and name which factor (size, diversity, collectivism) is most likely driving it.
  • Explain why diversity's benefit in the Sommers jury study came from majority-group behavior change rather than new information from minority members, and generalize that insight to a workplace scenario.
  • Walk through the Lehman Brothers case and identify the single decision (the September 1983 bonus allocation) that best illustrates a fairness-climate failure, citing the specific mechanisms Henley & Price's research would predict (no consultation, concentrated rewards, inconsistent explanation).
  • Explain what "a classic management error" meant in this case, and why a leader's mere physical absence from one meeting can represent a genuine governance failure.
  • The signature synthesis prompt: Peterson and Glucksman were about as different from each other — in style, background self-presentation, and worldview — as two co-CEOs could be. Does Phillips's diversity research suggest this difference should have been an asset? Explain why it became a liability instead, tying your answer to trust and psychological safety concepts from Units 1–2, not just to the diversity research alone.
  • Cross-unit synthesis: compare the "capital search" euphemism and Peterson's "I have resigned" moment (silence from the rest of the board) to Unit 3's NASA Chicken and humiliation-factor concepts — two more examples of people not speaking up when it mattered most.
