Team Contract Builder

Ten sections, each grounded in a specific course framework — fill this out with your real team and it doubles as a way to apply the readings, not just memorize them. Use it at your team's launch, and revisit it at the midpoint. Your answers save automatically in this browser only — nothing is sent anywhere or shared with anyone else.

This worksheet isn't a template your professor provided — it's built from course frameworks (Katzenbach & Smith's team basics, the process-loss fixes from Class 1, psychological safety, Riordan & O'Brien's social contract, and Barrett's team charter) to help you apply them to a real team. If your course later distributes an official team contract template or rubric, use that as the authoritative version and treat this as a head start. Your assignment's actual format requirement (check your syllabus) may call for a different structure — use this to think it through, then adapt into whatever format is required.

1. Purpose

Grounds: Katzenbach & Smith's "common, meaningful purpose"; "purposing" as an ongoing activity, not a one-time statement.

  • What demand or opportunity brought this team together? (What's the "solution space" your instructor/assignment gave you?)
  • In your own words, what is this team actually here to accomplish?
  • How will we revisit and re-sharpen this purpose as the project evolves?

2. Specific Performance Goals

Grounds: "specific performance goals" as the second team-basics element; goals provide trackability, small wins, and accountability that purpose alone can't.

  • What are the concrete, measurable deliverables and deadlines?
  • What does "success" look like at each milestone?

3. Complementary Skills & Division of Labor

Grounds: the three skill categories (technical/functional, problem-solving/decision-making, interpersonal); "base division of labor on the group's ultimate goal, not convenience."

  • What skills/strengths does each member bring?
  • What skill gaps exist, and how will the team close them as you go?
  • Who owns which piece of work, and why?
  • Is there a designated "integration"/"big picture" role?

4. Common Working Approach

Grounds: "common working approach" (economic/administrative + social); "committed to a common approach [but not too much]" — some flexibility is healthy.

  • How will we make decisions — consensus, majority vote, designated owner per topic?
  • How will we schedule and structure work sessions?
  • How rigid vs. flexible should our process be?

5. Coordination Plan

Grounds: coordination losses and their fixes — "discuss coordination strategy at the start and reassess during the task"; coordination neglect; the Team Scaling Fallacy.

  • How will we share information and status updates so nothing falls through the cracks?
  • What's our plan for catching duplicated or missed work?
  • If we add or lose a member mid-project, how will we re-coordinate?

6. Communication Norms

Grounds: the three causes of miscommunication — institutional factors (channel choice), curse of knowledge, lack of common language/jargon.

  • What's our primary communication channel, and when do we escalate to a richer one?
  • How will we check that everyone actually understood something?
  • Do we have any jargon or shorthand that needs explaining to everyone up front?

7. Mutual Accountability

Grounds: the defining test of a real team — "we hold ourselves accountable" vs. "the boss holds me accountable."

  • What happens if someone misses a deadline or falls short — who addresses it, and how?
  • How do we hold the team (not just individuals) accountable for the overall result?
  • What does follow-through look like if a member is struggling?

8. Motivation & Effort

Grounds: fixes for effort/motivation losses — clarify expectations early, make contributions identifiable, build trust, give feedback, reward performance.

  • How will individual contributions stay visible (guards against social loafing)?
  • How often will we give each other feedback, and in what format?
  • What's genuinely interesting or meaningful about this project to each member?

9. Psychological Safety & Conflict Norms

Grounds: ability-loss fixes (psychological safety, trust); Hackman's "deviant" — the member who challenges consensus and improves originality; Edmondson's four tactics (Unit 2).

  • How do we want disagreement or dissent to be raised and handled?
  • What will we do if someone feels unheard?
  • How do we want to handle a small friction moment before it compounds? (Rozovsky's "Ouch" example is a good model — name it directly.)

10. Review & Amendment

Grounds: the Team Performance Cycle — transition (planning) phase vs. action (doing) phase; "purposing" as continuous; Unit 2's finding that high-performing teams revisit their social contract over time.

  • When will we formally revisit this contract (e.g., at the project midpoint)?
  • What triggers an off-cycle revisit (a missed deadline, a new member, a scope change)?
