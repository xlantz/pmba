Inventory costing & long-lived assets

Two asset categories that behave very differently once you dig in. Unit 6, section by section

Why costing method even matters

When a company buys the same type of inventory at different prices over time, it has to decide which of those costs get expensed as Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) when a unit is sold, and which costs stay on the balance sheet as ending inventory. Three methods handle this differently, and — critically — two otherwise-identical companies can report very different COGS, gross profit, and inventory simply because they picked different methods.

The three methods

  • FIFO (First-In, First-Out) — assumes the oldest costs are expensed first. Ending inventory is made up of the most recently purchased costs.
  • LIFO (Last-In, First-Out) — assumes the most recently purchased costs are expensed first. Ending inventory is made up of the oldest costs (sometimes decades old for companies that have used LIFO a long time).
  • Weighted average — blends all costs together into a single average cost per unit, applied to both COGS and ending inventory.

Worked example

A company begins the year with 400 units at $10 each. It purchases 500 units at $12 each, then 300 units at $15 each. It sells 900 units. Total units available = 1,200; total cost available = 4,000 + 6,000 + 4,500 = $14,500.

MethodCOGS (900 units)Ending inventory (300 units)
FIFO400@$10 + 500@$12 = $10,000300@$15 = $4,500
LIFO300@$15 + 500@$12 + 100@$10 = $11,500300@$10 = $3,000
Weighted average900 × ($14,500/1,200 = $12.083) = $10,875300 × $12.083 = $3,625

Notice: whichever method produces the higher COGS necessarily produces the lower ending inventory, and vice versa — total cost available ($14,500) always splits between the two, no matter which method is used.

The direction flips with the price trend

All of the classic "which method does what" rules assume rising costs (the normal, inflationary case). In a period of rising costs:

  • FIFO: lowest COGS, highest ending inventory, highest gross profit — because it expenses old, cheap costs first.
  • LIFO: highest COGS, lowest ending inventory, lowest gross profit — because it expenses new, expensive costs first.
  • Weighted average: lands in between the two.

If costs are falling instead, every one of these relationships reverses: FIFO becomes the highest-COGS/lowest-inventory method, and LIFO becomes the lowest-COGS/highest-inventory method.

Exam angleDon't memorize "FIFO = low COGS" as an absolute fact — memorize the mechanism (FIFO expenses old costs first) and derive the effect from whatever price trend the question describes. Questions love to flip to a falling-price scenario specifically to catch memorized-rule answers.

What it is

Companies that use LIFO are required to disclose their LIFO reserve — the dollar difference between what inventory would be reported at under FIFO and what it's actually reported at under LIFO:

LIFO Reserve = FIFO Inventory − LIFO Inventory

In a rising-cost environment, the LIFO reserve is normally positive and grows over time, since LIFO inventory increasingly understates the current cost of what's actually on the shelves.

Why analysts care

The LIFO reserve is the bridge that lets an analyst compare a LIFO company with a FIFO company on equal footing:

FIFO Inventory = LIFO Inventory + LIFO Reserve

To convert LIFO figures to an as-if-FIFO basis: add the LIFO reserve to reported inventory (and, correspondingly, to total assets and equity, net of any tax effect a more advanced treatment would consider).

Converting COGS using the change in the reserve

The reserve's change during the year, not just its ending balance, converts COGS between the two methods:

FIFO COGS = LIFO COGS − Increase in LIFO Reserve during the year

Worked example: a company's LIFO reserve grows from $400,000 to $460,000 during the year. The $60,000 increase means LIFO COGS is $60,000 higher than FIFO COGS would have been for that same year — consistent with LIFO expensing more expensive, recent costs first in an inflationary environment.

Exam angleKeep the ending balance and the change in balance straight — the ending LIFO reserve converts a balance sheet figure (inventory) at a point in time; the change in the reserve over the year converts an income statement figure (COGS) for that year.

Inventory turnover

Measures how many times, on average, a company sells through its entire inventory balance in a year:

Inventory Turnover = COGS ÷ Average Inventory

Higher turnover generally means inventory is moving faster — less cash tied up sitting on shelves, and lower risk of obsolescence.

Days inventory outstanding (DIO)

Converts turnover into an average number of days inventory sits before being sold:

DIO = 365 ÷ Inventory Turnover

Worked example: a retailer reports COGS of $18 million and average inventory of $3 million. Turnover = $18M/$3M = 6.0. DIO = 365/6.0 ≈ 60.8 days — the retailer holds inventory for roughly 61 days on average before it sells.

Turnover, DIO, and costing method interact

Because LIFO and FIFO report different COGS and different average inventory in a rising-cost environment, a LIFO company's turnover ratio isn't directly comparable to a FIFO competitor's without adjusting for the LIFO reserve first — another reason the reserve matters for analysis, not just disclosure compliance.

Exam angleA rising turnover ratio (or falling DIO) isn't automatically good news — check whether it's driven by genuinely faster sales, or by a company running unusually lean (understocked) in a way that risks stockouts.

Straight-line depreciation

Spreads an asset's depreciable cost evenly over its useful life:

Annual Depreciation = (Cost − Salvage Value) ÷ Useful Life

Worked example: equipment costs $60,000, has a $5,000 salvage value, and a five-year useful life. Annual depreciation = ($60,000 − $5,000)/5 = $11,000 per year.

Partial-year depreciation

When an asset is placed in service partway through the year, depreciation for that first (and often last) year is prorated for the fraction of the year the asset was actually in service.

Worked example: the same equipment above is purchased on September 1. It's in service for 4 of 12 months that calendar year. First-year depreciation = $11,000 × 4/12 = $3,667 (rounded).

Net book value & disposal

An asset's net book value (NBV) at any point is:

Net Book Value = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation

When an asset is sold, compare the sale proceeds to its NBV at the time of sale:

Gain (or Loss) on Disposal = Proceeds − Net Book Value

Worked example: equipment with an original cost of $60,000 and accumulated depreciation of $44,000 has an NBV of $16,000. If sold for $21,000, the company recognizes a $5,000 gain ($21,000 − $16,000). If instead sold for $9,000, it recognizes a $7,000 loss ($9,000 − $16,000).

PP&E turnover

Measures how efficiently a company generates sales from its investment in long-lived assets:

PP&E Turnover = Sales ÷ PP&E

A higher ratio means the company generates more sales per dollar tied up in property, plant, and equipment — though capital-intensive industries (manufacturing, utilities) naturally run lower PP&E turnover than asset-light industries (services, software), so this ratio is most meaningful compared within an industry, not across very different ones.

Exam angleA sale at exactly NBV produces neither a gain nor a loss — a common wrong answer is assuming a sale always produces one or the other. Also double check whether a question is asking for depreciation for a full year or a partial year based on the purchase date given.

Before the exam

Likely exam & discussion angles

  • Given costs, quantities, and a price trend, compute COGS and ending inventory under FIFO, LIFO, and weighted average, then explain why the three don't match.
  • Explain why the standard "FIFO = low COGS" rule flips in a falling-cost environment.
  • Convert a LIFO company's inventory and COGS to a FIFO basis using the LIFO reserve and its year-over-year change.
  • Compute inventory turnover and DIO, and explain what a rising or falling value of each actually indicates.
  • Compute straight-line depreciation for a full year and for a partial year based on an asset's in-service date.
  • Given an asset's cost, accumulated depreciation, and sale price, determine whether the disposal produces a gain, a loss, or neither.
  • Compute PP&E turnover and explain why it's most meaningful compared within a single industry.
