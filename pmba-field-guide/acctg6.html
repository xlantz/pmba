Inventory costing & long-lived assets

Two asset categories that behave very differently once you dig in. Inventory costing methods (FIFO, LIFO, weighted average) can make otherwise-identical companies report wildly different numbers — and the direction of that difference flips depending on whether costs are rising or falling. Then property, plant & equipment: what actually gets capitalized into an asset's cost, three ways to spread that cost over time (straight-line, double-declining balance, units of production), and what happens on the day an asset is finally sold. Plus the cash conversion cycle and intangible assets — copyrights, trademarks, patents, and goodwill.

Unit 6, section by section

Why costing method even matters

When a company buys the same type of inventory at different prices over time, it has to decide which of those costs get expensed as Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) when a unit is sold, and which costs stay on the balance sheet as ending inventory. Three methods handle this differently, and — critically — two otherwise-identical companies can report very different COGS, gross profit, and inventory simply because they picked different methods.

The three methods

  • FIFO (First-In, First-Out) — assumes the oldest costs are expensed first. Ending inventory is made up of the most recently purchased costs.
  • LIFO (Last-In, First-Out) — assumes the most recently purchased costs are expensed first. Ending inventory is made up of the oldest costs (sometimes decades old for companies that have used LIFO a long time).
  • Weighted average — blends all costs together into a single average cost per unit, applied to both COGS and ending inventory.

Worked example

A company begins the year with 400 units at $10 each. It purchases 500 units at $12 each, then 300 units at $15 each. It sells 900 units. Total units available = 1,200; total cost available = 4,000 + 6,000 + 4,500 = $14,500.

MethodCOGS (900 units)Ending inventory (300 units)
FIFO400@$10 + 500@$12 = $10,000300@$15 = $4,500
LIFO300@$15 + 500@$12 + 100@$10 = $11,500300@$10 = $3,000
Weighted average900 × ($14,500/1,200 = $12.083) = $10,875300 × $12.083 = $3,625

Notice: whichever method produces the higher COGS necessarily produces the lower ending inventory, and vice versa — total cost available ($14,500) always splits between the two, no matter which method is used.

The direction flips with the price trend

All of the classic "which method does what" rules assume rising costs (the normal, inflationary case). In a period of rising costs:

  • FIFO: lowest COGS, highest ending inventory, highest gross profit — because it expenses old, cheap costs first.
  • LIFO: highest COGS, lowest ending inventory, lowest gross profit — because it expenses new, expensive costs first.
  • Weighted average: lands in between the two.

If costs are falling instead, every one of these relationships reverses: FIFO becomes the highest-COGS/lowest-inventory method, and LIFO becomes the lowest-COGS/highest-inventory method.

Exam angleDon't memorize "FIFO = low COGS" as an absolute fact — memorize the mechanism (FIFO expenses old costs first) and derive the effect from whatever price trend the question describes. Questions love to flip to a falling-price scenario specifically to catch memorized-rule answers.

What it is

Companies that use LIFO are required to disclose their LIFO reserve — the dollar difference between what inventory would be reported at under FIFO and what it's actually reported at under LIFO:

LIFO Reserve = FIFO Inventory − LIFO Inventory

In a rising-cost environment, the LIFO reserve is normally positive and grows over time, since LIFO inventory increasingly understates the current cost of what's actually on the shelves.

Why analysts care

The LIFO reserve is the bridge that lets an analyst compare a LIFO company with a FIFO company on equal footing:

FIFO Inventory = LIFO Inventory + LIFO Reserve

To convert LIFO figures to an as-if-FIFO basis: add the LIFO reserve to reported inventory (and, correspondingly, to total assets and equity, net of any tax effect a more advanced treatment would consider).

Converting COGS using the change in the reserve

The reserve's change during the year, not just its ending balance, converts COGS between the two methods:

FIFO COGS = LIFO COGS − Increase in LIFO Reserve during the year

Worked example: a company's LIFO reserve grows from $400,000 to $460,000 during the year. The $60,000 increase means LIFO COGS is $60,000 higher than FIFO COGS would have been for that same year — consistent with LIFO expensing more expensive, recent costs first in an inflationary environment.

Exam angleKeep the ending balance and the change in balance straight — the ending LIFO reserve converts a balance sheet figure (inventory) at a point in time; the change in the reserve over the year converts an income statement figure (COGS) for that year.

Inventory turnover

Measures how many times, on average, a company sells through its entire inventory balance in a year:

Inventory Turnover = COGS ÷ Average Inventory

Higher turnover generally means inventory is moving faster — less cash tied up sitting on shelves, and lower risk of obsolescence.

Days inventory outstanding (DIO)

Converts turnover into an average number of days inventory sits before being sold:

DIO = 365 ÷ Inventory Turnover

Worked example: a retailer reports COGS of $18 million and average inventory of $3 million. Turnover = $18M/$3M = 6.0. DIO = 365/6.0 ≈ 60.8 days — the retailer holds inventory for roughly 61 days on average before it sells.

Turnover, DIO, and costing method interact

Because LIFO and FIFO report different COGS and different average inventory in a rising-cost environment, a LIFO company's turnover ratio isn't directly comparable to a FIFO competitor's without adjusting for the LIFO reserve first — another reason the reserve matters for analysis, not just disclosure compliance.

The other two legs: DSO and DPO

DIO measures how fast inventory sells. Two related ratios measure the rest of the operating cycle — how fast the company collects cash from customers, and how slowly it pays its own suppliers:

DSO (Days Sales Outstanding) = (Average Accounts Receivable ÷ Sales Revenue) × 365
DPO (Days Payable Outstanding) = (Average Accounts Payable ÷ COGS) × 365

DSO measures the average number of days it takes to collect cash after a credit sale. DPO measures the average number of days the company takes to pay its own suppliers — note it uses COGS (what suppliers are being paid for), not sales.

Putting the three together: the cash conversion cycle

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) combines all three into a single measure of how many days of cash are tied up in day-to-day operations:

CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO

Worked example: a company reports average accounts receivable of $500,000 on sales of $6,000,000; average inventory of $700,000 on COGS of $4,200,000; and average accounts payable of $350,000.

MetricCalculationResult
DSO($500,000 / $6,000,000) × 36530.4 days
DIO($700,000 / $4,200,000) × 36560.8 days
DPO($350,000 / $4,200,000) × 36530.4 days
CCC30.4 + 60.8 − 30.460.8 days

The lower the CCC, the better — less cash is tied up between paying for inventory and collecting from customers. A company can even run a negative CCC (common in some retail and subscription models), meaning it collects cash from customers before it even has to pay its own suppliers for that same inventory.

Exam angleA rising turnover ratio (or falling DIO) isn't automatically good news — check whether it's driven by genuinely faster sales, or by a company running unusually lean (understocked) in a way that risks stockouts. For CCC questions specifically, watch the denominators: DSO and DPO both use 365-day annualization, but DSO divides by sales while DIO and DPO both divide by COGS — a common trap is dividing all three by the same figure.

What goes into the PP&E cost basis — and what doesn't

Not every dollar spent around the purchase of a long-lived asset gets capitalized (added to the asset's cost). The test is whether the cost is necessary to get the asset ready for its intended use, or whether it's an ongoing cost of operating the business.

Included in PP&E costExcluded (expensed when incurred)
Delivery and set-up costsMinor/regular repairs and maintenance
Taxes on the purchaseInsurance on the asset
Direct costs to build (materials, labor)Wages of employees who operate/use the asset
Overhauls that extend useful life or add substantial structural improvementsRoutine servicing that just keeps the asset running as originally intended

The recurring theme: costs that get the asset in place and ready to use, or that genuinely extend its life or capability, are capitalized. Costs that are part of the ordinary, repeated cost of owning and running the asset are expensed as incurred, even though the asset couldn't function without them (insurance, routine maintenance, operator wages).

Depreciation methods: three ways to spread the cost

Once a cost basis is set, it's depreciated over the asset's useful life using one of three methods. All three depreciate the same total amount (Cost − Salvage Value) over the asset's life — they differ only in when that total gets expensed.

Straight-line depreciation

The most common method. Spreads an asset's depreciable cost evenly over its useful life:

Annual Depreciation = (Cost − Salvage Value) ÷ Useful Life

Worked example: equipment costs $60,000, has a $5,000 salvage value, and a five-year useful life. Annual depreciation = ($60,000 − $5,000)/5 = $11,000 per year, every year, for five years.

Double-declining balance (DDB)

An accelerated method: depreciates more in the early years of an asset's life and less in later years. It applies a constant rate — double the straight-line rate — to the asset's declining net book value each year (not its original cost, and salvage value is ignored in the annual calculation, except as a floor):

DDB Rate = 2 × (1 ÷ Useful Life)     Annual Depreciation = DDB Rate × Beginning-of-Year Net Book Value

Worked example: the same $60,000 asset, $5,000 salvage value, five-year life. Straight-line rate = 1/5 = 20%, so the DDB rate = 40%.

YearBeginning NBVDepreciation (40%)Ending NBV
1$60,000$24,000$36,000
2$36,000$14,400$21,600
3$21,600$8,640$12,960
4$12,960$5,184$7,776
5$7,776$2,776*$5,000

*Year 5 is capped: the formula would call for $7,776 × 40% = $3,110, but that would push NBV below the $5,000 salvage value, which isn't allowed — an asset is never depreciated below salvage. So year 5's depreciation is plugged at whatever amount brings NBV down to exactly $5,000 ($7,776 − $5,000 = $2,776).

Units of production

Ties depreciation to actual usage (units made, hours run, miles driven) instead of time. Useful when an asset's wear and tear tracks output rather than the calendar:

Rate per Unit = (Cost − Salvage Value) ÷ Total Estimated Units of Output

Worked example: a machine costs $100,000, has a $10,000 salvage value, and is expected to produce 90,000 units over its life. Rate per unit = ($100,000 − $10,000)/90,000 = $1.00 per unit. If the machine produces 12,000 units in its first year, that year's depreciation = 12,000 × $1.00 = $12,000. A slower year with only 6,000 units produced would recognize just $6,000 of depreciation — the expense moves with output, not with the passage of time.

Partial-year depreciation

When an asset is placed in service partway through the year, depreciation for that first (and often last) year is prorated for the fraction of the year the asset was actually in service.

Worked example: the same equipment above is purchased on September 1. It's in service for 4 of 12 months that calendar year. First-year depreciation = $11,000 × 4/12 = $3,667 (rounded).

Net book value & disposal

An asset's net book value (NBV) at any point is:

Net Book Value = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation

When an asset is sold, compare the sale proceeds to its NBV at the time of sale:

Gain (or Loss) on Disposal = Proceeds − Net Book Value

Worked example: equipment with an original cost of $60,000 and accumulated depreciation of $44,000 has an NBV of $16,000. If sold for $21,000, the company recognizes a $5,000 gain ($21,000 − $16,000). If instead sold for $9,000, it recognizes a $7,000 loss ($9,000 − $16,000).

PP&E turnover

Measures how efficiently a company generates sales from its investment in long-lived assets:

PP&E Turnover = Sales ÷ PP&E

A higher ratio means the company generates more sales per dollar tied up in property, plant, and equipment — though capital-intensive industries (manufacturing, utilities) naturally run lower PP&E turnover than asset-light industries (services, software), so this ratio is most meaningful compared within an industry, not across very different ones.

Exam angleA sale at exactly NBV produces neither a gain nor a loss — a common wrong answer is assuming a sale always produces one or the other. Also double check whether a question is asking for depreciation for a full year or a partial year based on the purchase date given, and whether it names straight-line, DDB, or units of production — each produces a different annual number from the same cost and salvage value. For DDB specifically, remember the rate applies to declining NBV, not original cost, and that depreciation stops once NBV reaches salvage value.

What counts as an intangible asset

Intangible assets are long-lived assets with no physical substance, but that still provide the company a future economic benefit. Common types:

  • Copyright — legal protection for creative works (songs, films, written works, art).
  • Trademark — a symbol, logo, or phrase that legally identifies a company or its products.
  • Franchise/license — permission to operate under another company's brand, products, or processes.
  • Patent — the legal right to exclusively use an invention for a set period.
  • Goodwill — the portion of a company's value (typically arising in an acquisition) that isn't attributable to any of its individually identifiable assets.

What gets capitalized when a company creates its own intangible

This is the part that trips people up: for an intangible a company develops internally (rather than purchasing from someone else), very little of the cost actually gets capitalized onto the balance sheet as the intangible asset's cost.

  • Capitalized: direct costs to obtain legal/government approval — legal fees, filing fees for a patent or trademark application.
  • Expensed as incurred: essentially everything else that gives the intangible its value — R&D employee salaries, equipment, and office space used to develop the underlying invention, work, or brand.

In other words, a company can spend millions of dollars in R&D developing a breakthrough invention, all expensed as incurred, and then capitalize only a comparatively small legal/filing fee once it patents the result. This is a deliberate accounting choice reflecting how uncertain the payoff from R&D is until it actually succeeds — it's also why an internally-developed brand or invention is usually carried on the balance sheet at far less than what an outside buyer would actually pay for it (a company that instead purchases a patent, trademark, or other intangible from someone else capitalizes the full purchase price, since that cost is no longer uncertain).

Amortization: the intangible-asset version of depreciation

Intangible assets with a determinable, finite useful life are amortized — typically using the straight-line method — over that useful life, the same basic mechanic as straight-line depreciation for PP&E (Cost ÷ Useful Life, since intangibles are usually amortized to zero rather than to a salvage value).

Some intangibles have no determinable useful life because they're expected to provide a benefit indefinitely. These are not amortized at all — instead they're tested periodically for impairment. Common examples: goodwill, and trademarks/copyrights a company intends to renew and use indefinitely.

Exam angleTwo traps: (1) don't assume all the R&D-type spending behind a self-created intangible gets capitalized — only the direct legal/filing costs do; and (2) don't automatically amortize every intangible — check whether it has a finite useful life (amortize, usually straight-line) or an indefinite one like goodwill (don't amortize; test for impairment instead).

Before the exam

Likely exam & discussion angles

  • Given costs, quantities, and a price trend, compute COGS and ending inventory under FIFO, LIFO, and weighted average, then explain why the three don't match.
  • Explain why the standard "FIFO = low COGS" rule flips in a falling-cost environment.
  • Convert a LIFO company's inventory and COGS to a FIFO basis using the LIFO reserve and its year-over-year change.
  • Compute inventory turnover and DIO, and explain what a rising or falling value of each actually indicates.
  • Compute DSO, DPO, and the cash conversion cycle from average receivables, inventory, payables, sales, and COGS.
  • Given a list of costs around a PP&E purchase, sort which ones are capitalized into the asset's cost and which are expensed immediately.
  • Compute straight-line, double-declining-balance, and units-of-production depreciation for the same asset, and explain why they produce different annual numbers from the same cost and salvage value.
  • Given an asset's cost, accumulated depreciation, and sale price, determine whether the disposal produces a gain, a loss, or neither.
  • Compute PP&E turnover and explain why it's most meaningful compared within a single industry.
  • Explain what gets capitalized versus expensed for a self-created intangible asset, and identify which intangibles are amortized versus tested for impairment.
