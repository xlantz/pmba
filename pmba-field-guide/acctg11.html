ACCTG 6000 · Unit 11

Cash flows

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. This unit is about the statement that shows where a company's cash actually came from and where it went, sorted into three buckets: running the business (operating), buying and selling long-term assets (investing) and dealing with lenders and owners (financing). Read top to bottom and you go from “how can a profitable company go broke?” to every number on the exam. The professor's slides, his 30 practice problems and the 10-question homework are all folded in below: the first sections get you to the test, the homework-flavored parts (ratios, free cash flow, life-cycle puzzles) are the harder layer.

Step 1 · Start here

Follow the cash, and ask who is on the other side

The cash flow statement sorts every dollar that moved into three buckets by asking one question: who was on the other side of the payment?

Think of your own bank account

Your paycheck and your rent are the day-to-day running of your life (operating). Buying a car or selling a rental property is a long-term bet (investing). Taking out a student loan, paying it back, or getting a gift from family is about the people who fund you (financing). Your income statement could say you “earned” $5,000 this month, but the bank statement is the one that tells you if you can pay rent on Friday.

Five words to know first

Operating (CFO)
Cash from the everyday business: customers, suppliers, employees.
Investing (CFI)
Cash from buying or selling long-term assets and investments.
Financing (CFF)
Cash to and from lenders and owners.
Indirect method
Start with net income, then correct it into cash.
CAPEX
Capital expenditures: cash spent buying long-lived assets.

Tiny example: a year in one line each

Start the year with $120,000 of cash.

Operating: +$185,000 (customers paid more than the bills we paid)
Investing: −$210,000 (bought equipment for growth)
Financing: +$80,000 (borrowed to help pay for it)
Net change: +$55,000 → ending cash = $120,000 + $55,000 = $175,000

Operations did not fully fund the expansion, so borrowing filled the gap. That story is exactly what the statement is built to tell.

Step 2 · Learn it

Unit 11, section by section

Open them in order. Each one ends with an exam angle. Sections 01–08 and 10 are the professor's test; 09 and 11 are the homework and real-world layer.

Show:

Profit is an accounting measure. Cash is what pays employees, suppliers, lenders and owners. A company can report growing sales and profits while running short of cash, because the income statement follows the accrual rule (count revenue when earned, expenses when incurred) and cash follows when money actually moves.

Three statements, three jobs

StatementAnswersTime frame
Income statementHow did we perform? (includes noncash items)A period
Balance sheetWhat do we own and owe?One date
Statement of cash flowsWhy did cash change between the two balance sheet dates?A period

Together they give profitability, financial position and liquidity. The cash flow statement is built from the other two: it takes the income statement's net income and the balance sheet's changes and translates them into cash.

Can a company with record net income still go bankrupt? Yes.

A startup books $500,000 of sales on 90-day payment terms and reports a record profit. Payroll of $200,000 and a loan payment are due this month; the customers have not paid yet. Profit is real, but the bank balance cannot cover Friday's payroll. Lenders and employees accept cash, not net income.

That is also why revenue rising while receivables rise faster is a warning: more of each sale is sitting uncollected.

Exam angleConceptual: the three categories help users separate cash from core operations from cash used for investments from cash exchanged with creditors and owners (professor Q21). Net income does not guarantee positive cash flow. An A/R increase reduces operating cash flow (Q16).

Two quick sorting rules

1. Did it come from a customer, supplier, employee or an expense on the income statement? Operating. 2. Is it a long-term asset or an investment in another company? Investing. 3. Is it a lender or an owner on the other side? Financing.

PP&E and intangibles are not operating assets because they are long-term; their cash purchases and sales fall under investing (Q23). Cash paid for a new warehouse is not operating (Q20); cash paid to vendors for services is.

Wording note on Q18The question reads “A repurchase of common stock is not a financing activity because…” but the key (C) says it is financing because cash is returned to shareholders. Trust the key: a repurchase is a financing outflow.

Noncash deals (for example, issuing shares to acquire an asset) move no cash, so they are left out of the three sections and disclosed separately.

Exam angleClassify an item (Q17, Q20, Q25: sale of an equity investment is investing). Solve for a missing section from the total change in cash (Q9). Remember that a negative CFI is not automatically bad: it often means the company is growing.

Financing reports cash raised from and returned to lenders and owners. It is about principal and capital, not interest expense. Borrow = inflow; repay principal = outflow. Issue stock = inflow; repurchase stock = outflow; pay dividends = outflow.

Why is principal financing but interest operating? Interest is an expense: it is in net income, a cost of running the business, so its cash lives in operating. Principal is just handing back what you borrowed, a transaction with the lender.

Can financing inflows mask weak operations? Yes. A company can borrow to cover cash burned by operations. A positive CFF can signal a thriving company raising growth money or a distressed one staying alive: read it next to CFO and CFI.

Retiring bonds at a gain or loss

A bond's carrying value is what the books say is owed (face value minus unamortized discount, or plus premium). If the company pays less than carrying value to retire it, it books a gain; more, a loss. Either way the cash paid is financing, and the gain or loss, which sits in net income, is reversed in operating.

Two worked cases

Q10: carrying value = 800,000 − 77,000 + 22,000 = 745,000
gain = 745,000 − 733,000 = 12,000
Financing (733,000); Operating (12,000) → answer D

Paxon (homework): retire bonds with face and book value 630,000
at a 40,500 gain → cash paid = 630,000 − 40,500 = 589,500
issue 450,000 of bonds at 463,500 → cash in = 463,500
Financing = 463,500 − 589,500 = (126,000)

Rule: cash paid = carrying value − gain (or + loss). Paying more than carrying value is a loss and is added back in operating.

Missing-item puzzles (professor Q5) are simple subtraction: financing of $750,000 with loan proceeds of $690,000 and dividends of $225,000 leaves a $285,000 stock issuance (690 − 225 + 285 = 750). The $500,000 sale of PP&E is a distractor: it is investing.

Exam angleSum the financing items with the right signs (Q7: 150 − 90 + 200 − 45 − 70 = +145,000; acquiring and selling subsidiary shares is investing, so ignore it). Pick the right section for each item in a list (Q14: only debt repayment and stock repurchase are financing, so −662,799). Bond retirement: cash paid in financing, gain out of operating.

Investing is “what is the company betting on?” It covers cash transactions in long-term assets, business acquisitions and investments. Outflows (−): buy PP&E, acquire a business (net of cash acquired), buy debt or equity investments. Inflows (+): sell PP&E, sell a business, sell or collect investments. Why does a $10 million equipment purchase show up here instead of its annual depreciation? Because the cash left when it was bought; depreciation is just the cost spread out over years.

Solving for CAPEX with a PP&E roll-forward

The change in net PP&E is not the cash spent. Purchases add to the account; depreciation and the book value of anything sold take away. Fill in what you know and solve for purchases.

Selling equipment: gain in earnings, full price in investing

The gain is subtracted in operating because it is a nonoperating item already in net income, and the full proceeds are reported in investing. Without the subtraction the cash would be counted twice. Slide check: sold equipment with a $30,000 book value for $42,000? The $12,000 gain appears on the income statement and is reversed (−12,000) in operating; the $42,000 is investing.

The professor's Franklin Concrete problem (Q3), step by step

Here the gross PP&E and accumulated depreciation are both given. You need cost sold, accumulated depreciation, book value and purchases.

Accum. depr. sold = beginning + expense − ending = 1,235,000 + 285,000 − 1,245,000 = 275,000
Book value sold = cost sold − accum. depr. sold = 975,000 − 275,000 = 700,000
Gain = proceeds − book value = 800,000 − 700,000 = 100,000 (comes out of operating)
Gross PP&E: 2,700,000 + purchases − 975,000 = 2,400,000 → purchases = 675,000
Net PP&E cash flow = +800,000 − 675,000 = +125,000 → answer A

Shortcut when you only need purchases from gross PP&E: purchases = ending − beginning + cost of assets sold. You don't even need depreciation (2,400 − 2,700 + 975 = 675).

Intangibles (patents, copyrights) behave like PP&E: buying them is an investing outflow, selling them an investing inflow (full proceeds), with any gain or loss reversed in operating. Paxon: purchased $360,000 of intangibles and sold intangibles costing $292,500 at a $27,000 loss → proceeds = 292,500 − 27,000 = $265,500. Investing = −360,000 + 265,500 = (94,500), and the $27,000 loss is added back in operating.

Exam angleQ3 (net PP&E cash flow), Q13 (investing = −725,361 + 884,399 = +159,038; the gain does not change it because investing uses full proceeds), and Q8 (equipment sold: investing +81,000). Always use the full proceeds in investing and move only the gain or loss into operating.

Operating cash flow answers: is the company generating cash from its central operations? The indirect method starts from the net income you already have and corrects it into cash. It is a bridge with four steps.

StepWhat you do
1Start with accrual-basis net income.
2Reverse noncash and nonoperating items: + depreciation & amortization, + stock-based compensation, + losses on disposals, − gains on disposals.
3Adjust for changes in operating assets and liabilities (next section has the sign rules).
4The total is net cash provided by (used in) operating activities.

Why add back depreciation but subtract a gain? Depreciation reduced net income but no cash left (the cash went out when the asset was bought, in investing), so we undo the subtraction. A gain increased net income but the cash from the sale is reported in investing, so we remove it from operating. Same logic for stock-based compensation: an expense with no cash payment, so add it back.

The slide's example: $245,000 of profit becomes $268,000 of cash

Can operating cash flow exceed net income without higher sales? Yes: noncash expenses added back (depreciation), receivables collected, inventory sold down, or payables stretched and customers paying in advance. None of those require more revenue.

Traps the professor flags

  • Adding back depreciation, amortization or stock-based compensation does not mean they generate cash.
  • The gain on an asset sale is removed from operating; the full proceeds go to investing.
  • Changes in A/R, inventory and A/P are not automatically good or bad. Context matters (strong CFO caused mainly by rising payables is not the same as strong collections).
  • Prepaid expenses and depreciation are different cases: depreciation is added back directly; an expense you prepaid (insurance) is captured through the change in the prepaid balance (Q30, below).

Step 3 of the bridge turns balance sheet changes into cash. Operating assets are things like receivables, inventory and prepaids. Operating liabilities are things like payables, wages payable, deferred revenue and taxes payable. The rule fits on one grid.

More operating accounts

  • Prepaid expenses. Prepaids up 6,000 means you paid 6,000 more than you expensed: subtract. Lincoln's prepaid insurance rose from 5,000 to 7,000, so the adjustment is (2,000).
  • Deferred revenue (customer advances). It is an operating liability. Up: you collected cash ahead of revenue, so add. Down: you recognized revenue from cash collected earlier, so subtract. A relatively large decrease (Q22) means more previously deferred revenue was recognized than new advance payments were collected.
  • Inventory. A balance rising from $500,000 to $900,000 is shown in the operating section as an increase in inventory of ($400,000), not as a $500,000 or $900,000 payment (Q1). Do not put it in investing (Q1 option C).

Is an increase in accounts payable always good news? It boosts CFO now, but it means you are paying suppliers later. If it reverses next year, CFO falls. Strong CFO caused mainly by rising payables deserves a closer look.

Quality of earnings ratio = Operating cash flow ÷ Net income

Two companies can report the same profit and have very different cash. The ratio shows how much of each dollar of profit turned into operating cash.

  • Above 1 (A = 1.50): cash exceeds earnings. Investigate why: depreciation and other noncash expenses, deferred revenue, working-capital timing.
  • Below 1 (B = 0.45): profit isn't converting to cash. Investigate receivables, inventory and timing.
  • Is 1.50 always better? No. Timing can distort it: stretching payables or a one-time burst of customer prepayments lifts the ratio temporarily. Look across several years.

Professor Q26: the ratio compares operating cash flow with reported net income (answer A). Q27: if Company A's ratio is 2 and B's is 1, A generates more operating cash per dollar of net income, potentially signaling stronger cash conversion (answer B). It does not say A has twice the net income, and it says nothing about B's investments.

Exam angleCompute CFO ÷ NI and interpret it; know which direction means stronger cash conversion; know that a high ratio can come from benign or less sustainable sources. Don't confuse it with CFO ÷ CAPEX or CFO ÷ current liabilities (next section).

The signs of CFO, CFI and CFF together reveal where a company is in its life. Read them in order, not in isolation.

Can a company have negative investing cash flow and still be financially strong? Yes: a growing or mature company that reinvests from strong operating cash is exactly that pattern.

Messier cases: how to reason

  1. Look at CFO first. Negative means the business isn't yet (or no longer) paying its own way: introduction or decline. Positive means growth or mature.
  2. Then CFI. Negative means still reinvesting (introduction, growth, mature). Positive means selling assets (decline, or a late mature stage).
  3. Then CFF. Positive means taking in outside money. Negative means paying lenders and owners back, which fits a mature or declining firm.
  4. Break ties with size. Compare the magnitudes to each other and to net income.

Two discussion questions from the slides

Which industries have favorable cash collections? Businesses paid before they deliver (subscriptions, memberships) or at the register (retail). Which have favorable noncash expenses? Asset-heavy businesses with big depreciation (my examples, general knowledge: airlines, utilities, telecom), where net income is depressed by an expense that uses no cash this year.

Exam angleConceptual and short: the professor's questions on deferred revenue (Q22) and quality of earnings (Q26–Q27) come from this thinking. Know why deferred revenue and fast inventory turns help CFO, and why receivable and inventory growth hurt it.

The direct method reports operating cash flow as actual cash receipts and payments (cash collected, cash paid) instead of starting from net income. It reaches the same CFO. Section 07's working-backward formulas are exactly the direct method.

Noncash investing and financing

Some significant deals involve no cash at all, such as issuing shares to acquire an asset. Because no cash moves, they are left out of the three sections and disclosed separately. Example: issuing $200,000 of stock to buy land changes the balance sheet (land and equity up) but does not change CFO, CFI, CFF or cash. A reader who looked only at the three sections would never see it, which is why the disclosure exists.

What the slides and homework say (US GAAP): interest paid is operating; dividends paid are financing; loan principal is financing; the principal portion of a finance-lease payment is financing (homework Q1); purchases of intangibles are investing.

General background, not from the slides: under US GAAP, interest and dividends received and income taxes paid are also generally operating. International standards (IFRS, IAS 7) are more flexible: an entity may classify interest and dividends paid as operating or financing, and interest and dividends received as operating or investing, if it applies the choice consistently. Treat this as background to check against the current standard, and use the slides' US GAAP rules for the exam.

Why classification matters

Moving a $10 million payment from financing to operating changes CFO without changing the total change in cash. That is why analysts compare CFO across companies carefully and why the classification rule is part of every statement's footnotes.

Apple paid out $106,132M against FCF of $98,767M, $7,365M more than it generated after reinvestment. Does returning more than annual FCF automatically mean a company is unhealthy? No. The slide's own prompt is to examine cash balances and financing:

  • Where did the extra come from? A one-year gap funded from a large cash and investment balance is a choice. The same gap funded by new debt, year after year, is a warning.
  • Is it repeated? One year proves little. A multi-year pattern of distributions above FCF steadily drains the cushion.
  • What is FCF made of? CFO can be lifted by working-capital timing (section 08). FCF built on stretched payables is less durable than FCF built on collections.
  • Note: FCF as defined here (CFO − CAPEX) is an analyst measure, not a line item on the statement. Companies and analysts sometimes define it slightly differently, so check the definition before comparing.

Connect it to the life-cycle table: a mature company (+, −, −) returning cash to owners is the expected pattern. The question is sustainability, not the sign.

Traps the exam sets

  • Treating depreciation, amortization or stock comp as if it generates cash (it is just added back).
  • Reporting only the gain from an equipment sale in investing (report the full proceeds; remove the gain in operating).
  • Getting the sign backwards on an operating asset (A/R up = subtract) or liability (A/P up = add).
  • Treating the change in net PP&E as cash spent (add back depreciation and book value sold).
  • Counting an investing or financing item in CFO (e.g. the equity-investment purchase in Q4).
  • Forgetting that bond retirements are reported at cash paid in financing, with the gain or loss reversed in operating.
  • Using full credit sales as cash (Q15) or ignoring prior-year receivables and payables collected or paid this year.
  • Calling interest paid financing (it is operating) or principal operating (it is financing).
  • Assuming a ratio of 1.50 is always better, or negative FCF always means trouble.

Likely exam angles
