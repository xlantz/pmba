Building strong brands

A brand is a promise that lives in customers' minds. Its value is measured as customer-based brand equity and as a financial valuation; it is built step by step up the brand resonance pyramid with well-chosen elements, programs and borrowed associations, organized through portfolios, cobranding and extensions, renewed when it fades or is hit by a crisis, and pushed to its limit in luxury.

What a brand is

A brand is "a name, term, design, symbol or any other feature that identifies one seller's goods or service as distinct from those of other sellers." Its ultimate purpose is to create, for consumers, the company and its collaborators, value beyond what the product and service aspects create. Branding is the process of endowing products and services with that power: it is all about creating differences between products, using brand names and other elements to tell consumers "who" and "what" the product is and why they should care.

Branding is old. Medieval European guilds required craftspeople to put trademarks on products to protect themselves and customers against inferior quality; in fine art, branding began with artists signing their works. Firms start brand creation through marketing, but a brand ultimately resides in the minds and hearts of consumers: a perceptual entity rooted in reality but reflecting consumers' views. Differences can come from the product itself (Gillette, Merck and 3M through innovation) or from non-product imagery (Gucci, Chanel and Louis Vuitton).

Anything where a consumer has a choice can be branded: a good (Tesla), a service (Singapore Airlines), a store (Nordstrom), a place (the city of Sydney), an organization (U2, AAA) or an idea (racial justice, free trade).

Competitors can copy manufacturing and product design, but not "the lasting impressions left in the minds of individuals and organizations by years of favorable product experiences."

Case: New Coke (1985)

Battered by Pepsi taste-test challenges, Coca-Cola spent $4 million on research; blind tests showed drinkers preferred a sweeter formula. The launch caused a national uproar (letters, protests, lawsuit threats). Ten weeks later the old formula returned as "Classic Coke". Researchers had measured taste but not the emotional attachment. New Coke disappeared around 1992, and the old formula ended up with stronger attitudes and sales. (Revived briefly 34 years later with Netflix's Stranger Things.)

Case snapshots from the chapter's opening

  • Barbie (Mattel): 1956 idea (Ruth Handler); toy buyers said it would not interest girls, but TV ads made it a hit. A $1 billion brand by 1992, then Bratz and Disney Princess cut peak-1997 sales in half by 2015. Mattel's 2018 revamp added 175 dolls with different abilities, skin tones, hair and body shapes; the Barbie movie and about 100 brand collaborations made the brand culturally relevant again.
  • Real Madrid: enterprise value over $4 billion, brand itself about $1.5 billion. Since 2000, Florentino Perez signed players who were brands themselves (Beckham, Zidane, Ronaldo), which fed three lines of business: broadcast rights, sponsorship/endorsement, and match-day revenue.

Strategic brand management has four steps: identify and establish brand positioning; plan and implement brand marketing; measure and interpret brand performance; grow and sustain brand value.

Exam angleTell consumer roles (promise, risk reduction, identity) from firm roles (legal property, price premium, barriers to entry). Use New Coke as the example of loyalty and emotional attachment that research missed.

Customer-based brand equity (CBBE)

Customer-based brand equity is "the differential effect that brand knowledge has on consumer response to the marketing of that brand." If consumers react more favorably to a product and its marketing when the brand is identified than when it is not, equity is positive; if less favorably, negative. If there is no difference, the product is a commodity and competition will probably be based on price.

The differences come from brand knowledge: brand awareness plus all the thoughts, feelings, images, experiences and beliefs linked to the brand. Strong, favorable and unique associations: Toyota (reliability), Hallmark (caring), Amazon (convenience and wide selection). Awareness can also drive behavior: GEICO spends tens of millions so drivers think of it first, and a top-of-mind brand becomes the reference point or default option others are judged against.

The 11 benefits of strong equity

  1. Improved perception of product performance
  2. Greater loyalty
  3. Less vulnerability to competitive actions and marketing crises
  4. Larger margins
  5. More inelastic response to price increases, more elastic response to price decreases
  6. Greater trade cooperation and support
  7. More effective marketing communications
  8. Expanded licensing opportunities
  9. More brand extension opportunities
  10. Improved employee recruiting and retention
  11. Greater financial market returns

Equity is a bridge from past to future. Looking back, each year's marketing dollars are investments in consumer brand knowledge (quality, not quantity, matters; you can overspend if money is spent unwisely). Looking forward, consumer knowledge decides where the brand may go, and they grant or deny permission: Bengay aspirin, Cracker Jack cereal, Frito-Lay lemonade, Fruit of the Loom laundry detergent and Smucker's premium ketchup all failed as inappropriate extensions.

Measuring equity: audit vs. tracking

An indirect approach tracks consumer brand knowledge (sources of equity); a direct approach assesses the actual impact of that knowledge on consumer response. The approaches complement each other.

The brand value chain

A structured way to see how marketing activity creates brand value (Figure 10.1). Value flows left to right through four stages, and three multipliers raise or lower what passes from one stage to the next.

Stage 1: the firm invests in a marketing program (communications, trade or intermediary support, product R&D and design) aimed at actual or potential customers. Stage 2: this changes customers' mindset (thoughts and feelings linked to the brand). Stage 3: mindset affects buying behavior and response to all later marketing (price, channels, communication, product), driving market share and profitability. Stage 4: the investment community uses market performance to assess shareholder value.

Brand valuation (financial equity)

Brand valuation (financial-based brand equity) estimates the total financial value of the brand: the premium on a company's valuation due to owning the brand, or the net present value of the total financial returns the brand will generate over its lifetime. In well-known companies brand value is typically more than half of market capitalization. U.S. companies do not list brand equity on balance sheets (no agreement on estimates), but companies in the United Kingdom, Hong Kong and Australia do. Interest rose with the 1980s mergers and acquisitions wave.

John Stuart of Quaker Oats: "If this business were split up, I would give you the land and bricks and mortar, and I would take the brands and trademarks, and I would fare better than you."

Brand building as a climb

The brand resonance model sees brand building as an ascending series of four steps, from bottom to top, which together form a pyramid of six brand building blocks (Figure 10.2). It stresses the duality of brands: the rational route is on the left side of the pyramid, the emotional route on the right.

  1. Ensure customers identify the brand and associate it with specific products, services or needs.
  2. Firmly establish brand meaning by strategically linking tangible and intangible associations.
  3. Elicit the proper customer responses: brand-related judgments and feelings.
  4. Convert responses into intense, active loyalty.

ImageryExtrinsic properties, including how the brand meets psychological or social needs

ResonanceThe relationship: intensity of the psychological bond and the level of activity it engenders. Examples: Harley-Davidson, Apple, eBay. Fox News finds higher resonance and engagement lead to greater recall of its ads

Case: MasterCard "Priceless"

Overshadowed in the mid-1990s by Visa and American Express, MasterCard launched "Priceless" in 1997 (with McCann Erickson): three tangible items with prices, then an intangible payoff ("Real conversation with 11-year-old son: Priceless"), ending "There are some things money can't buy; for everything else, there's MasterCard." It communicated rational advantages (wide acceptance) and the emotional payoffs they allow: a duality. Over 25 years in 102 markets and 50 languages; now shifted from observing to enabling priceless moments ("Priceless Experiences").

Where brand knowledge comes from: three factors

Equity is built by positioning the brand and creating the right awareness and associations; success depends on all brand-related contacts, marketer-initiated or not. Three main factors:

  1. Brand elements (names, logos, symbols, etc.). Example: Microsoft named its search engine Bing as short, appealing, memorable, active, and multicultural.
  2. The product/service and all accompanying marketing activity and programs. Example: General Mills using QR codes, apps and AR.
  3. Other associations indirectly transferred by linking the brand to some other entity (person, place, thing). Example: 42BELOW vodka references a latitude through New Zealand and its 42 percent alcohol content, leveraging New Zealand's perceived purity.

Marketing Insight: building a startup brand

When resources are limited, focus, consistency and creativity matter most. Seven guidelines:

  1. Find a compelling product or service performance advantage (Dropbox's single-folder approach).
  2. Focus on one or two strong brands and one or two key associations (Volcom's "Youth Against Establishment").
  3. Encourage trial (See's Candies samples; SMARTFOOD sampling).
  4. Develop a cohesive digital strategy (Away's storytelling).
  5. Create buzz and a loyal brand community (Evernote power users).
  6. Employ a well-integrated set of brand elements: SMARTFOOD launched with no advertising, using a unique package and sampling. Family names may suffer in pronounceability or memorability.
  7. Leverage secondary associations (Smartwater/Vitaminwater endorsements; Glacéau sold to Coca-Cola).

Liquid Death (water in 16.9-oz "Tallboy" cans, gothic imagery, "Murder Your Thirst"): a $1.4 billion valuation in a short time on a tight budget, 5 million TikTok and 3 million Instagram followers, exclusive water at 100+ concert venues and 35 festivals via Live Nation.

Exam angleIdentify which block a scenario builds (awareness = salience; "works well" = performance; "feels like me" = imagery; "my opinion" = judgments; "I feel" = feelings; deep loyalty = resonance). Know left = rational, right = emotional, and the three sources of brand knowledge.

Choosing brand elements

Brand elements are trademarkable features that identify and differentiate the brand: names, logos, symbols, characters, slogans, jingles, packages, signage. Most strong brands use several (Nike: the "swoosh", "Just Do It", and the name of the Greek winged goddess of victory). The test: what would consumers think or feel about the product if the element were all they knew? (Nature Valley suggests healthful food; Panasonic Toughbook suggests durable.) Legal protection: trademarks for names and elements, patents for manufacturing processes, copyrights and proprietary designs for packaging.

Watch for brands drifting generic (Kleenex, Kitty Litter, Jell-O, Scotch Tape, Xerox, Fiberglass): owners must keep trademark rights. A name with inherent meaning may make it harder to add a different meaning or reposition later. Important elements often capture intangible traits: insurers use symbols of strength (Rock of Gibraltar for Prudential, the stag for Hartford) or security (Allstate's "good hands", the Travelers umbrella, Fireman's Fund hard hat).

  • Slogans are efficient "hooks" ("Like a Good Neighbor, State Farm Is There"). Be careful replacing good ones: Citi dropped "Citi Never Sleeps" for "Let's Get It Done" and returned to the old one; Avis replaced "We Try Harder" (50 years) with the weaker "It's Your Space."
  • Characters have human traits that boost likability and make the brand interesting and fun: Keebler elves, Michelin's Bibendum (safety; credited with 80 percent awareness worldwide; a "Passport" limits its use), M&M's spokescandies, Ronald McDonald (since 1963), Mr. Clean (1957). They help break through clutter, soft-sell a benefit, and "don't demand pay raises or misbehave."
  • Packaging is a cost-effective way to build equity; called the "last five seconds of marketing", "permanent media", "the last salesman", even the "fifth P". It builds equity directly (functional or aesthetic differentiation) or indirectly (reinforcing awareness and image). Color codes matter (red: Ritz, Folgers, Colgate, Target, Coca-Cola).
  • Labels communicate written, electronic or graphical information on or attached to the product: identify, describe attributes, highlight benefits, instruct on use, storage and disposal, add aesthetic appeal, and leverage the brand.

Marketing programs: contacts and the DRIVE test

A brand contact is any information-bearing experience, positive or negative, a customer or prospect has with the brand, its category or its market (observation, word of mouth, company personnel, online or phone experiences, payment). Elements help, but the biggest contribution comes from the product or service and its supporting programs. Grade the program with DRIVE:

LetterQuestion
D: DistinctivenessHow unique, creative, differentiating?
R: RelevanceHow meaningful to customers; is it a brand they should seriously consider?
I: IntegratedDo all aspects combine for maximum impact now and over time, balancing continuity and change?
V: ValueShort-run and long-run value: profitable sales now, equity later?
E: ExcellenceDesigned to the highest standards, state-of-the-art thinking?

A well-integrated, relevant, unique program yields greater ROI: the California Milk Processor Board's "Got Milk?" reversed a decades-long decline in California milk consumption despite being outspent by Coca-Cola, Pepsi and Budweiser.

Leveraging secondary associations

Linking the brand to a person, place or thing with meaning in consumers' minds creates secondary sources of brand knowledge (Figure 10.3).

Example: Burton's "Dominator" surfboard

Burton (snowboards, about a third of the snowboard market) could: sub-brand it "Dominator by Burton" (company); rely on rural New England origins (country/region, weak fit for surfing); sell through popular surf shops (channels); cobrand a strong foam or fiberglass ingredient brand (other brands, like Wilson using Goodyear rubber); sign top pro surfers (spokespeople); sponsor the World Surf League Championship Tour (events); publicize ratings from Surfer (third party).

Risk: anything bad that happens to the other entity may also attach to the brand. When endorsers Tiger Woods, Lance Armstrong and Kanye West (Ye) got into trouble, many firms cut ties.

Exam angleSort the six element criteria into build vs. defend and match an example to each. Apply DRIVE to a program description. Name which secondary-association source a tactic uses (endorsement, sponsorship, ingredient brand, origin...), and the risk.

What brand architecture decides

Brand architecture gives guidelines about a company's branding strategy and which brand elements apply across new and existing products and services. It is a "product roadmap to the future" that clarifies where the brand can go and how. It defines brand breadth (boundaries: which products share a name) and brand depth (complexity: how many variations of the name). Related decisions: brand portfolios, cobranding and brand extensions.

Brand portfolios

A brand can only be stretched so far, and not every segment views it equally. The brand portfolio is the set of all brands and brand lines a firm offers in a category or segment. Reasons for multiple brands: serve multiple segments; increase shelf presence and retailer dependence; retain variety seekers who might switch; increase internal competition; exploit economies of scale in advertising, sales, merchandising and distribution.

  • Principle: maximize market coverage (no target customers ignored) and minimize brand overlap. Each brand must be clearly differentiated and appeal to a segment big enough to justify its costs.
  • Optimal portfolio: each brand maximizes equity in combination with the others. If profits rise by dropping brands the portfolio is too big; if they rise by adding brands it is not big enough.
  • Monitor over time and prune weak brands: poorly differentiated offerings suffer cannibalization and brand dilution.

Marketing it brings short-term sales and long-term equity for a range. Branded variants are specific lines supplied to specific retailers or channels because of retailer pressure for distinctive offerings (low-end cameras to mass merchandisers; Valentino lines for different department stores).

Cobranding

Cobranding (dual branding) combines two or more brands marketed together to create superior market value.

FormExample
Same-companyGeneral Mills advertising Trix cereal and Yoplait yogurt
Joint-ventureGE and Hitachi lightbulbs in Japan; Citi/AAdvantage Platinum Select World Elite Mastercard (three parties)
Multiple-sponsorTaligent, a technology alliance of Apple, IBM and Motorola
RetailPizza Hut, KFC and Taco Bell sharing one location

Advantages: convincing positioning through multiple brands; more sales from the existing market and new consumers/channels; lower introduction cost by combining two well-known images; learning about consumers. Disadvantages: risk and loss of control; high expectations so poor performance hurts both brands; overexposure if a brand has many cobranding deals dilutes the association. Requirements: each brand has separate equity (adequate awareness and positive image); above all a logical fit (complementary and unique quality, not redundant); fit in values, capabilities and goals; detailed legal, financial and marketing coordination. Often the brand more deeply invested in production pays the other a licensing fee and royalty. Nabisco: "Giving away your brand is a lot like giving away your child."

Ingredient branding

Ingredient branding is a special case of cobranding that builds equity for materials, components or parts necessarily contained within other branded products. It helps host products with weaker brands by adding differentiation and quality signals. Examples: Dolby, GORE-TEX, Scotchgard, Vibram soles (The North Face, Timberland, Nike...), DuPont's Corian and Kevlar. Westin's self-branded "Heavenly Bed" created a halo for the whole brand. Intel Inside convinced PC buyers to demand Intel chips so Dell, HP and Lenovo paid a premium. Three requirements:

  1. Consumers believe the ingredient matters to the end product's performance (ideally visible or experienced).
  2. Consumers are convinced it is superior (communications, with help from manufacturers and retailers).
  3. A distinctive symbol or logo signals the host contains it, like a simple, versatile "seal".
Exam angleClassify a company as house of brands, branded house or sub-brand and name the pro/con. Tell if a portfolio is too big or too small. Name the cobranding form, and the three ingredient-branding requirements.

Definitions

A brand extension is using an established brand to introduce a new product in a different category or price tier (Honda: cars, motorcycles, snowblowers, lawn mowers, marine engines, snowmobiles, "six Hondas in a two-car garage"). The existing brand that gives birth to the extension is the parent brand; if it is already linked to multiple products it is also a master brand or family brand. When a new brand is combined with the parent (Hershey Kisses, Adobe Acrobat, Toyota Camry, Courtyard by Marriott) the extension is also called a sub-brand.

Most new products (typically 80 to 90 percent in any year) are brand extensions, and many top performers are (Dunkin' Donuts coffee, Progresso Light soups, Hormel Compleats). Still, many new products launch as new brands.

When to extend: vertical vs. horizontal

Judge each extension by how well it leverages parent equity and how well it contributes to parent equity (Crest Whitestrips used Crest's dental reputation and reinforced its dental authority; Bulgari moved into hotels, restaurants, fragrances, chocolate and skin care).

Questions to ask and research insights

Does the parent have significant equity? Is there a strong basis of fit? Will the extension have the optimal points of parity and points of difference? How can marketing programs enhance extension equity? What are the implications for parent equity and profitability?

  • Success needs favorable parent associations plus perceived fit (product-related attributes and benefits, or common usage situations or user types). Kate Spade went from handbags to fragrances, jewelry and apparel.
  • A brand seen as prototypical of its category is hard to extend outside it (Coca-Cola fresh-squeezed juices); positive associations in the original category can turn negative. Concrete attribute associations are harder to extend than abstract benefit associations.
  • Vertical extensions often need sub-branding to prevent negative associations (upscale) or dilution and cannibalization (downscale): Armani Exchange.

Case: Bic perfume (a failed extension)

Bic's "inexpensive, disposable" strategy created markets for ballpoint pens (late 1950s), lighters (early 1970s) and razors (early 1980s). It then launched $5 perfumes ("Nuit", "Jour", "Bic for Men", "Bic Sport for Men") in quarter-ounce bottles that looked like fat lighters, with a $20 million campaign ("Paris in Your Pocket"). It failed: Bic lacked cachet and had negative image associations. The mistake was judging fit on one or a few associations instead of all consumers' brand knowledge.

Brand revitalization

Successful brands grow by acquiring new customers and securing intense, active loyalty from existing ones, so most evolve. When a brand loses momentum or stops growing, it needs revitalization. Cadillac, Fiat and Volkswagen turned fortunes around; GM rescued Cadillac with an overhaul of its lineup (CT sedans; XT and Escalade SUVs; LYRIQ and CELESTIQ electric vehicles) and breakthrough marketing (Lenny Kravitz designing his own CELESTIQ).

First step: understand the original sources of brand equity. Are positive associations losing strength or uniqueness? Have negative associations become linked? Then decide whether to retain the positioning or create a new one.

  • Back to basics: when the marketing program fails to deliver on the brand promise. Harley-Davidson regained leadership by living up to expectations for product performance; Pabst returned to its roots, using iconic packaging and imagery and a perception of authenticity.
  • Reinvention: when the old positioning is no longer viable. Mountain Dew overhauled its image; Old Spice moved from the aftershave and cologne gift set boomers gave their dads to contemporary men's grooming for Millennials, using product innovation and tongue-in-cheek communications (P&G). It is often easier to revive a brand that is alive but forgotten.
  • Burberry (150-year history; trench coats, royal supplier): plaid was overused and counterfeited; outerwear only 20 percent of business. It reinforced its heritage and core luxury products, removed the plaid from 90 percent of products, created 300+ coats, opened stores in desirable locations, trained sales staff and redesigned its online presence for Millennials and Gen Z. (Burberry sits between the poles in this guide's reading; the chapter does not label it.)

Managing a brand crisis

Assume a crisis will come (Chick-fil-A, BP, Domino's, Toyota; financial firms such as Bank of America, JPMorgan and AIG). Repercussions: lost sales, reduced marketing effectiveness, more sensitivity to rivals' marketing, and less impact on competing brands; executives and even founders may need to step down. The stronger the brand and corporate image (especially credibility and trustworthiness), the more likely it weathers the storm, but preparation and a managed crisis program are also critical. Consumers must see the response as swift and sincere; the longer the delay the more negative impressions form, and some permanently switch.

External crises: COVID-19

Nike's four-phase response: Containment, Recovery, Normalization, Return to Growth. It developed PPE for frontline workers, assured pay continuity for staff, launched "Play Inside", gave 90 days free of the premium exercise app, and moved more business to its e-commerce app. Social media's share of the marketing budget rose from 13 percent (January 2020) to 23 percent (May 2020). King Arthur Flour sold smaller bags, added staff to its Baker's Hotline, and handled booming digital traffic.

Exam angleDecide between back to basics (execution problem) and reinvention (strategy problem). Judge crisis responses as swift and sincere (J&J) vs. slow or defensive (Perrier, Gerber). Recall Nike's four phases and the four guidelines.

What makes a brand "luxury"

Luxury brands (Prada, Gucci, Cartier, Louis Vuitton) are among the purest examples of branding: brand and image are key competitive advantages. Some believe it is now a $350 billion industry. They are priced significantly above typical items in the category. Once mostly about social status, luxury in many developed countries is now about style and substance, combining personal pleasure and self-expression. The shopper must feel they get something truly special, so the common denominators are quality and uniqueness. A winning formula for many is craftsmanship, heritage, authenticity and history, often critical to justify a high price. Hermès sells classics "not because they are in fashion, but because they never go out of fashion."

Principles of managing luxury brands

  • Align all marketing decisions (product, service, pricing, sales incentives, communication, distribution) with the brand image.
  • Create a premium, aspirational image.
  • Luxury brands frequently span categories, so competitors are defined broadly.
  • Protect identity and aggressively combat trademark infringement and counterfeits.
  • Align brand elements (names, logos, symbols, packaging) and associations (personalities, events, countries) with the image.

Luxury is not viewed the same everywhere; marketers are often "selling a dream" anchored in quality, status and prestige. A trend is to wrap experiences around products: Gucci invites top spenders to fashion shows, equestrian events and the Cannes Film Festival; Porsche Sport Driving Schools and Experience Centers (45-degree off-road inclines, a simulated ice hill in southern California). Online: social channels (Facebook, X, Instagram, WeChat), e-commerce (Net-a-Porter, Gilt, Farfetch; Dior, Louis Vuitton and Fendi sites that also combat online fakes), and blended experiences (Gucci with Samsung: transparent displays, a digital "shop-in-shop"). Success needs the right balance of classic and contemporary imagery, continuity and change.

Marketing Spotlight: BMW

BMW Group includes BMW, MINI, Rolls-Royce and BMW Motorrad. Founded 1916 as an aircraft-engine maker; the roundel (Bavarian colors, trademarked 1917). Slogans: "Sheer Driving Pleasure" (1972), "The Ultimate Driving Machine" (U.S. since 1973), expressing the duality of luxury (ultimate) and performance (driving machine). Growth in the 1980s and 1990s came from affluent baby boomers (3, 5 and 7 Series); around 2000 buyers wanted more choice, so BMW broadened its portfolio.

Brand
A name, term, design, symbol or any other feature that identifies one seller's goods or service as distinct from those of other sellers. Its purpose: value beyond the product itself, for consumers, the company and collaborators.
Branding & the Role of Brands
Branding
Endowing products and services with the power of a brand by creating differences between them. A brand is a promise that sets expectations and reduces risk, and it resides in consumers' minds.
Branding & the Role of Brands
Customer-based brand equity (CBBE)
The differential effect brand knowledge has on consumer response to the marketing of that brand. Positive if people react more favorably when the brand is identified; negative if less favorably.
Brand Equity & Valuation
Brand audit vs. brand tracking
Audit = focused procedures to assess brand health and find the sources of equity (do it at planning time/annually). Tracking = quantitative consumer data collected over time as a baseline.
Brand Equity & Valuation
Brand value chain
Marketing program investment > customer mindset > market performance > shareholder value, with three multipliers (program, customer, market) between the stages.
Brand Equity & Valuation
Brand valuation vs. goodwill
Valuation (financial-based equity) = net present value of the brand's total lifetime returns. Goodwill = monetary value of ALL intangible assets (brands, patents, copyrights, know-how, licenses, culture...), so it is broader.
Brand Equity & Valuation
Three valuation approaches
Cost (cost to build or replace the brand), market (branded vs. identical unbranded revenue, minus brand-building cost), financial (NPV of future brand earnings, risk-adjusted).
Brand Equity & Valuation
Brand resonance model
Brand building as an ascending pyramid of 4 steps and 6 building blocks: salience; performance + imagery; judgments + feelings; resonance. Left side = rational route, right side = emotional route.
Brand Resonance Model
Brand salience
How often and easily customers think of the brand in various purchase or consumption situations: the depth and breadth of awareness. The base of the pyramid.
Brand Resonance Model
Brand performance vs. imagery
Performance = how well the offering meets functional needs. Imagery = extrinsic properties, how the brand meets psychological or social needs.
Brand Resonance Model
Brand judgments vs. feelings
Judgments = customers' own personal opinions and evaluations (head). Feelings = emotional responses and reactions (heart).
Brand Resonance Model
Brand resonance
The top of the pyramid: the intensity of the psychological bond with the brand and the level of activity it engenders; customers feel 'in sync'. Examples: Harley-Davidson, Apple, eBay.
Brand Resonance Model
Brand elements and the 6 criteria
Trademarkable identifiers (names, logos, symbols, characters, slogans, jingles, packages, signage). Choose them to be memorable, meaningful, likable (build) and transferable, adaptable, protectable (defend).
Brand Elements, Programs & Associations
DRIVE
Test of marketing-program quality: Distinctiveness, Relevance, Integrated, Value, Excellence.
Brand Elements, Programs & Associations
Secondary associations
Meaning borrowed by linking the brand to another entity: company, country/region, channels, other brands, characters, spokespeople, events, third-party sources. Efficient but risky (Tiger Woods, Lance Armstrong, Kanye West/Ye).
Brand Elements, Programs & Associations
Packaging principles
Visibility (cut through clutter), differentiation (stand out from competitors), transparency (communicate the value proposition). Called the 'last five seconds of marketing', 'permanent media', 'last salesman', the 'fifth P'.
Brand Elements, Programs & Associations
Brand architecture
Guidelines on branding strategy and which brand elements apply across new and existing products. Defines brand breadth (boundaries) and brand depth (complexity).
Brand Architecture & Cobranding
Brand portfolio
All brands and brand lines a firm offers in a category or segment. Maximize coverage, minimize overlap. Drop brands if that raises profit (too big); add if that raises profit (too small). Includes flagship products and branded variants.
Brand Architecture & Cobranding
House of brands / branded house / sub-brand
House of brands = separate names (General Mills). Branded house = one corporate umbrella (Heinz, GE). Sub-brand = corporate + product name (Kellogg's Rice Krispies); sits between the two ends.
Brand Architecture & Cobranding
Cobranding
Two or more brands marketed together (dual branding). Four forms: same-company, joint-venture, multiple-sponsor, retail. Needs separate equity of each brand and, above all, logical fit.
Brand Architecture & Cobranding
Ingredient branding
A special case of cobranding: building equity for materials, components or parts contained in other brands' products (Dolby, GORE-TEX, Intel Inside). Needs perceived importance, perceived superiority, and a distinctive symbol.
Brand Architecture & Cobranding
Line vs. category extension
Line extension = new flavor/form/color/ingredient/size within a category the brand already serves (Dannon Low Fat). Category extension = stretch into a category not currently served (Swiss Army watches).
Brand Extensions
Vertical vs. horizontal extension
Vertical = across price points (Giorgio, Emporio, Armani Exchange), usually needs a sub-brand. Horizontal = across many product categories under one image (Ralph Lauren, Calvin Klein).
Brand Extensions
Brand dilution vs. cannibalization
Dilution = consumers no longer link the brand to a specific set of products and think less of it (Porsche SUVs). Cannibalization = extension sales come from the parent's own sales (acceptable if preemptive, e.g. Tide).
Brand Extensions
Back to basics vs. reinvention
Back to basics when execution fails the brand promise (Harley-Davidson, Pabst). Reinvention when the old positioning is no longer viable (Mountain Dew, Old Spice). A continuum, with blends between.
Revitalization & Crisis
Brand-crisis response
Swift and sincere. Strong brand + preparation helps. Perrier (slow, confused) and Gerber (kept product on shelves) hurt; Johnson & Johnson (Tylenol, triple tamper-proof packaging) is the model. Four crisis guidelines: Empathy, Value, Strategy, Innovation.
Revitalization & Crisis
Luxury brand common denominators
Priced well above category norms; quality and uniqueness. Winning formula: craftsmanship, heritage, authenticity, history. Shoppers must feel they get something truly special.
Luxury Brands
Luxury brand management principles
Align ALL marketing decisions with the brand image; premium aspirational image; competitors defined broadly; fight counterfeits; align elements and associations; wrap experiences around the product.
