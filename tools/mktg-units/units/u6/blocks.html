What is a business market?

Organizational buying is the process by which formal organizations establish the need to buy products and services and then identify, evaluate, and choose among alternative brands and suppliers. The business (B2B) market is every organization that buys goods and services used to make other products or services that are sold, rented, or supplied to others. Business markets are often less visible than consumer markets but substantially larger: more dollars and items change hands in sales to business buyers.

Concrete example (the chapter’s shoe chain): hide dealers sell hides to tanners, who sell leather to shoe manufacturers, who sell shoes to wholesalers, who sell to retailers, who sell to you. Only the last step is a consumer sale; every earlier step is a business sale, and each party also buys many other goods and services to run its operations.

The chapter’s big warning is commoditization: customers see different companies’ products as identical, which eats margins and weakens loyalty. The cure is to convince buyers that meaningful differences exist and are worth the extra cost, so a critical B2B task is to create and communicate relevant differentiation.

Consumer marketing still applies. The Institute for the Study of Business Markets names three big B2B hurdles: integrating sales and marketing, managing innovation, and gathering and using customer and market insights. B2B marketers also need holistic marketing and strong loyalty relationships, just like consumer marketers.

Three buying situations

SituationWhat happensDecisions / risk
Straight rebuyPurchasing reorders routine items (office supplies, bulk chemicals) from an approved supplier list. Suppliers try to keep quality up and propose automatic reordering; challengers try to offer something new or exploit dissatisfaction.Fewest decisions.
Modified rebuyBuyer wants to change specifications, prices, delivery, or other terms. Needs extra negotiation; can produce a new agreement or a change of suppliers.In between.
New buyFirst-time purchase (an office building, a new security system). Greater risk or cost means more participants, more information gathering, and a longer decision.Most decisions; the marketer’s greatest opportunity and challenge.

Over time, new buys become straight rebuys. In a new buy the buyer must settle product specifications, price limits, delivery terms and times, service terms, payment terms, order quantities, acceptable suppliers, and the selected supplier. The chapter says the process runs from awareness through interest, evaluation, trial, and adoption: mass media matter most at awareness, salespeople at interest, and technical sources at evaluation. Purchasing agents are most influential in rebuys; other employees matter more in new buys. Engineers usually influence component choice, while purchasing agents dominate supplier choice.

The buying center: seven roles

The buying center is the decision-making unit of a buying organization: everyone who plays one or more of seven roles. One person can hold several roles, and several people can share one role. A purchasing manager is often buyer, influencer, and gatekeeper at once. A buying center typically has five or six members and sometimes dozens, and some may be outside the company (consultants, government officials, technical advisors).

RoleWhat they doEveryday example
InitiatorsRequest that something be purchased.A line supervisor asks for a new machine.
UsersUse the product; often help define requirements.The operators of the machine.
InfluencersHelp define specifications and evaluate alternatives; technical people matter most.An engineer who writes the spec.
DecidersDecide on product requirements or suppliers.A plant manager.
ApproversAuthorize what deciders or buyers propose.A finance director signing off.
BuyersHold formal authority to select the supplier and arrange terms; negotiate price.The purchasing manager.
GatekeepersCan keep sellers or information from reaching other members.Receptionists, telephone operators, purchasing agents.

Selling to a buying center

Hospital surgical-gown example (from the chapter): the VP of purchasing decides whether to buy disposable or reusable gowns. If disposable, the operating-room administrator compares brands on absorbency, antiseptic quality, design, and cost, and normally buys the brand that meets functional needs at lowest cost. Surgeons influence the decision retroactively by reporting their satisfaction.

Members want different things: engineers want performance, production wants ease of use and reliable supply, finance wants the economics, purchasing worries about operating and replacement costs, and union officials stress safety. Marketers should periodically review who really sits in the center: SAP raised new-customer license sales by shifting its focus from CIOs to individual corporate units lower in the organization.

Rational and emotional. Purchasing decisions are made by individuals, not organizations. Businesspeople are buying solutions to two problems: the organization’s economic and strategic problem and their own need for achievement and reward. Trust, confidence, pride, and likability matter; the chapter cites a Binet and Field study that B2B messages appealing to emotion were seven times more likely to boost long-term sales, revenue, and profits than purely rational ones. Examples: Volvo Trucks’ “Epic Split,” Caterpillar’s “Doers,” Salesforce’s mascot Astro.

Exam angleClassify a scenario as straight rebuy, modified rebuy, or new buy. Name the buying-center role in a short story (the receptionist is a gatekeeper; the person who negotiates terms is the buyer). Recall that there are seven roles.

The eight stages

The chapter presents one popular model with eight stages (its Figure 4.1). In modified or straight rebuys some stages are compressed or bypassed, because the buyer usually has a favorite or ranked supplier list and can skip search and proposal solicitation.

StageWhat happensMarketer’s opening
1. Problem recognitionSomeone sees a need. Internal triggers: a new product needing new equipment, a broken machine, unsatisfactory material. External triggers: trade show, ad, email, blog, sales call.Stimulate recognition with direct marketing.
2. Need descriptionBuyer defines general characteristics and quantity; for complex items works with engineers and users on reliability, durability, price.Show how your product meets or exceeds the need.
3. Product specificationTechnical specs, often with a product-value-analysis team that checks whether components can be redesigned, standardized, or made cheaper without hurting performance. Tight specs let buyers refuse costly or substandard parts.Be active early to influence the specs.
4. Supplier searchTrade directories, other companies, trade ads, trade shows, the internet. Buying alliances (purchasing consortia) pool volume; electronic marketplaces include catalog sites, vertical e-hubs, pure-play auctions, spot markets, and private exchanges.Be findable; online buying cuts transaction costs but may erode loyalty.
5. Proposal solicitationBuyer invites written proposals, then a few suppliers present formally.Research, write, and present proposals in customer terms of value and benefits.
6. Supplier selectionBuying center ranks desired supplier attributes (price, reputation, reliability, agility) and rates suppliers with a supplier-evaluation model. Many firms cut supplier counts, some to single sourcing, which has risks (disasters, pandemics, conflicts; suppliers growing comfortable).Frame benefits and cost savings so the difference is clear.
7. Contract negotiationFinal order: specs, quantity, delivery time, return policy, warranties. Many firms lease heavy equipment. Blanket contracts (stockless purchase plans) and vendor-managed inventory are growing.Blanket contracts lock suppliers in tighter.
8. Performance reviewBuyer contacts end users, uses a weighted-score method, or totals the cost of poor performance. May continue, modify, or end the relationship.Keep adding value to win rebuys.

Why it matters: the summary says marketers must ensure the value of their offering “comes through at each stage.” Online buying shaves transaction costs, shortens time between order and delivery, consolidates purchasing systems, and builds more direct relationships, but it may erode supplier-buyer loyalty and create security problems.

From products to solutions

Many business buyers prefer to buy a total problem solution from one seller. Systems selling began with government purchases of major weapons and communications systems: a prime contractor assembles subcomponents from second-tier contractors and delivers a turnkey solution (the buyer just turns one key). Systems contracting is a variant where one supplier provides all of a buyer’s maintenance, repair, and operating (MRO) needs and manages the inventory; Shell Oil does this for business customers.

Services, brands, and communications

Services: adding services creates value and closer ties. Rolls-Royce earns high margins from “power by the hour” repair and maintenance contracts. The chapter notes Adobe moved Photoshop, Illustrator, and InDesign to cloud subscriptions, which removes the need to persuade users to upgrade and lets it sell support services.

B2B brands: brands give buyers peace of mind and help justify purchases (“Nobody gets fired for buying IBM”). The corporate brand is often critical because it covers so many products; ABB, Emerson Electric, and SAS rebranded to unify or clarify theirs.

Communications: B2B firms use SEO, SEM, webinars, blogs, white papers, and podcasts (Makino, Kinaxis); some borrow consumer-style tactics (Xerox’s Marriott concierge ad).

Overcoming price pressure

Buyers still spend much time haggling on price. Sellers can respond with framing, total cost of ownership (the life-cycle cost of using the product), superior service, productivity gains, restrictive low-price conditions, or collaboration (Lincoln Electric’s Guaranteed Cost Reduction program promises cost savings that meet or exceed the price gap).

Economic value analysis (EVA) monetizes functional benefits such as performance, reliability, and warranty. The chapter’s value logic: buyers add up economic, functional, and psychological benefits from product, services, people, and image, subtract total customer cost (money plus time, energy, and psychological costs of acquiring, using, maintaining, owning, and disposing), and choose the highest perceived value.

Caterpillar can improve its offer in three ways: raise total customer benefit, cut the buyer’s nonmonetary costs, or cut monetary cost. To win, it must offer more customer-perceived value than Komatsu.

Trust, credibility, and relationship type

Four forces shape a business relationship: availability of alternatives, importance of supply, complexity of supply, and supply-market dynamism. Relationships range from basic buying and selling (simple routine exchanges) to collaborative partnerships built on trust and commitment. Roles can shift: not every buyer wants a close tie, and suppliers may skip customers with limited growth.

ConceptDefinitionWhat drives it
TrustA firm’s willingness to rely on a business partner.Perceived competence, integrity, honesty, benevolence.
Corporate credibilityExtent customers believe a firm can design and deliver what they need; the foundation of a strong relationship.Expertise (able to make/sell), trustworthiness (honest, dependable, sensitive to customers), likability (attractive, prestigious, dynamic).

A firm looks trustworthy when it gives full, honest information, aligns employee incentives with customer needs, partners to create market value, and offers valid comparisons with competitors. Online, buyers worry about quality and delivery; sellers worry about getting paid.

Risk and opportunism

A close relationship creates tension between safeguarding (predictability) and adapting (flexibility). Specific investments are expenditures tailored to one partner (training, equipment, systems). They raise efficiency but can lock firms in: buyers can be held up because of switching costs, and suppliers risk dedicated assets and intellectual property. Opportunism is cheating or under-supplying relative to an implicit or explicit contract; it forces costly monitoring. The chapter’s example is Peanut Corporation of America, whose contaminated product led to a very large recall. When the future horizon is long and solidarity norms are strong, specific investments shift from expropriation toward bonding.

The Red Bull “So what?” ladder

Slide notes for the top of the ladder: you do not really need support for it, because it is the insight; make sure the laddering takes you somewhere unique; and abstract on it a little more.

“Yes, but… ok, then”: test it against competitors

StepRed Bull example
Yes, but…Coffee and Coca-Cola do the same thing.
Ok, then…Less serious than coffee, stronger than cola.
So…Playful image: fun and light. Small can: a little goes a long way, stronger than cola, easy to take with you, does not get in the way of adventure.

Try it yourself (a worked pattern, not from the slides): a project-management app → “it shows all tasks in one view” → so what? → “nothing slips through” → so what? → “you stop worrying about deadlines.” Then ask “yes, but…” and see what rivals also claim.

What a creative brief is

The creative brief is the key link between marketing strategy and creative execution. It is prepared by the account management team or, in a larger agency, the strategy team. It summarizes thinking within the agency and gets client buy-in. The slides note there are as many formats as there are agencies, since everyone thinks theirs is the best.

Outline of a typical brief (six questions)

QuestionWhat goes there (slide guidance)
1. Why are we talking?Goals, objectives, problems to overcome, etc.
2. Who are we talking to?Not just “women 18-35”: go into detail. Can use a persona if desired.
3. What are we trying to say?What do we want them to believe? Product position or major selling point.
4. How should we say it?Attitude, tone, etc.
5. Why should they believe us?Support.
6. Special considerations?Anything else that constrains the work.

Worked example: the Red Bull brief

QuestionRed Bull answer (from the slides)
1. Why are we talking?Red Bull is a new brand being introduced to the US for the first time. Goal: establish a personality for the brand and a campaign platform for the introduction. A key objective is to sell the American consumer on the concept of an energy drink.
2. Who are we talking to?Young and male: high school and college students, young workers. Out of the house, busy with school, work, and friends.
3. What are we trying to say?Red Bull provides the energy to live a fuller, more fun, more adventurous life.
4. How should we say it?Confident, playful, light image. Fun and adventure. Unusual or unbelievable situations.
5. Why should they believe us?The energy content of the drink and the history of the brand in Thailand, which has spread by word of mouth.
6. Special considerations?Yes. Red Bull must be seen as different from soft drinks.

Connect the dots: notice that answer 3 is the top of the laddering exercise (fuller, more fun, more adventurous life). The ladder supplies what to say; the brief packages it with audience, tone, proof, and constraints. For your VOC Project Part 2, the ladder feeds question 3 and the brief fills in the rest.
