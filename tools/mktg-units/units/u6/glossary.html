Organizational buying
Decision process by which formal organizations establish the need to purchase and then identify, evaluate, and choose among brands and suppliers.
Business vs. Consumer
Business (B2B) market
All organizations that acquire goods/services used to produce other products or services sold, rented, or supplied to others.
Business vs. Consumer
Commoditization
Customers see different companies' products as identical; erodes margins and loyalty. The B2B marketer's biggest enemy.
Business vs. Consumer
Fewer but larger buyers
Business marketers deal with far fewer buyers who buy far larger quantities (e.g., a handful of automakers).
Business vs. Consumer
Professional purchasing
Trained purchasing agents follow policies; use RFQs, proposals, contracts; want technical data.
Business vs. Consumer
Derived demand
Demand for business goods comes from demand for consumer goods (e.g., coal and steel from consumer demand for power and cars).
Business vs. Consumer
Inelastic demand
Total demand for many business goods barely changes with price, especially short-run or for small-cost items like shoelaces.
Business vs. Consumer
Fluctuating demand
B2B demand is more volatile than consumer demand; plant and equipment swing the most.
Business vs. Consumer
Straight rebuy
Routine reorder from an approved supplier list (office supplies, bulk chemicals). Fewest decisions.
Buying Center
Modified rebuy
Buyer wants to change specs, prices, delivery or terms; requires negotiation; may change suppliers.
Buying Center
New buy
First-time purchase; most risk, most participants, most information, longest time. Biggest opportunity and challenge.
Buying Center
Buying center
The decision-making unit of a buying organization: everyone who plays one or more of seven roles.
Buying Center
Initiator
Requests that something be purchased.
Buying Center
User
Will use the product or service; often initiates and helps define requirements.
Buying Center
Influencer
Helps define specifications and supplies information to evaluate alternatives; technical people are key.
Buying Center
Decider
Decides on product requirements or on suppliers.
Buying Center
Approver
Authorizes the actions proposed by deciders or buyers.
Buying Center
Buyer (role)
Has formal authority to select the supplier and arrange terms; negotiates price.
Buying Center
Gatekeeper
Can keep sellers or information from reaching buying-center members (receptionists, purchasing agents).
Buying Center
Eight-stage buying process
Problem recognition, need description, product specification, supplier search, proposal solicitation, supplier selection, contract negotiation, performance review.
Buying Process
Product-value analysis
Engineering team studies whether components can be redesigned, standardized, or made cheaper without hurting performance.
Buying Process
Buying alliance (GPO)
Companies buying the same goods join to find suppliers faster and win volume discounts (e.g., Topco).
Buying Process
E-hubs / electronic marketplaces
Catalog sites, vertical markets, pure-play auctions, spot markets, private exchanges.
Buying Process
Blanket contract
Long-term agreement: supplier resupplies as needed at agreed prices; aka stockless purchase plan.
Buying Process
Vendor-managed inventory
Supplier sees customer's inventory and takes responsibility for replenishment.
Buying Process
Supplier-performance review
Three ways: ask end users, weighted-score rating, or total the cost of poor performance. Result: continue, modify, or end.
Buying Process
Systems selling (turnkey)
Selling a total solution from one seller; started with government weapons and communications systems.
Programs & Value
Systems contracting
One supplier provides all of a buyer's MRO needs and manages the inventory.
Programs & Value
Total cost of ownership
Life-cycle cost of using a product; a way to counter price pressure.
Programs & Value
Economic value analysis (EVA)
Tool that puts a money value on functional benefits (performance, reliability, warranty).
Programs & Value
Customer-perceived value
Total customer benefit minus total customer cost; price decides how value splits between buyer and seller.
Programs & Value
Trust
Willingness to rely on a business partner; built on competence, integrity, honesty, benevolence.
Relationships & Gov't
Corporate credibility
Belief a firm can deliver on needs: expertise, trustworthiness, likability.
Relationships & Gov't
Specific investments
Spending tailored to one partner (training, equipment, systems); helps efficiency but creates lock-in and hold-up risk.
Relationships & Gov't
Opportunism
Cheating or under-supplying relative to an implicit or explicit contract; costs monitoring resources.
Relationships & Gov't
Institutional market
Schools, hospitals, nursing homes, prisons that serve people in their care; low budgets, captive clienteles.
Relationships & Gov't
Government buying
Usually sealed bids, often lowest bidder; negotiated contracts for complex, low-competition projects; heavy paperwork.
Relationships & Gov't
SAM and GSA
SAM database: the starting point for U.S. government work. GSA: agency with web catalogs linking buyers and contract suppliers.

Laddering
Move up from product features to customer benefits (ask 'So what?'), and down to support for the main selling point.
Laddering
Make vs. Sell
Make: color cosmetics, Sell: confidence. Make: bad-tasting light beer, Sell: laughs and good times with bros.
Laddering
Red Bull ladder
Caffeine, so energy, so you can do more, so a fuller, more adventurous life, then 'Red Bull gives you Wings'.
Laddering
Yes, but... ok, then
Test the insight against rivals: coffee and cola do the same thing; so Red Bull is less serious than coffee, stronger than cola.

Creative brief

Six brief questions
Why talking? Who to? What say? How say it? Why believe us? Special considerations?
